UNIT I · 7 HOURS

Introduction to Pharmaceutical Analysis, Errors and Impurities

Q1. Define pharmaceutical analysis.

Pharmaceutical analysis is the application of analytical methods to identify pharmaceutical substances, determine their amount or concentration, assess purity, and check whether raw materials and finished products meet quality specifications.

Q2. What are the main techniques of pharmaceutical analysis?

Common techniques include volumetric or titrimetric analysis, gravimetric analysis, qualitative chemical tests, instrumental methods such as spectroscopy and chromatography, electroanalytical methods, and biological assays where required. The method is selected according to the substance and the purpose of testing.

Q3. Differentiate between qualitative and quantitative analysis.

Qualitative analysis identifies which substance or ion is present. Quantitative analysis measures how much of a substance is present, for example its mass, concentration, percentage content or potency.

Q4. What is volumetric analysis?

Volumetric analysis determines the amount of an analyte by measuring the volume of a solution of known concentration that reacts with it. Titration is a common form of volumetric analysis.

Q5. Define a standard solution.

A standard solution is a solution whose concentration is known accurately. It is used in quantitative analysis, especially titration, to determine the concentration or amount of another substance.

Q6. Differentiate between primary and secondary standards.

A primary standard is sufficiently pure, stable and well-characterized to prepare a solution of accurately known concentration by direct weighing. A secondary standard is a solution whose concentration is established by standardization against a primary standard or another validated reference solution.

Q7. State the properties of a good primary standard.

It should have high purity, a known formula, good stability in air, low hygroscopicity, relatively high molar or equivalent mass, good solubility in the selected solvent, and a rapid, complete and well-defined reaction with the analyte.

Q8. Give examples of primary and secondary standards.

Common primary-standard examples include anhydrous sodium carbonate for acid standardization and potassium hydrogen phthalate for base standardization. Hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide solutions are commonly treated as secondary standards because their concentrations are established by standardization. The exact standard depends on the analytical method.

Q9. What are the common ways to express solution strength?

Strength may be expressed as molarity (mol/L), molality (mol/kg solvent), normality (equivalents/L), mass concentration (g/L or mg/mL), percentage strength (w/w, w/v or v/v), parts per million (ppm), parts per billion (ppb), or a ratio strength such as 1 in 1,000.

Q10. Define molarity and normality.

Molarity (M) is moles of solute per litre of solution. Normality (N) is the number of equivalents of reactive substance per litre of solution. Normality depends on the reaction and equivalence factor, so molarity is often more unambiguous.

Q11. Explain percentage strength.

% w/w means grams of solute per 100 g of final solution or mixture. % w/v means grams of solute per 100 mL of final solution. % v/v means millilitres of liquid solute per 100 mL of final solution. Always identify which percentage convention is being used.

Q12. What is ratio strength?

Ratio strength expresses the amount of solute relative to a stated amount of preparation, for example 1:1,000. The convention must be specified because it may mean mass-in-volume, mass-in-mass or another defined ratio depending on the context.

Q13. Define error in analytical chemistry.

An error is the difference between a measured result and an accepted reference value. Error can arise from instruments, methods, environmental conditions, sample handling or the analyst.

Q14. Differentiate between systematic and random errors.

Systematic error shifts results in a consistent direction, for example from an incorrectly calibrated balance. Random error causes unpredictable variation between repeated measurements. Systematic errors affect trueness; random errors mainly affect precision.

Q15. What are gross errors?

Gross errors are major mistakes such as using the wrong reagent, misreading a scale, recording a wrong digit or losing part of a sample. Careful procedures, labels, independent checks and clear records help prevent them.

Q16. Define accuracy and precision.

Accuracy describes how close a result is to the accepted value. Precision describes how close repeated results are to one another. A series of results can be precise but inaccurate if a systematic error is present.

Q17. How can analytical errors be minimized?

Calibrate and maintain instruments, use suitable reference standards, follow validated methods, use clean and appropriate glassware, control temperature and other conditions, avoid contamination, repeat measurements when required, record results immediately and apply suitable quality-control checks.

Q18. What are significant figures?

Significant figures are the digits that convey the meaningful precision of a measured or calculated value. In general, multiplication and division are limited by the least precise significant-figure count, while addition and subtraction are limited by the least precise decimal place. Follow the reporting rules required by the method.

Q19. Define impurity in a pharmaceutical substance.

An impurity is an unwanted chemical, residual material, contaminant or other component present in a pharmaceutical substance or product in addition to the intended substance and permitted ingredients.

Q20. What are common sources of pharmaceutical impurities?

Sources include starting materials, reagents, catalysts, solvents, manufacturing by-products, incomplete reactions, degradation during storage, containers and closures, water, equipment, cross-contamination and environmental contamination.

Q21. Classify pharmaceutical impurities.

Impurities may be classified as organic impurities, inorganic impurities and residual solvents. Inorganic impurities can include salts, reagents, catalysts and elemental contaminants. Organic impurities may include starting materials, intermediates, by-products and degradation products.

Q22. Why are impurity limits important?

Impurity limits help protect patient safety and ensure product quality, stability and consistency. Limits are based on the relevant official standard, the nature of the impurity, exposure, route of administration and regulatory requirements.

Q23. What is a limit test?

A limit test is a semi-quantitative or comparative test used to check whether a specified impurity is below a prescribed limit. The sample response is compared with a reference standard prepared under defined conditions.

Q24. Explain the principle of the limit test for chloride.

Chloride ions react with silver nitrate in an acidic medium to form a white turbidity or precipitate of silver chloride. Under the official procedure, the sample is compared with a chloride standard; the sample must not show greater opalescence than the permitted reference.

Reaction: Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s).

Q25. Explain the principle of the limit test for sulphate.

Sulphate ions react with barium ions under specified acidic conditions to form barium sulphate turbidity. The turbidity from the test solution is compared with that from a standard sulphate solution under the prescribed conditions.

Reaction: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄(s).

Q26. What is the general principle of the limit test for iron?

The test converts iron into a coloured complex using specified reagents, and the colour intensity is compared with a reference containing a known amount of iron. Exact reagents and conditions depend on the applicable pharmacopoeial method.

Q27. What is the principle of the limit test for arsenic?

Classical pharmacopoeial arsenic tests convert arsenic into a detectable form and compare the resulting stain or response with a reference standard. Because arsenic is highly toxic and methods can involve hazardous reagents or gases, the test must only be performed in a properly equipped laboratory by trained personnel following the current official method.

Q28. What are lead and heavy-metal impurity tests intended to establish?

They assess whether specified elemental contaminants are within permitted limits. Older general tests may compare coloured metal-sulphide responses; modern methods often use validated instrumental techniques such as atomic absorption or ICP-based analysis. The correct method and limit depend on the substance and current standard.

Q29. What is meant by a modified limit test for chloride or sulphate?

A modified limit test adapts the general comparison procedure to the sample's properties, such as colour, acidity, solubility or matrix interference. The modification must follow a recognized pharmacopoeial procedure or validated method; improvised changes can invalidate the result.

UNIT II · 8 HOURS

Acid–Base Chemistry, Buffer Systems, Isotonicity and Electrolytes

Q1. Define acid and base using the Brønsted–Lowry theory.

A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton (H⁺), while a Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton. In aqueous solution, acids and bases can also be described using other theories, including Arrhenius and Lewis theories.

Q2. What is pH?

pH is defined as the negative logarithm of hydrogen-ion activity: pH = −log₁₀ a(H⁺). In dilute solutions, hydrogen-ion concentration is often used as an approximation. At about 25°C, pH 7 is neutral in pure water; the neutral pH changes with temperature.

Q3. What is a buffer solution?

A buffer solution resists a large change in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. An acidic buffer usually contains a weak acid and its conjugate base; a basic buffer usually contains a weak base and its conjugate acid.

Q4. Write the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation for an acidic buffer.

For a weak acid HA and its conjugate base A⁻, pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]). It is most useful when both buffer components are present in meaningful amounts and the solution behaves approximately ideally.

Q5. Give a simple buffer pH calculation.

If a buffer has pKa = 4.76 and the ratio [A⁻]/[HA] = 1, then log₁₀(1) = 0, so pH = 4.76. If the ratio is 10, pH = 4.76 + 1 = 5.76. Use concentrations or activities consistently with the assumptions of the problem.

Q6. What is buffer capacity?

Buffer capacity describes how much strong acid or base must be added to change the pH of a specified amount of buffer by one unit. It generally increases with the total concentration of buffer components and is greatest near pH = pKa for a simple weak-acid buffer.

Q7. Define isotonicity and explain its pharmaceutical importance.

Two solutions are isotonic when they exert the same effective osmotic pressure across a particular biological membrane under the relevant conditions. Tonicity matters for ophthalmic preparations and parenteral fluids because large differences can cause cells to swell or shrink and can produce discomfort or tissue injury.

Q8. Differentiate between isotonic, hypotonic and hypertonic solutions.

Isotonic: similar effective osmotic pressure to the reference fluid. Hypotonic: lower effective osmotic pressure, tending to make cells gain water. Hypertonic: higher effective osmotic pressure, tending to make cells lose water. Actual biological effects depend on membrane permeability and the solutes present.

Q9. What are the common approaches to adjust isotonicity?

Pharmaceutical calculations may use the freezing-point depression (cryoscopic) method, sodium chloride equivalent (E-value) method, or White–Vincent method. Any preparation must follow the official formulation, validated calculation and sterile-compounding requirements where applicable.

Q10. What are electrolytes?

Electrolytes are substances that form ions in solution and conduct electricity. Physiological electrolytes such as sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, bicarbonate, phosphate and magnesium are essential for fluid balance, nerve signalling, muscle contraction and acid–base regulation.

Q11. State the physiological functions of sodium.

Sodium is the main extracellular cation. It contributes to extracellular-fluid volume and osmolality, helps conduct nerve impulses and supports muscle function and nutrient transport. Abnormal sodium levels can affect the nervous system and require clinical assessment.

Q12. State the physiological functions of potassium.

Potassium is the main intracellular cation. It is important for resting membrane potential, nerve and muscle function, and normal cardiac electrical activity. Both low and high blood potassium can be dangerous.

Q13. What are the functions of calcium and chloride?

Calcium supports bone and teeth structure, muscle contraction, nerve transmission, cell signalling and blood clotting. Chloride is the main extracellular anion and helps maintain fluid balance, electrical neutrality and gastric acid production.

Q14. What is sodium chloride used for in pharmacy?

Sodium chloride is used in appropriate formulations to provide sodium and chloride, adjust tonicity and prepare certain replacement fluids. Concentration, route, sterility and clinical indication determine its safe use.

Q15. What is potassium chloride used for?

Potassium chloride is used to prevent or treat potassium deficiency when clinically indicated. Potassium replacement requires careful dose selection and monitoring because excessive potassium can cause life-threatening cardiac disturbances.

Q16. What is calcium chloride used for?

Calcium chloride is a source of calcium used in selected medical situations under appropriate supervision. Its route and administration rate matter because extravasation can damage tissue and rapid or inappropriate administration can cause serious complications.

Q17. What is oral rehydration salt (ORS)?

ORS is a measured mixture of glucose and electrolytes, especially sodium and potassium salts, that promotes water and electrolyte absorption in the intestine. It is used to prevent or treat dehydration due to diarrhoea and other fluid losses. It must be mixed with the exact volume of safe water stated on the packet.

Q18. Explain physiological acid–base balance.

The body maintains blood pH through chemical buffers, regulation of carbon dioxide by the lungs and regulation of bicarbonate and hydrogen-ion excretion by the kidneys. Disturbances are classified as respiratory or metabolic acidosis and alkalosis according to their primary cause.

UNIT III · 14 HOURS

Acid–Base, Non-Aqueous, Precipitation, Gravimetric, Complexometric and Redox Titrations

Q1. What is an acid–base titration?

An acid–base titration determines the amount or concentration of an acid or base by reacting it with a standard base or acid. The equivalence point is where reactants have combined in the stoichiometric ratio of the reaction.

Q2. What is an acid–base indicator?

An acid–base indicator is a weak acid or base whose ionized and unionized forms have different colours. Its colour change occurs over a characteristic pH range, so an indicator is selected to match the titration's endpoint region.

Q3. Explain the theory of acid–base indicators.

In the ionization theory, an indicator exists in forms with different colours depending on pH. In the quinonoid theory, colour differences are related to structural forms of certain indicators. These models explain why indicator colour changes as the equilibrium shifts with pH.

Q4. Classify acid–base titrations.

Common types are strong acid–strong base, weak acid–strong base, strong acid–weak base and weak acid–weak base titrations. Their pH curves and suitable indicators differ because acid/base strength affects the pH at the equivalence point and the steepness of the curve.

Q5. What is the difference between endpoint and equivalence point?

The equivalence point is the theoretical point at which stoichiometrically equivalent amounts have reacted. The endpoint is the observed signal, such as an indicator colour change. A good method minimizes the difference between them.

Q6. Why are hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide solutions standardized?

Their concentrations may change or cannot always be established accurately by direct weighing alone. Standardization determines their actual concentration by titration against a suitable standard using an approved method.

Q7. What is a neutralization curve?

A neutralization curve is a graph of pH against the volume of titrant added. It shows the initial pH, buffering region where present, steep pH change near equivalence and the pH after excess titrant has been added.

Q8. What is the assay of ammonium hydroxide intended to determine?

The assay determines the amount or concentration of ammonia expressed according to the specified official method, often by acidimetric titration. The exact sample handling, reaction conditions, calculation and endpoint must follow the current monograph.

Q9. What is non-aqueous titration?

Non-aqueous titration is an acid–base titration performed in a solvent other than water. It is useful for weak acids or weak bases that do not give a sufficiently sharp endpoint in water or have limited aqueous solubility.

Q10. Classify solvents used in non-aqueous titration.

Solvents are commonly described as protogenic (proton donors), protophilic (proton acceptors), amphiprotic (can donate and accept protons) and aprotic (neither readily donates nor accepts protons). Solvent selection changes acid–base behaviour and endpoint sharpness.

Q11. Differentiate between acidimetry and alkalimetry in non-aqueous titration.

Acidimetry uses a standard acid to determine a basic substance. Alkalimetry uses a standard base to determine an acidic substance. In non-aqueous methods, the solvent and titrant are chosen for the analyte's properties.

Q12. What is the estimation of sodium benzoate based on?

Sodium benzoate can be assayed using a suitable validated titrimetric method, including non-aqueous procedures where specified. The exact solvent, titrant, endpoint and stoichiometric calculation must be taken from the relevant current official monograph or laboratory method rather than assumed.

Q13. Define precipitation titration.

Precipitation titration is a volumetric method in which analyte and titrant react to form a sparingly soluble precipitate. Argentometric titrations using silver nitrate are common examples for halide determination.

Q14. Explain Mohr's method.

Mohr's method is a direct argentometric titration commonly used for chloride or bromide under controlled near-neutral conditions, using potassium chromate as indicator. After the halide has precipitated, excess silver ions form a brick-red silver chromate endpoint. The method has limitations with strongly acidic or alkaline samples and interfering ions.

Q15. Explain Volhard's method.

Volhard's method uses thiocyanate titration with ferric ions as an indicator in an acidic medium. It is commonly used as a back-titration for halides: excess silver nitrate is added to precipitate the halide, and remaining silver is titrated with thiocyanate. The exact procedure controls possible interference from the precipitate.

Q16. Explain Fajans' method.

Fajans' method is a precipitation titration using an adsorption indicator. Near the equivalence point, the surface charge of the precipitate changes and the indicator is adsorbed, producing a colour change. Selection depends on the precipitate and solution conditions.

Q17. What is gravimetric analysis?

Gravimetric analysis determines an analyte by measuring mass. In precipitation gravimetry, the analyte is converted into a sparingly soluble compound, which is filtered, washed, dried or ignited as required, and weighed in a known chemical form.

Q18. State the main steps of precipitation gravimetry.

The usual steps are sample preparation, controlled precipitation, digestion where needed, filtration, washing, drying or ignition, cooling in a desiccator, weighing to constant mass and calculation from the balanced reaction. Losses and contamination must be minimized.

Q19. What is the gravimetric estimation of barium sulphate based on?

In a suitable gravimetric procedure, sulphate is precipitated as barium sulphate, then the precipitate is collected, washed, dried or ignited as specified, and weighed. The amount of analyte is calculated from the known stoichiometric relationship. Follow the official procedure for the sample matrix and exact calculation.

Q20. Define complexometric titration.

Complexometric titration determines metal ions by forming a stable soluble complex with a ligand. EDTA is commonly used because it forms complexes with many metal ions in a defined stoichiometric ratio, often 1:1.

Q21. What is EDTA and why is it used?

EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, commonly used as its disodium salt) is a chelating agent that binds metal ions through several donor atoms. Its complexes are useful in quantitative analysis of ions such as calcium and magnesium. pH and indicator selection are important.

Q22. What are metallochromic indicators?

Metallochromic indicators form coloured complexes with metal ions. During a complexometric titration, EDTA binds the metal more strongly under suitable conditions, releasing the indicator and producing the endpoint colour change.

Q23. What are masking and demasking agents?

A masking agent selectively binds or otherwise prevents an interfering ion from reacting during an analysis. A demasking agent releases the masked ion so it can be determined later. Their use improves selectivity when multiple metal ions are present.

Q24. How are magnesium sulphate and calcium gluconate analysed by complexometry?

Complexometric assays can use EDTA to quantify magnesium or calcium under controlled pH and indicator conditions. Sample preparation, buffer, indicator, titrant concentration and calculation differ by official monograph; use the current prescribed procedure rather than applying one set of conditions to both substances.

Q25. Define oxidation and reduction.

Oxidation is loss of electrons or an increase in oxidation state. Reduction is gain of electrons or a decrease in oxidation state. They occur together in a redox reaction because electrons lost by one species are gained by another.

Q26. What are redox titrations?

Redox titrations use an oxidation–reduction reaction to determine the amount of analyte. The endpoint may be detected by the titrant's colour, a redox indicator or an instrumental signal.

Q27. What is permanganometry?

Permanganometry uses potassium permanganate as an oxidizing titrant. In acidic solution, permanganate is commonly reduced to manganese(II), and its intense purple colour can often indicate the endpoint without a separate indicator. The reaction medium and stoichiometry must be controlled.

Q28. What is cerimetry?

Cerimetry uses a cerium(IV) solution as an oxidizing titrant to determine reducing agents. The exact indicator and conditions depend on the analyte and method.

Q29. Differentiate between iodimetry and iodometry.

Iodimetry is a direct titration with standard iodine solution, commonly used for reducing agents. Iodometry is an indirect method in which an oxidizing analyte liberates iodine from iodide, and the liberated iodine is titrated, commonly with sodium thiosulphate.

Q30. What is the role of potassium iodate in redox analysis?

Potassium iodate is a stable oxidizing agent that can serve as a primary standard in certain methods and can generate iodine in the presence of iodide and acid. Its use depends on the specific reaction and validated analytical procedure.

UNIT IV · 10 HOURS

Gastrointestinal Agents, Antimicrobials and Radiopharmaceuticals

Q1. What are gastrointestinal agents?

Gastrointestinal agents are substances used to manage selected conditions of the digestive tract. This syllabus unit includes acidifiers, antacids, agents that promote bowel movement and antimicrobial agents used in defined contexts.

Q2. What are gastric acidifiers? Give examples from the syllabus.

Gastric acidifiers are substances intended to increase acidity in selected circumstances. The syllabus lists sodium acid phosphate and dilute hydrochloric acid. Their use depends on the clinical indication and prescribed formulation; they are not for self-treatment.

Q3. Define antacids.

Antacids are substances that neutralize some of the acid already present in the stomach and can provide temporary relief of acid-related symptoms. They do not all act in the same way and may interact with other medicines.

Q4. State the ideal properties of an antacid.

An ideal antacid would act promptly, neutralize acid effectively for a suitable period, avoid systemic alkalosis, not produce excessive gas or rebound acidity, not cause constipation or diarrhoea, have minimal interactions, be stable and palatable, and be safe at the intended dose. No single product perfectly meets every ideal.

Q5. Why are antacid combinations used?

Combinations may balance unwanted effects; for example, an aluminium-containing antacid tends to cause constipation, while a magnesium-containing antacid can cause diarrhoea. Combination products can also change onset and duration, but interactions and contraindications still matter.

Q6. Describe sodium bicarbonate as an antacid.

Sodium bicarbonate neutralizes gastric hydrochloric acid and produces sodium chloride, water and carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide can cause belching and abdominal distension, and sodium loading or excessive use may be unsuitable for some patients.

Reaction: NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂.

Q7. Describe aluminium hydroxide gel.

Aluminium hydroxide gel is an antacid that neutralizes gastric acid. It can cause constipation and may bind or reduce absorption of certain medicines. Its use and timing should follow product instructions or professional advice.

Q8. What are bowel-movement-promoting agents?

These are substances used to promote evacuation of the bowel in selected circumstances. The syllabus lists magnesium hydroxide, sodium orthophosphate, sodium potassium tartrate and magnesium trisilicate. Their actions, indications and safety profiles differ, and some salts can disturb fluid or electrolyte balance.

Q9. What is magnesium hydroxide used for?

Magnesium hydroxide can act as an antacid and, at appropriate formulations and doses, as an osmotic laxative. It can cause diarrhoea and may lead to magnesium accumulation in people with significant kidney impairment.

Q10. What are pharmaceutical antimicrobials?

Pharmaceutical antimicrobials are substances used to inhibit or destroy microorganisms in specified applications. The syllabus examples include potassium permanganate, boric acid, hydrogen peroxide, chlorinated lime, iodine and its preparations. Their suitability depends on concentration, formulation, route and official directions.

Q11. Describe potassium permanganate.

Potassium permanganate is a strong oxidizing agent that has been used in very dilute, appropriately prepared external solutions for selected purposes. Concentrated solutions can cause burns and are hazardous if swallowed. It must not be prepared or used on the body without a correct approved formulation and professional guidance.

Q12. Describe boric acid in pharmaceutical context.

Boric acid has been used in some external preparations, but safety depends on concentration, route and patient group. It can be toxic if absorbed or swallowed in sufficient amounts, and it should not be assumed safe for all eyes, wounds or mucosal surfaces. Follow current approved product directions.

Q13. What is hydrogen peroxide?

Hydrogen peroxide is an oxidizing agent that can release oxygen as it decomposes. Its pharmaceutical use depends on concentration and formulation. Concentrated solutions can injure tissue; only approved products should be used according to their labels.

Q14. What is chlorinated lime?

Chlorinated lime, historically called bleaching powder, contains available chlorine and has been used as a disinfecting material. Its activity and safe handling depend on the preparation and concentration. It must not be mixed with acids or ammonia-containing products because hazardous gases may be released.

Q15. Describe iodine and its preparations.

Iodine is an antiseptic active against a range of microorganisms. Pharmaceutical preparations may include iodine solutions or iodophors such as povidone-iodine. The product and route determine appropriate use; allergy, irritation and thyroid-related considerations may be relevant.

Q16. What is radioactivity?

Radioactivity is the spontaneous transformation of unstable atomic nuclei with emission of radiation. Types include alpha particles, beta particles and gamma rays. Different emissions have different penetration and biological effects.

Q17. What are radiopharmaceuticals?

Radiopharmaceuticals are medicinal preparations containing radionuclides used for diagnostic imaging or therapy. Their selection depends on the radionuclide, labelled compound, target tissue, physical half-life and clinical purpose.

Q18. State the uses of the radionuclides listed in the syllabus.

Iodine-131: used in selected thyroid diagnostic or therapeutic applications. Technetium-99m: widely used for diagnostic nuclear imaging because of its useful gamma emission and short half-life. Cobalt-60: a gamma source used in radiotherapy equipment and sterilization applications, subject to strict controls. Phosphorus-32: a beta emitter historically used in selected diagnostic or therapeutic settings. Specific use depends on current clinical protocols and regulations.

Q19. What precautions are required when handling radiopharmaceuticals?

Follow radiation-protection rules, authorization, staff training and institutional procedures. Core principles include minimizing time near sources, maximizing appropriate distance, using suitable shielding, monitoring exposure, preventing contamination, using labelled shielded containers and maintaining records. Only authorized personnel should handle these materials.

Q20. How should radiopharmaceuticals be stored and disposed of?

Storage and disposal must follow the applicable radiation-safety regulator, licensed facility procedures, radionuclide half-life, contamination status and waste classification. Never dispose of radioactive material in ordinary waste or drains unless an authorized procedure explicitly permits it.

UNIT V · 6 HOURS

Miscellaneous Compounds: Expectorants, Emetics, Haematinics, Poisons and Antidotes

Q1. What are expectorants?

Expectorants are substances intended to help loosen or clear respiratory mucus. Their clinical usefulness depends on the product and condition; they do not treat every cause of cough.

Q2. Describe potassium iodide as listed under expectorants.

Potassium iodide is an inorganic salt that has had specific medicinal uses, including some historical expectorant uses. It can affect thyroid function and interact with medicines, so it should only be used for an appropriate indication under professional guidance.

Q3. Describe ammonium chloride.

Ammonium chloride is an inorganic salt that has been used as an expectorant and, in selected clinical settings, as a systemic acidifying agent. Its use is limited by potential acid–base and electrolyte disturbances, so it requires appropriate supervision.

Q4. What are emetics?

Emetics are substances that induce vomiting. They have historical pharmaceutical importance, but inducing vomiting is not recommended for poisoning because it can cause aspiration or further injury. Suspected poisoning requires immediate advice from emergency services or a poison-control professional.

Q5. What is the significance of copper sulphate and sodium potassium tartrate in the syllabus?

They are listed under emetics in the supplied syllabus, reflecting historical pharmaceutical use. Copper sulphate is toxic and should not be used to induce vomiting. Sodium potassium tartrate is also known as Rochelle salt and has had various historical medicinal uses; neither should be used without a current approved indication.

Q6. Define haematinics.

Haematinics are substances that support haemoglobin formation and red-blood-cell production when a relevant deficiency exists. Treatment depends on the cause of anaemia and may involve iron, folate, vitamin B12 or other therapy as clinically indicated.

Q7. Describe ferrous sulphate.

Ferrous sulphate is an iron salt used to provide elemental iron in the prevention or treatment of iron deficiency when indicated. Gastrointestinal adverse effects are common, and accidental iron overdose can be dangerous, especially in children. Keep iron products securely stored.

Q8. Describe ferrous gluconate.

Ferrous gluconate is another iron salt used in oral iron preparations. The amount of elemental iron differs from the total salt mass, so products should be compared using their labelled elemental iron content and used as directed.

Q9. Define poison and antidote.

A poison is a substance that can cause harm when absorbed in a sufficient amount by a particular route. An antidote is an agent that counteracts a specific poison's effects or reduces its toxicity. Not all poisons have a specific antidote.

Q10. Classify antidotes.

Physical or mechanical antidotes reduce absorption by an appropriate mechanism. Chemical antidotes react with or bind a poison. Physiological or pharmacological antidotes oppose the poison's effects through a biological action. Modern poisoning care may also require supportive care and specific treatments selected by specialists.

Q11. What is sodium thiosulphate?

Sodium thiosulphate is an inorganic compound used in selected medical and analytical contexts. Clinically, it is used in specific circumstances such as part of certain cyanide-poisoning protocols and other specialist indications. Treatment of poisoning is an emergency and must be directed by trained clinicians; this summary is not a treatment instruction.

Q12. What should be done if poisoning is suspected?

Seek emergency medical help or contact the local poison-control service immediately. Do not induce vomiting, give improvised antidotes or delay care. Keep the container or label available if safe to do so, and follow professional instructions.
