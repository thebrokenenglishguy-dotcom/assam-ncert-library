UNIT I · 10 HOURS

Fundamentals of Pharmacognosy

Q1. Define pharmacognosy.

Pharmacognosy is the branch of pharmaceutical science that studies drugs and medicinal substances obtained from natural sources, including plants, animals, microorganisms, marine organisms and minerals. It covers their identity, history, constituents, quality, uses and evaluation.

Q2. Explain the scope of pharmacognosy.

Its scope includes identification of crude drugs, study of natural sources and active constituents, cultivation and conservation of medicinal plants, quality control, detection of adulteration, extraction and analysis of natural products, and research into potential pharmaceutical compounds.

Q3. Briefly describe the history and development of pharmacognosy.

People have used plants and other natural materials in healing for centuries. Traditional knowledge was recorded in systems such as Ayurveda, Unani and traditional Chinese medicine. Later, botanical identification, microscopy, chemistry and pharmacology improved drug evaluation. Modern pharmacognosy also uses chromatography, spectroscopy, DNA-based identification and other analytical tools.

Q4. What are crude drugs?

Crude drugs are natural substances used medicinally in whole, fragmented or relatively unprocessed form. They may consist of a plant part, animal material, microorganism-derived material or mineral substance. They can undergo cleaning, drying and size reduction while retaining their natural identity.

Q5. Classify the sources of crude drugs.

Sources include: (1) plant sources, such as leaves, roots, bark, flowers and seeds; (2) animal sources, such as certain animal-derived products; (3) microbial sources, including substances produced by bacteria or fungi; (4) marine sources, including organisms from the sea; and (5) mineral sources, including selected mineral materials used in pharmaceutical preparations.

Q6. Give examples of plant drugs and their parts.

Senna—leaflets; clove—flower buds; cinnamon—bark; ginger—rhizome; turmeric—rhizome; fennel—fruits commonly called seeds; isabgol—seed husk; and rauwolfia—root. The correct botanical identity and plant part are important for quality.

Q7. Name important historical milestones in natural-product drug discovery listed in the syllabus.

Examples include morphine from opium poppy, quinine from cinchona, aspirin developed from salicylate-related chemistry, warfarin linked historically to spoiled sweet clover and later developed as a medicine, penicillin from a fungus, cephalosporins from fungal sources, taxol (paclitaxel) first isolated from yew, and artemisinin from sweet wormwood.

Q8. State the natural origin and importance of morphine.

Morphine is an alkaloid originally isolated from opium poppy (Papaver somniferum). It is a potent opioid analgesic used in selected clinical situations. It can cause respiratory depression, dependence and other serious adverse effects, so it is subject to strict controls and professional supervision.

Q9. What is quinine?

Quinine is an alkaloid associated with cinchona bark. It has a historical role in treating malaria, although modern treatment depends on the malaria species, resistance patterns and current clinical guidelines. It can have important adverse effects and is not suitable for unsupervised use.

Q10. How is aspirin related to natural products?

Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) is a synthetic medicine developed from the history of salicylate compounds associated with plants such as willow. It is used for pain and inflammation and, in selected patients, for antiplatelet therapy. It can cause bleeding and other adverse effects.

Q11. What are penicillin and cephalosporins?

Penicillins and cephalosporins are beta-lactam antibiotic groups whose discovery was connected to microorganisms. They interfere with bacterial cell-wall synthesis. Their use depends on the infection, likely organism, resistance and patient factors; allergy and other adverse effects must be considered.

Q12. What is taxol?

Taxol is the former common name associated with paclitaxel, an anticancer drug originally isolated from the Pacific yew. Paclitaxel affects microtubule function and is used in selected cancer treatments under specialist supervision.

Q13. What is artemisinin?

Artemisinin is a natural compound isolated from Artemisia annua (sweet wormwood). Artemisinin derivatives are important components of combination therapies for malaria, following current treatment guidelines.

Q14. What is a pharmacopoeia?

A pharmacopoeia is an official collection of standards for the identity, purity, strength, tests and quality of drugs and pharmaceutical materials. It helps ensure that medicines meet defined quality requirements.

Q15. Name the pharmacopoeias and herbal compendia listed in the syllabus.

The syllabus names the Indian Pharmacopoeia, British Pharmacopoeia, United States Pharmacopeia—Herbal Medicines and Dietary Supplements, Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India, Unani Pharmacopoeia of India and American Herbal Pharmacopoeia. The exact scope and legal status differ between publications.

Q16. What is the difference between official and non-official drugs?

In this context, an official drug or crude drug is one included in a recognized official pharmacopoeia or compendium with a defined standard. A non-official drug is not included in the relevant official reference being used. This status depends on the jurisdiction and edition.

Q17. Differentiate between codified and non-codified drugs.

Codified drugs have an established written standard or monograph in a recognized compendium. Non-codified drugs lack such a monograph in that reference. Lack of a monograph does not automatically prove a substance is ineffective, safe or unsafe; quality and evidence must be evaluated.

Q18. Explain alphabetical classification of crude drugs.

Drugs are arranged by name in alphabetical order. It is simple to use for lookup but does not show botanical relationships, chemical composition or pharmacological action.

Q19. Explain morphological classification.

Crude drugs are grouped according to the plant part or form used, such as leaves, roots, rhizomes, bark, wood, flowers, fruits, seeds, whole herbs, exudates or unorganized products such as gums and resins. It is convenient for identification but does not necessarily group chemically similar drugs together.

Q20. Explain taxonomical classification.

Drugs are grouped according to botanical relationships, such as family, genus and species. It helps connect related plants but may place drugs with different chemical constituents or uses in the same group.

Q21. Explain chemical classification.

Crude drugs are grouped by major chemical constituents, such as alkaloids, glycosides, tannins, volatile oils, saponins or flavonoids. This can help in studying chemical properties and tests, but a single drug may contain several classes of compounds.

Q22. Explain pharmacological classification.

Drugs are grouped by their main biological or therapeutic action, such as laxatives, expectorants, carminatives or analgesics. This is useful for understanding effects, but one drug can have several actions and uses can change as evidence develops.

Q23. What is chemotaxonomic classification?

Chemotaxonomy uses chemical constituents or chemical patterns alongside taxonomic relationships to classify or compare organisms. It may help identify related species and guide natural-product research, but should be used with other evidence.

Q24. State the merits and limitations of crude-drug classification.

Alphabetical classification is easy to consult but provides little scientific grouping. Morphological classification is practical for whole drugs but less useful for powders or extracts. Taxonomical classification shows plant relationships but not necessarily chemical similarity. Chemical classification helps constituent-based study but can overlap. Pharmacological classification connects drugs to actions but may oversimplify. Chemotaxonomy adds chemical evidence but requires reliable analysis.

UNIT II · 8 HOURS

Cultivation, Collection, Processing and Storage of Drugs of Natural Origin

Q1. Why are medicinal plants cultivated?

Cultivation can provide a reliable supply, consistent quality, traceability and more sustainable harvesting. It also allows control over growing conditions and can reduce pressure on wild populations.

Q2. Name common methods of medicinal-plant cultivation.

Methods include seed propagation, vegetative propagation through cuttings or divisions, grafting where suitable, nursery raising and controlled cultivation. The best method depends on the species, plant part used, climate and production goals.

Q3. What is Good Agricultural and Collection Practice (GACP)?

GACP is a set of practices intended to maintain the identity, quality and safety of medicinal plant materials from cultivation or wild collection through harvesting and initial handling. It includes suitable site selection, correct identification, clean inputs, trained workers, harvest controls, traceability and documentation.

Q4. What factors affect cultivation of medicinal plants?

Important factors include climate, temperature, rainfall, light, altitude, soil type and pH, water availability, nutrients, season, seed or planting-material quality, pests, diseases and cultivation methods. These can affect both yield and the amount of active constituents.

Q5. What factors affect collection of medicinal plants?

The plant species, correct stage of growth, season, time of day, weather, plant part required, harvest technique and environmental conditions can affect quality. Correct identification and avoidance of contamination are essential.

Q6. Why does the time of collection matter?

The amount and profile of natural constituents can change with season, maturity and plant part. Therefore, the recommended collection stage should be selected according to the species and quality standard, rather than using one rule for all plants.

Q7. Describe the basic processing of crude drugs after collection.

Typical steps include sorting, removal of foreign matter, washing where appropriate, cutting or size reduction, drying, grading, packaging and labelling. Processing must be chosen to avoid loss or degradation of important constituents and to prevent contamination.

Q8. Why is drying important?

Drying reduces moisture that can encourage microbial growth, enzymatic changes and deterioration. Excessive heat or unsuitable drying conditions may destroy volatile or heat-sensitive constituents. The method and endpoint depend on the material.

Q9. What are suitable storage conditions for crude drugs?

Store correctly identified, adequately dried material in clean, suitable and labelled containers, protected from excessive moisture, heat, light, pests and contamination. Follow the monograph or validated storage specification for each drug.

Q10. What are plant hormones?

Plant hormones are chemical signals that regulate plant growth and development. Major groups include auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid and ethylene. Their effects depend on concentration, tissue and environmental conditions.

Q11. How can plant hormones be used in cultivation?

Depending on the crop and validated practice, plant growth regulators can support rooting, shoot formation, germination, flowering, fruit development or tissue culture. Their use must follow appropriate protocols because the response varies by species and dose.

Q12. What is polyploidy?

Polyploidy is the condition of having more than two complete sets of chromosomes. In plant breeding it may change plant size, fertility or metabolite production, but outcomes vary and must be assessed for the target species.

Q13. What are mutation and hybridization in medicinal-plant improvement?

Mutation is a change in genetic material that may create new traits. Hybridization crosses genetically different plants to combine selected characteristics. Breeding aims may include yield, disease resistance, adaptation and desired constituent profile, followed by testing for stability and quality.

Q14. Differentiate between in-situ and ex-situ conservation.

In-situ conservation protects a species in its natural habitat, for example through protected areas and habitat management. Ex-situ conservation protects it outside the natural habitat, for example in botanical gardens, seed banks, tissue culture collections or field gene banks.

Q15. What is eco-pharmacognosy?

Eco-pharmacognosy considers the ecological and sustainability aspects of medicinal natural products, including conservation, responsible sourcing, biodiversity, cultivation and the environmental effects of harvesting and trade.

Q16. Why should endangered medicinal plants such as kutki and chirata be conserved?

Overharvesting, habitat loss and unsustainable trade can reduce wild populations. Conservation helps protect biodiversity and future availability. Useful measures include habitat protection, cultivation, traceable sourcing, sustainable harvesting and public awareness. The status of a species should be checked against current conservation sources.

UNIT III · 8 HOURS

Quality Control of Drugs of Natural Origin

Q1. Why is quality control important for natural drugs?

Natural materials vary with species, growing conditions, harvest and processing. Quality control helps confirm identity, detect adulteration or contamination, assess consistency and verify that material meets the relevant quality standard.

Q2. What is adulteration of crude drugs?

Adulteration is the intentional or unintentional substitution, dilution or contamination of a crude drug with inferior, incorrect or unwanted material. It can reduce quality and may create safety risks.

Q3. What is organoleptic evaluation?

Organoleptic evaluation uses sensory observations such as appearance, colour, odour, taste when appropriate and texture. It is a quick preliminary check but cannot alone confirm identity, purity or safety. Unknown substances should not be tasted.

Q4. What is microscopic evaluation?

Microscopic evaluation examines characteristic structures, such as stomata, trichomes, fibres, vessels, starch grains, crystals and tissue arrangement. It is useful for identifying plant materials, including powdered crude drugs.

Q5. Differentiate between qualitative and quantitative microscopy.

Qualitative microscopy looks for diagnostic features to help identify a material. Quantitative microscopy measures or counts defined features, such as stomatal number, stomatal index, palisade ratio or vein-islet number, when applicable to the drug and method.

Q6. What is physical evaluation of crude drugs?

Physical evaluation measures properties such as moisture content, ash values, extractive values, swelling index, foaming index, viscosity, refractive index, optical rotation and other parameters specified for the material.

Q7. What is chemical evaluation?

Chemical evaluation uses chemical reactions or analytical techniques to identify or quantify constituents. Methods may include characteristic colour or precipitation tests, chromatography, spectroscopy and validated assays.

Q8. What is biological evaluation?

Biological evaluation uses a biological system or validated bioassay to assess a defined biological effect or activity. It is used when chemical content alone does not fully predict the required biological potency or response.

Q9. Define foreign organic matter.

Foreign organic matter consists of unwanted plant parts or other organic materials that should not be present in the crude drug, such as other species, weeds, mouldy fragments or extraneous material. Limits are specified by the relevant quality standard.

Q10. What is moisture content and why is it measured?

Moisture content is the amount of water in a material, expressed by a defined method. Excess moisture may encourage microbial growth, enzymatic degradation and poor storage stability. The acceptable limit depends on the particular drug and official method.

Q11. What are ash values?

Ash values measure inorganic residue remaining after a crude drug is incinerated under specified conditions. Total ash estimates total inorganic residue; acid-insoluble ash helps indicate siliceous material such as sand; water-soluble ash measures the water-soluble portion of the ash. Interpretation depends on the monograph.

Q12. What is extractive value?

Extractive value estimates the amount of material extracted from a crude drug by a specified solvent under stated conditions. Water-soluble and alcohol-soluble extractive values can help assess quality and detect some forms of substitution or exhaustion.

Q13. What is bitterness value?

Bitterness value is a comparative measure of the bitter taste intensity of a material relative to a defined reference, using a prescribed method. It is relevant for certain bitter crude drugs and must be performed only under an approved safe method.

Q14. What is the foaming index?

The foaming index is a measure used for crude drugs containing saponins or other foam-forming constituents. A specified dilution is shaken under standard conditions and foam formation is observed. The calculation follows the relevant pharmacopoeial procedure.

Q15. What is swelling index?

Swelling index estimates the volume occupied by a specified quantity of a material after it has swollen in a liquid under defined conditions. It is useful for mucilage-containing drugs such as isabgol husk.

Q16. Define viscosity, optical rotation and refractive index.

Viscosity is resistance to flow. Optical rotation is the rotation of plane-polarized light by an optically active substance. Refractive index describes how light changes direction when passing between media and can help characterize oils and liquids.

Q17. What are acid value and saponification value?

Acid value is the amount of potassium hydroxide, in milligrams, needed to neutralize free acids in one gram of a fat or oil. Saponification value is the amount of potassium hydroxide, in milligrams, needed to saponify one gram of fat or oil under the specified method. They help assess quality and composition.

Q18. What is DNA barcoding?

DNA barcoding uses a short, standardized DNA sequence to help identify a species. It can support authentication of medicinal plants, especially when morphology is unclear or the material is powdered. Results depend on suitable markers, reference sequences and sample quality; DNA barcoding does not by itself measure purity or active-constituent content.

Q19. How can adulteration of natural drugs be detected?

Methods include visual and organoleptic checks, microscopy, physical constants, chemical profiling, chromatographic comparison, DNA-based methods and testing for contaminants. A suitable combination is selected based on the drug, likely adulterants and official standard.

UNIT IV · 12 HOURS

Plant Metabolites and Traditional Systems of Medicine

Q1. Define plant metabolites.

Plant metabolites are small molecules produced during plant metabolism. They support growth, development, energy storage, defence, signalling and interactions with the environment.

Q2. Differentiate between primary and secondary metabolites.

Primary metabolites are directly involved in basic growth and survival, such as carbohydrates, proteins, nucleic acids and many lipids. Secondary metabolites often have ecological roles such as defence, attraction or signalling and include many alkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, tannins and glycosides. The distinction is useful but not absolute.

Q3. What are carbohydrates and why are they important?

Carbohydrates are compounds made mainly of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. They provide energy, structural material and storage. In pharmacognosy, examples include starch, gums, mucilages and sugars used as natural products or excipients.

Q4. What are proteins?

Proteins are chains of amino acids folded into specific structures. They perform roles in enzymes, transport, structure, signalling and defence. Natural sources may contain proteins or enzymes with pharmaceutical or diagnostic relevance.

Q5. What are lipids?

Lipids are a broad group of hydrophobic or amphipathic substances, including fats, oils, waxes and phospholipids. They store energy, form cell membranes and can serve as raw materials for pharmaceutical products.

Q6. Define alkaloids and give examples.

Alkaloids are a diverse group of naturally occurring nitrogen-containing compounds, many of which have pronounced biological activity. Examples include morphine, quinine, atropine and caffeine. Their effects vary widely, and some are toxic or controlled medicines.

Q7. What are glycosides?

Glycosides contain a sugar part (glycone) linked to a non-sugar part (aglycone or genin). Hydrolysis may separate these parts. Different glycoside groups include cardiac, anthraquinone, saponin and other glycosides, each with distinct properties and safety considerations.

Q8. What are flavonoids?

Flavonoids are plant polyphenolic compounds found in many fruits, leaves, flowers and other plant parts. They contribute to pigmentation and plant defence and are studied for various biological activities. Laboratory findings do not automatically prove clinical benefit.

Q9. What are tannins?

Tannins are polyphenolic compounds that can bind proteins and produce an astringent sensation. They occur in many barks, leaves and fruits and are used in some traditional preparations and quality tests.

Q10. What are terpenoids?

Terpenoids are a large group of natural products built from isoprene-derived units. They include volatile-oil constituents, pigments and other compounds. Examples include menthol, camphor and artemisinin, which have different properties and uses.

Q11. What are volatile oils?

Volatile oils are aromatic mixtures that evaporate readily and are often obtained from plants by distillation or other suitable methods. They may contain terpenes, terpenoids and aromatic compounds. Their composition can vary and some may irritate skin or be toxic if misused.

Q12. What are resins?

Resins are complex, usually non-volatile plant exudates or extracts that may contain terpenoid and related compounds. They can be used as natural materials in certain pharmaceutical products, depending on their identity and quality.

Q13. What are traditional systems of medicine?

Traditional systems of medicine are organized bodies of health knowledge and practice developed within cultural and historical settings. The syllabus mentions AYUSH systems and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). Their theories, diagnostic approaches and treatment practices differ, and evidence and safety should be assessed for each intervention.

Q14. What does AYUSH refer to in India?

AYUSH is an umbrella term for Ayurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha and Homoeopathy. The term is used in India's policy and institutional context. Each system has its own traditions, training and regulatory arrangements.

Q15. What is traditional Chinese medicine (TCM)?

TCM is a traditional medical system that includes practices such as herbal preparations, acupuncture and other approaches. Its concepts and practices differ from modern biomedical models. Specific products and interventions require assessment for evidence, quality, interactions and safety.

Q16. What is the role of pharmacognosy in traditional medicine?

Pharmacognosy helps authenticate natural ingredients, identify plant parts, assess chemical constituents, detect adulteration and contamination, establish quality specifications and study potential biological activity. These steps can improve consistency and support safety evaluation.

Q17. How are dosage forms used in AYUSH preparations?

Depending on the tradition and formulation, dosage forms can include powders, decoctions, tablets or pills, pastes, oils, fermented preparations, extracts and topical applications. Names, ingredients, preparation methods and doses vary, so products should be assessed against their applicable standards and labels.

Q18. How does pharmacognosy support allopathic medicine?

Natural products can provide drug leads, active compounds, excipients and analytical markers. Pharmacognosy supports identification, extraction, isolation, structure determination, quality control and research. A natural origin alone does not guarantee safety or effectiveness.

UNIT V · 7 HOURS

Phytotherapeutic Agents

For each agent below, learn its biological source, commonly studied constituents and syllabus-level uses. These summaries are for academic identification, not personal treatment recommendations.

Q1. What are adaptogens?

Adaptogens is a term used for substances traditionally or experimentally proposed to support adaptation to stress. Definitions and evidence vary, and the term should not be treated as proof that a product is effective or safe for everyone.

Q2. Describe Ashwagandha.

Biological source: roots of Withania somnifera. Major constituents: withanolides and related compounds. Syllabus category: adaptogen and immunomodulator. Product composition varies; interactions, adverse effects and suitability should be checked with a qualified professional.

Q3. Describe Tulsi.

Biological source: leaves of Ocimum tenuiflorum (also known as O. sanctum). Constituents: volatile-oil components such as eugenol in some chemotypes, plus other phenolic compounds. Traditional context: used in several traditional preparations. Chemical composition varies with variety and growing conditions.

Q4. Describe Amla.

Biological source: fruit of Phyllanthus emblica (synonym Emblica officinalis). Constituents: vitamin C and various polyphenolic compounds, with amounts affected by processing and storage. Traditional context: used in Ayurveda and food preparations.

Q5. Describe milk thistle.

Biological source: fruits of Silybum marianum. Major constituents: silymarin complex, including flavonolignans. Syllabus category: hepatoprotective agents. Evidence for particular liver conditions varies, and it should not replace medical evaluation or prescribed treatment.

Q6. Describe Kutki.

Biological source: rhizomes and roots of Picrorhiza kurroa. Constituents: iridoid glycosides including picrosides and kutkoside. Syllabus category: hepatoprotective agent. Wild populations face conservation concerns, so responsible sourcing is important.

Q7. Describe garlic.

Biological source: bulbs of Allium sativum. Constituents: organosulfur compounds, including allicin formed after tissue disruption. Syllabus category: cardiovascular support. Garlic products can interact with anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines and may increase bleeding risk in some situations.

Q8. Describe Arjuna.

Biological source: bark of Terminalia arjuna. Constituents: triterpenoids, tannins, flavonoids and other compounds. Syllabus category: cardiovascular agent in traditional use. It should not replace evidence-based treatment for heart disease.

Q9. Describe Gymnema.

Biological source: leaves of Gymnema sylvestre. Constituents: gymnemic acids and related saponins. Syllabus category: antidiabetic agent. Products may affect blood glucose and interact with diabetes medicines; clinical monitoring is important.

Q10. Describe Fenugreek.

Biological source: seeds of Trigonella foenum-graecum. Constituents: mucilage, steroidal saponins, trigonelline and other compounds. Syllabus category: antidiabetic agent in traditional use. It may affect blood glucose and can cause allergy or gastrointestinal effects.

Q11. Describe Turmeric.

Biological source: rhizomes of Curcuma longa. Major constituents: curcuminoids, including curcumin, and volatile oils. Syllabus category: anti-inflammatory and analgesic agent. Evidence depends on the condition and formulation; concentrated products may have interactions or adverse effects.

Q12. Describe Boswellia.

Biological source: oleo-gum-resin from Boswellia species, commonly B. serrata in South Asian products. Constituents: boswellic acids and volatile components. Syllabus category: anti-inflammatory agent. Product quality and evidence vary.

Q13. Describe Brahmi.

Common source in many Indian pharmacognosy texts: Bacopa monnieri, though the common name Brahmi can be applied to different plants regionally. Constituents: bacosides. Syllabus category: CNS agent. Correct botanical identification is essential because common names can be ambiguous.

Q14. Describe Ginkgo.

Biological source: leaves of Ginkgo biloba. Constituents: flavone glycosides and terpene lactones. Syllabus category: CNS agent. It may interact with medicines, including those affecting bleeding; product use should be discussed with a qualified professional.

Q15. Describe Neem.

Biological source: several parts of Azadirachta indica, depending on the preparation. Constituents: limonoids such as azadirachtin and other compounds. Syllabus category: antimicrobial and antiviral agent in the syllabus. Evidence and safety differ by preparation; some neem products can be toxic if swallowed, particularly concentrated oil.

Q16. Describe Andrographis.

Biological source: aerial parts of Andrographis paniculata. Major constituent: andrographolide. Syllabus category: antimicrobial/antiviral agent. It can cause adverse effects and interactions; use should not replace appropriate diagnosis or treatment.

Q17. Describe Psyllium.

Biological source: dried seeds or seed husk of Plantago ovata. Constituents: hydrophilic mucilage and soluble fibre. Syllabus category: gastrointestinal agent, commonly used as a bulk-forming laxative. It must be taken with adequate fluid and may affect the absorption of some medicines.

Q18. Describe Aloe.

Biological source: leaf gel or leaf latex from Aloe vera and related accepted species, depending on the preparation. Constituents: gel polysaccharides; latex may contain hydroxyanthracene derivatives such as aloin. Syllabus category: dermatological agent. Gel and latex are not interchangeable; oral latex products can cause serious adverse effects.

Q19. Describe Chasteberry.

Biological source: fruits of Vitex agnus-castus. Constituents: iridoids, flavonoids and diterpenes. Syllabus category: women's health. Evidence and suitability vary; possible hormone-related effects and medicine interactions should be considered.

Q20. Describe Shatavari.

Biological source: roots of Asparagus racemosus. Constituents: steroidal saponins known as shatavarins and other compounds. Syllabus category: women's health and traditional use. Clinical evidence for specific indications is variable.

Q21. Describe Vasaka.

Biological source: leaves of Justicia adhatoda (synonym Adhatoda vasica). Constituents: quinazoline alkaloids including vasicine and vasicinone. Syllabus category: respiratory agent, traditionally used in cough preparations. Safety and suitability depend on the preparation and patient.

Q22. Why must botanical source and plant part be recorded for each crude drug?

Different species or different parts of the same plant may contain different constituents and have different safety profiles. Recording the accepted scientific name, plant part, source, harvest and processing information supports authentication, reproducibility and traceability.

Q23. What precautions are important when studying phytotherapeutic agents?

Natural products can cause adverse effects, vary in composition, be contaminated or adulterated, and interact with medicines. A traditional use or laboratory result is not proof of clinical effectiveness. Use reliable quality-controlled sources and qualified advice for clinical decisions.
