UNIT I · 12 HOURS

Introduction to Human Body, Cells, Tissues and Cell Injury

Basic Anatomical Terminology

1. Planes

• a) Median Plane: The median plane is an imaginary vertical plane that divides the body into two equal halves: right and left.

• b) Sagittal Plane: The sagittal plane is an imaginary vertical plane that divides the body into right and left portions, which may be unequal.

• c) Coronal Plane: The coronal plane is an imaginary vertical plane that divides the body into front (anterior) and back (posterior) parts.

• d) Transverse Plane: The transverse plane is an imaginary horizontal plane that divides the body into upper (superior) and lower (inferior) parts.

2. Directional Terms

• a) Cranial: Refers to a direction towards the head or upper part of the body.

• b) Anterior: Refers to the front part of the body.

• c) Posterior: Refers to the back part of the body.

• d) Medial: Refers to a position closer to the midline of the body.

• e) Lateral: Refers to a position away from the midline of the body.

• f) Proximal: Refers to a position closer to the point of attachment or origin of a limb.

• g) Distal: Refers to a position farther from the point of attachment or origin of a limb.

• h) Plantar: Refers to the sole or bottom surface of the foot.

• i) Dorsal of the Foot: Refers to the upper surface of the foot.

• j) Palmar: Refers to the palm or front surface of the hand.

• k) Dorsal of the Hand: Refers to the back surface of the hand.

• l) Parietal: Refers to the layer of a membrane that lines the wall of a body cavity.

• m) Visceral: Refers to the layer of a membrane that covers the surface of an internal organ.

Q1. Define anatomy, physiology and pathophysiology.

• Anatomy is the study of body structures and how they are arranged.

• Physiology is the study of how the body and its parts function.

• Pathophysiology studies the functional changes associated with disease or injury.

Q2. Explain the levels of structural organization in the human body.

• The main levels are: (1) chemical level—atoms and molecules

• (2) cellular level—cells

• (3) tissue level—groups of similar cells and their surrounding material

• (4) organ level—two or more tissues working together

• (5) organ-system level—organs working together; and (6) organism level—the complete human being.

Q3. What are the major organ systems of the body?

• The major systems are integumentary, skeletal, muscular, nervous, endocrine, cardiovascular, lymphatic/immune, respiratory, digestive, urinary and reproductive systems.

• They work together to maintain life and internal balance.

Q4. What are the basic life processes?

• Basic life processes include metabolism, responsiveness, movement, growth, differentiation and reproduction.

• The body also requires nutrition, oxygen, waste removal and regulation of internal conditions to survive.

Q5. Define homeostasis.

• Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite changes inside or outside the body.

• Examples include regulation of body temperature, blood glucose, blood pressure, pH and fluid balance.

Q6. Explain negative and positive feedback.

• Negative feedback opposes a change and helps return a variable toward its usual range; body temperature regulation is an example.

• Positive feedback strengthens a change until a particular event is completed; uterine contractions during childbirth are a common example.

Q7. Give examples of basic anatomical terms.

• Superior: toward the head.

• Inferior: toward the feet.

• Anterior: toward the front.

• Posterior: toward the back.

• Medial: toward the midline.

• Lateral: away from the midline.

• Proximal: nearer to the trunk or point of origin.

• Distal: farther away.

• Superficial: near the surface.

• Deep: farther from the surface.

Q8. What are the main body planes?

• The sagittal plane divides the body into left and right parts; the midsagittal plane divides it into equal left and right halves.

• The frontal or coronal plane divides front and back.

• The transverse or horizontal plane divides upper and lower parts.

Q9. What is a cell? Explain its main structure and functions.

• A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of the human body. Human cells differ in shape, size and function.

• Plasma membrane: a selectively permeable phospholipid bilayer with proteins, cholesterol and carbohydrates. It separates the cell from its surroundings and controls transport, recognition and signalling.

• Cytoplasm: includes cytosol and organelles between the plasma membrane and nucleus. Many metabolic reactions occur here.

• Nucleus: contains most cellular DNA. Nuclear pores regulate exchange with the cytoplasm; the nucleolus makes ribosomal components.

• Mitochondria: produce most cellular ATP and participate in calcium regulation and cell-death pathways.

• Ribosomes: make proteins. Free ribosomes mainly make cytosolic proteins; attached ribosomes make many secreted and membrane proteins.

• Rough endoplasmic reticulum: synthesizes and processes many proteins. Smooth endoplasmic reticulum: makes lipids, supports detoxification and stores calcium in some cells.

• Golgi apparatus: modifies, sorts and packages proteins and lipids into vesicles.

• Lysosomes: digest waste, microbes and worn-out cell parts. Peroxisomes: break down fatty acids and help neutralize hydrogen peroxide.

• Cytoskeleton: microfilaments, intermediate filaments and microtubules maintain shape, movement and intracellular transport.

• Centrosome/centrioles: organize microtubules and help form the mitotic spindle. Vesicles: transport and store substances.

Q10. Describe important cell organelles and their functions.

• Nucleus: stores DNA and regulates gene expression and cell activities.

• Nucleolus: produces ribosomal RNA and assembles ribosomal subunits.

• Mitochondria: generate ATP and help regulate calcium and programmed cell death.

• Ribosomes: synthesize proteins by translating messenger RNA.

• Rough endoplasmic reticulum: makes and folds many proteins for secretion, membranes and organelles.

• Smooth endoplasmic reticulum: makes lipids, supports detoxification and stores calcium in some cells.

• Golgi apparatus: modifies, sorts and packages proteins and lipids for delivery.

• Lysosomes: digest material taken into the cell and recycle damaged organelles.

• Peroxisomes: break down very-long-chain fatty acids and help neutralize hydrogen peroxide.

• Cytoskeleton: maintains shape, supports movement and intracellular transport, and helps cell division.

• Centrosome and centrioles: organize microtubules and assist spindle formation.

• Plasma membrane: controls entry and exit of substances and contains receptors for communication.

Q11. State the functions of important cell organelles.

• Mitochondria: produce much of the cell's ATP.

• Ribosomes: make proteins.

• Rough endoplasmic reticulum: helps synthesize and process many proteins.

• Smooth endoplasmic reticulum: participates in lipid synthesis and other processes.

• Golgi apparatus: modifies, sorts and packages materials.

• Lysosomes: digest cellular material.

• Nucleus: stores DNA and regulates gene expression.

Q12. Explain transport across the cell membrane, including mechanism and examples.

• 1. Simple diffusion (passive): small non-polar molecules move directly through the lipid bilayer from higher to lower concentration without ATP. Example: oxygen and carbon dioxide.

• 2. Facilitated diffusion (passive): substances move down their concentration or electrochemical gradient through channel or carrier proteins. Channels form pores; carriers bind the substance and change shape. Example: glucose entering many cells through GLUT carriers; ions passing through ion channels.

• 3. Osmosis (passive): water moves across a selectively permeable membrane toward the side with higher effective solute concentration, often through aquaporins. Example: water movement across red blood cell membranes.

• 4. Filtration: water and small solutes pass through a membrane due to a pressure difference. Example: filtration in kidney glomeruli.

• 5. Primary active transport: membrane pumps use ATP directly to move substances against their gradients. Example: the sodium-potassium pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ into the cell per ATP.

• 6. Secondary active transport: a carrier uses energy stored in an ion gradient, commonly the Na⁺ gradient. Symport moves substances in the same direction; antiport moves them in opposite directions. Example: sodium-glucose cotransport in intestine and kidney.

• 7. Endocytosis: the membrane surrounds material and forms a vesicle. Phagocytosis takes in large particles; pinocytosis takes in fluid; receptor-mediated endocytosis selectively takes in ligand-bound substances. Example: immune cells engulfing microbes.

• 8. Exocytosis: intracellular vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release contents outside. Example: release of neurotransmitters or insulin.

• Key difference: passive transport moves down a gradient without direct energy use; active transport uses energy directly or indirectly and can move substances against a gradient.

Q13. What is osmosis?

• Osmosis is the net movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential toward lower water potential.

• It helps regulate cell volume and fluid movement.

Q14. Explain cell division and the stages of mitosis.

• Cell division produces new cells for growth, repair and replacement. The cell cycle includes interphase and the M phase.

• Interphase—G₁: the cell grows, performs normal functions and makes proteins and organelles.

• Interphase—S phase: DNA is replicated so each chromosome has two sister chromatids.

• Interphase—G₂: the cell grows further, checks replicated DNA and prepares for division.

• Prophase: chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes; the mitotic spindle begins to form.

• Prometaphase: the nuclear envelope breaks down and spindle fibres attach to chromosome kinetochores.

• Metaphase: chromosomes line up at the cell equator and spindle attachment is checked.

• Anaphase: sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles.

• Telophase: chromosomes reach the poles, begin to uncoil and new nuclear envelopes form.

• Cytokinesis: the cytoplasm divides, usually producing two genetically similar daughter cells. In animal cells, a cleavage furrow forms.

• Meiosis: produces cells with half the chromosome number through meiosis I (homologous chromosomes separate) and meiosis II (sister chromatids separate). Crossing over in prophase I increases genetic variation.

• Cell-cycle checkpoints monitor DNA integrity, replication and spindle attachment. Failed regulation can contribute to abnormal cell growth.

Q15. Explain the general principles and importance of cell communication.

• Meaning: cell communication is how cells send, receive and respond to signals from other cells or their environment.

• Basic sequence: a signalling cell releases or displays a signal; a target cell with the correct receptor detects it; intracellular pathways transmit the message; effector molecules produce a response; the signal is then switched off.

• Importance: coordinates growth, development, tissue repair, immune responses, metabolism, secretion, movement, nerve transmission and homeostasis.

• Specificity: cells usually respond only if they have the appropriate receptor. The same signal can produce different responses in different cells.

• Amplification: one activated receptor can activate many downstream molecules, producing a large response from a small signal.

• Regulation: signals may be broken down, taken back up or removed; receptors and signalling proteins can be inactivated to stop the response.

Q16. Classify cell signalling types and explain each with examples.

• 1. Contact-dependent: a membrane-bound signal on one cell binds a receptor on a neighbouring cell; direct contact is needed. Example: signalling between immune cells.

• 2. Paracrine: a local mediator acts on nearby cells. Example: nitric oxide relaxing nearby vascular smooth muscle.

• 3. Autocrine: a signal acts on the cell that released it or similar nearby cells. Example: some immune-cell cytokines.

• 4. Synaptic: a neuron releases a neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft to act on a nearby target. Example: acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction.

• 5. Endocrine: hormones travel in blood to distant target cells. Example: insulin released by pancreatic beta cells acts on muscle and fat.

• 6. Neuroendocrine: a neuron releases a neurohormone into blood. Example: antidiuretic hormone.

• Intracellular signalling refers to events inside a target cell after a signal is detected; it is the pathway process rather than a distance-based signalling type.

Q17. Define tissue and classify the four basic types of tissue.

• Tissue: a group of similar cells, together with their extracellular material where present, that work together to perform a particular function.

• 1. Epithelial tissue: closely packed cells arranged in sheets; covers body surfaces, lines cavities and forms glands. Main functions include protection, absorption, secretion and filtration.

• 2. Connective tissue: cells are separated by extracellular matrix containing ground substance and fibres. It supports, binds, protects, stores energy and transports substances. Examples include areolar tissue, adipose tissue, cartilage, bone and blood.

• 3. Muscular tissue: made of contractile cells containing actin and myosin. It produces movement, maintains posture and generates heat. Types are skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle.

• 4. Nervous tissue: consists of neurons and supporting glial cells. It detects stimuli, processes information and transmits electrical and chemical signals.

Q18. Explain epithelial tissue, its classification, structure, locations and functions.

• General structure: epithelial cells are closely packed with little extracellular matrix, rest on a basement membrane, have an apical surface and a basal surface, and usually lack blood vessels. Nutrients diffuse from underlying connective tissue. Cells are joined by specialised junctions.

• Classification basis 1—number of layers: simple epithelium has one cell layer; stratified epithelium has multiple layers; pseudostratified epithelium appears multilayered but all cells contact the basement membrane; transitional epithelium changes shape when stretched.

• Classification basis 2—cell shape: squamous cells are flat; cuboidal cells are approximately cube-shaped; columnar cells are taller than they are wide.

• 1. Simple squamous: one layer of flat cells. Location: alveoli of lungs, lining of blood vessels (endothelium), serous membranes and kidney glomerular capsule. Function: rapid diffusion and filtration; reduces friction in serous cavities.

• 2. Simple cuboidal: one layer of cube-shaped cells. Location: kidney tubules, small ducts of glands and thyroid follicles. Function: absorption and secretion.

• 3. Simple columnar: one layer of tall cells; may contain microvilli and goblet cells. Location: stomach and intestines; ciliated type occurs in some small airways and uterine tubes. Function: absorption and secretion; cilia move material.

• 4. Pseudostratified columnar: cells of different heights with nuclei at different levels; all cells contact the basement membrane. Location: ciliated type in much of the upper respiratory tract; non-ciliated type in parts of the male reproductive tract. Function: mucus secretion and movement of trapped particles; secretion and absorption in some ducts.

• 5. Stratified squamous, keratinised: many layers with a surface layer of dead, keratin-filled cells. Location: epidermis of skin. Function: protects against abrasion, water loss and entry of microbes.

• 6. Stratified squamous, non-keratinised: many layers with moist living surface cells. Location: mouth, oesophagus, vagina and anal canal. Function: protection from abrasion while keeping the surface moist.

• 7. Stratified cuboidal: usually two or more layers with cuboidal surface cells. Location: larger ducts of sweat, mammary and salivary glands. Function: protection and limited secretion.

• 8. Stratified columnar: multiple layers with columnar surface cells; relatively uncommon. Location: parts of the male urethra and large ducts of some glands. Function: protection and secretion.

• 9. Transitional epithelium (urothelium): stratified epithelium with surface cells that change shape during stretching. Location: urinary bladder, ureters and part of the urethra. Function: stretches as urine volume changes and forms a protective barrier against urine.

• Glandular epithelium: specialised epithelial cells form glands. Exocrine glands release products through ducts (e.g., sweat glands); endocrine glands release hormones into blood or tissue fluid (e.g., thyroid gland).

Q19. Explain muscular tissue and compare its three types.

• General structure: muscle cells (fibres) contain actin and myosin proteins that slide past each other to produce contraction. Muscle tissue is specialised for force and movement.

• 1. Skeletal muscle: long cylindrical fibres, striated, multinucleated with nuclei near the periphery. Location: attached mainly to bones and some structures such as the tongue. Function: voluntary movement, posture, joint stabilisation and heat production. Example: biceps muscle.

• 2. Cardiac muscle: short, branched, striated cells, usually with one central nucleus and intercalated discs containing mechanical junctions and gap junctions. Location: myocardium of the heart. Function: involuntary rhythmic contractions pump blood.

• 3. Smooth muscle: spindle-shaped cells without visible striations, usually with one central nucleus. Location: walls of intestine, blood vessels, bladder, uterus and airways. Function: involuntary movement of contents, regulation of vessel diameter and control of organ openings.

• Key comparison: skeletal muscle is striated and generally voluntary; cardiac muscle is striated and involuntary; smooth muscle is non-striated and involuntary.

Q20. Explain connective tissue, its structure, classification, locations and functions.

• General structure: connective tissue usually has cells spread within an extracellular matrix made of ground substance and fibres. Collagen fibres provide tensile strength, elastic fibres allow stretch and recoil, and reticular fibres form delicate supporting networks.

• 1. Areolar (loose) connective tissue: loosely arranged collagen and elastic fibres with fibroblasts and immune cells. Location: beneath epithelia, around vessels and nerves, between organs. Function: binds tissues, supports vessels, holds tissue fluid and participates in defence.

• 2. Adipose tissue: mainly adipocytes storing fat. Location: under skin, around kidneys and other organs, and in some marrow spaces. Function: energy storage, insulation, cushioning and hormone signalling.

• 3. Reticular connective tissue: fine branching reticular fibres and supporting cells. Location: lymph nodes, spleen and bone marrow. Function: forms a supporting framework for blood and immune cells.

• 4. Dense regular connective tissue: parallel collagen bundles with fibroblasts. Location: tendons and many ligaments. Function: resists pulling forces mainly in one direction.

• 5. Dense irregular connective tissue: collagen bundles arranged in many directions. Location: dermis and fibrous capsules around organs and joints. Function: resists stress from multiple directions.

• 6. Elastic connective tissue: rich in elastic fibres. Location: walls of large elastic arteries and some spinal ligaments. Function: permits stretch and recoil.

• 7. Cartilage: chondrocytes lie in lacunae within a firm, hydrated matrix; cartilage is avascular. Hyaline cartilage: joints, nose, trachea and costal cartilages; smooth support and reduced friction. Elastic cartilage: external ear and epiglottis; flexible support. Fibrocartilage: intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis and menisci; resists compression and tensile stress.

• 8. Bone: osteocytes lie in lacunae within a mineralised collagen matrix; bone is vascular. Location: skeleton. Function: support, protection, movement with muscles, mineral storage and housing of marrow for blood-cell production.

• 9. Blood: red cells, white cells and platelets suspended in plasma. Location: heart and blood vessels. Function: transport of gases, nutrients, hormones and waste; immune defence and clotting.

• 10. Lymph: fluid connective tissue containing fluid and immune cells within lymphatic vessels. Function: returns excess tissue fluid to blood, transports absorbed fats and supports immune defence.

Q21. Define cell injury.

• Cell injury occurs when a harmful stimulus or stress disrupts normal cell function.

• If the damage is mild or brief, the cell may recover; if it is severe or persistent, irreversible damage and cell death may occur.

Q22. Name common causes of cellular injury.

• Causes include oxygen deprivation, physical agents, chemicals and drugs, infections, immune reactions, genetic defects, nutritional imbalance and ageing.

• The effect depends on the type, strength and duration of the harmful stimulus and the cell's condition.

Q23. Explain major mechanisms of cell injury.

• Mechanisms include ATP depletion, mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium imbalance, oxidative stress from free radicals, damage to cell membranes, protein misfolding or ribosome dysfunction, DNA damage and activation of cell-death pathways.

Q24. What is reversible cell injury?

• Reversible cell injury is damage from which the cell can recover if the harmful cause is removed.

• Cell swelling and fatty change in some cells are common examples.

• Continued or severe injury may progress to irreversible damage.

Q25. What is irreversible cell injury?

• Irreversible cell injury is damage beyond the cell's ability to recover, often involving severe membrane damage, mitochondrial failure and major nuclear changes.

• It can lead to cell death by necrosis or apoptosis, depending on the process.

Q26. Explain normal cellular responses to different levels of stress.

• Normal conditions: cells maintain homeostasis, make ATP, synthesise needed molecules and perform specialised functions.

• Low or mild stress: cells may adapt by adjusting metabolism or function while maintaining viability. If the stress is removed, normal function may continue.

• Mild-to-moderate persistent stress: cells may develop adaptation such as atrophy, hypertrophy, hyperplasia or metaplasia, depending on the stimulus and tissue. Adaptation has limits.

• Severe or prolonged stress: adaptive capacity is exceeded; ATP production falls, calcium balance and membranes may be disturbed, proteins and DNA may be damaged, and cell injury can progress to cell death.

• Important distinction: adaptation is not the same as injury. If the harmful stimulus is removed early, reversible injury may recover; persistent severe injury may become irreversible.

Q27. Define atrophy, hypertrophy and hyperplasia, with causes and examples.

• Atrophy: decrease in cell size, often causing a smaller tissue or organ. Causes include disuse, loss of nerve supply, reduced blood supply, inadequate nutrition, loss of hormonal stimulation and ageing. Example: skeletal muscle wasting after prolonged immobilisation.

• Hypertrophy: increase in cell size, leading to enlargement of a tissue or organ. It occurs when workload or hormonal stimulation increases. Example: skeletal muscle enlargement with resistance training; uterine smooth muscle enlargement during pregnancy.

• Hyperplasia: increase in the number of cells due to proliferation. It occurs in tissues capable of cell division and may be physiological or pathological. Example: glandular breast tissue growth during pregnancy; endometrial hyperplasia with excessive oestrogen stimulation.

• Difference: atrophy means smaller cells; hypertrophy means larger cells; hyperplasia means more cells. Hypertrophy and hyperplasia can occur together in some tissues.

Q28. Define metaplasia and dysplasia, with causes and examples.

• Metaplasia: a potentially reversible change in which one mature cell type is replaced by another mature cell type better able to tolerate a persistent stress. Example: in smokers, some respiratory epithelium may change from ciliated columnar to stratified squamous epithelium.

• Causes of metaplasia: chronic irritation, inflammation or altered local signals. Although adaptive, it may reduce normal function and can increase risk of further abnormal changes if the stimulus persists.

• Dysplasia: disordered cell growth and maturation with variation in cell size and shape, abnormal organisation and sometimes increased mitotic activity. It is not simply a normal adaptation.

• Example of dysplasia: cervical epithelial dysplasia associated with persistent high-risk human papillomavirus infection.

• Clinical significance: dysplasia can regress, persist or progress depending on grade, tissue and cause; some forms are precancerous, but dysplasia does not always mean cancer.

Q29. What is cell swelling?

• Cell swelling occurs when water accumulates inside a cell, often because ATP depletion disrupts ion pumps in the cell membrane.

• Sodium accumulates inside and water follows.

• It is a common early feature of reversible cell injury.

Q30. What is intracellular accumulation?

• Intracellular accumulation is the abnormal build-up of substances within cells due to altered metabolism, defective removal or excessive entry.

• Examples can include fat, proteins, glycogen or pigments, depending on the condition.

Q31. Differentiate between necrosis and apoptosis.

• Necrosis is a form of cell death associated with severe injury, loss of membrane integrity and often inflammation.

• Apoptosis is a regulated cell-death process in which cells are dismantled in an orderly way and usually cause less surrounding inflammation.

Q32. Define commonly used medical terms: sign, symptom, syndrome and diagnosis.

• Sign: an observable or measurable finding.

• Symptom: a subjective experience reported by the patient.

• Syndrome: a group of signs and symptoms that occur together.

• Diagnosis: identification of a disease or condition based on assessment and evidence.

Q33. What are cell junctions? Classify them and state their structure and functions.

• Cell junctions connect neighbouring cells to each other or attach cells to extracellular matrix. They provide sealing, mechanical strength and communication.

• 1. Tight junctions (zonula occludens): seal spaces between adjacent cells and limit movement between them. Example: intestinal epithelium and kidney tubules.

• 2. Adherens junctions (zonula adherens): connect actin filaments between cells through cadherins and help cells stick together and change shape. Example: epithelial sheets.

• 3. Desmosomes (macula adherens): strong spot-like attachments linking intermediate filaments; they resist stretching. Example: epidermis and cardiac muscle.

• 4. Hemidesmosomes: attach epithelial cells to the basement membrane through integrins and intermediate filaments.

• 5. Gap junctions: channels made of connexins connect neighbouring cell cytoplasm and allow ions and small molecules to pass. Example: electrical coordination in cardiac muscle.

• 6. Focal adhesions: integrin-based attachments connect actin to extracellular matrix and help cells attach, migrate and sense mechanical signals.

• Classification: sealing—tight junctions; anchoring—adherens junctions, desmosomes, hemidesmosomes and focal adhesions; communicating—gap junctions.

Q34. What are signalling molecules and receptors? Give examples.

• Signalling molecules (ligands) carry information. Examples include hormones (insulin, adrenaline), neurotransmitters (acetylcholine), cytokines, growth factors and local mediators such as nitric oxide.

• Cell-surface receptors bind signals that cannot readily cross the membrane. Examples: G-protein-coupled receptors for adrenaline, insulin receptor tyrosine kinase, and ligand-gated ion channels for some neurotransmitters.

• Intracellular receptors bind lipid-soluble signals that cross the membrane. Examples include receptors for cortisol and thyroid hormone; activated receptors can regulate gene expression.

• Specific binding: a ligand binds a matching receptor and changes its activity to start a response.

• Receptor regulation: cells can increase or decrease receptor number or sensitivity; prolonged exposure may cause desensitisation or down-regulation.

Q35. Explain intracellular signalling pathways activated by an extracellular signal molecule.

• Step 1—Signal release: a signalling cell releases a ligand, such as adrenaline, insulin or a growth factor.

• Step 2—Receptor binding: the ligand binds its specific receptor on the target-cell surface or inside the cell if it can cross the membrane.

• Step 3—Receptor activation: binding changes receptor activity. It may open an ion channel, activate a G protein or trigger protein phosphorylation.

• Step 4—Intracellular relay: signalling proteins pass the message through the cell; second messengers such as cAMP or Ca²⁺ may be produced.

• Step 5—Amplification and integration: one activated molecule can activate many downstream molecules, and pathways can interact.

• Step 6—Cellular response: target proteins change enzyme activity, ion movement, secretion, contraction, metabolism or gene expression.

• Step 7—Termination: the ligand is removed, the receptor is inactivated, second messengers are broken down and phosphatases remove phosphate groups.

• Example—cAMP pathway: adrenaline binds a suitable GPCR; a stimulatory G protein activates adenylyl cyclase; ATP is converted to cAMP; cAMP activates protein kinase A; the kinase phosphorylates target proteins and changes cell activity. Phosphodiesterase breaks down cAMP to help stop the signal.

• Example—insulin pathway: insulin binds its receptor tyrosine kinase and activates pathways including PI3K–Akt. In muscle and fat cells, this promotes GLUT4 movement to the membrane and increases glucose uptake.

Q36. List the major functions of a human cell.

• Metabolism: chemical reactions that build or break down substances and provide energy.

• Absorption and transport: uptake of nutrients and movement of materials across or within the cell.

• Synthesis: production of proteins, lipids, nucleic acids and other molecules.

• Secretion and excretion: release of products such as hormones and removal of metabolic waste.

• Energy production: formation of ATP to power cellular activities.

• Response and communication: detection of signals and responses through receptors and pathways.

• Movement: cell movement or movement of materials inside the cell.

• Growth, repair and reproduction: increase in size, replacement of damaged parts and cell division.

• Homeostasis: regulation of ion levels, pH, water balance and internal conditions.

Q37. Explain nervous tissue, its structure, location and functions.

• Structure: nervous tissue contains neurons and supporting glial cells. Neurons have a cell body, dendrites that receive signals and an axon that carries signals away; synapses allow communication with other cells.

• Neurons: excitable cells that receive, process and transmit electrical signals and communicate chemically at many synapses.

• Neuroglia (glial cells): support, protect, nourish and insulate neurons and help maintain the neural environment. Examples include astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia and ependymal cells in the CNS, and Schwann and satellite cells in the PNS.

• Location: brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves, ganglia and sensory structures.

• Functions: detect sensory information, integrate and process information, control muscles and glands, coordinate body activities, support reflexes, memory and learning, and maintain homeostasis.

Q38. Explain the pathogenesis of cell injury, including membrane, mitochondrial, nuclear and ribosomal damage.

• Pathogenesis: the sequence of molecular and cellular events through which a harmful stimulus causes cell dysfunction, injury and, if severe, cell death.

• 1. ATP depletion: hypoxia or mitochondrial injury reduces ATP production. Ion pumps fail, sodium and water enter the cell, cell swelling occurs, and anaerobic glycolysis may increase lactic acid and lower intracellular pH.

• 2. Plasma membrane damage: lipid peroxidation, toxins, calcium-activated enzymes and ATP depletion can damage membrane lipids and proteins. The cell may lose ions and metabolites, take in excess water and leak enzymes; severe damage can lead to necrosis.

• 3. Mitochondrial damage: calcium overload, reactive oxygen species or toxins can impair oxidative phosphorylation and reduce ATP. Mitochondria may release proteins that activate apoptosis; severe dysfunction promotes cell death.

• 4. Calcium imbalance: excess cytosolic calcium activates phospholipases, proteases, endonucleases and ATPases. These enzymes damage membranes, cytoskeleton, DNA and energy stores.

• 5. Oxidative stress: reactive oxygen species damage membrane lipids, proteins and nucleic acids when antioxidant defences cannot neutralise them.

• 6. Nuclear and DNA damage: radiation, chemicals, oxidative stress or replication errors can damage DNA. The cell may pause the cycle and attempt repair; severe damage may activate apoptosis. In necrosis, nuclear changes include pyknosis (shrinkage), karyorrhexis (fragmentation) and karyolysis (fading/dissolution).

• 7. Ribosomal and endoplasmic reticulum damage: ATP depletion or toxins can detach ribosomes from rough ER and inhibit protein synthesis. Loss of essential enzymes and structural proteins impairs cell maintenance and repair.

• 8. Protein misfolding and ER stress: damaged or misfolded proteins accumulate in the ER. The cell first activates protective responses; prolonged stress can trigger apoptosis.

• 9. Lysosomal damage: rupture can release digestive enzymes into the cytoplasm and worsen cellular breakdown.

• 10. Outcomes: mild injury may be reversible if the cause is removed. Persistent severe injury may cause necrosis or regulated cell death such as apoptosis.

Q39. Explain the morphology of reversible cell injury.

• Meaning: morphology means the visible structural changes in a cell or tissue. In reversible injury, the cell can recover if the harmful cause is removed in time.

• 1. Cell swelling (hydropic change): ATP falls and membrane ion pumps work poorly. Sodium and water enter or remain in the cell, making it swollen; small clear vacuoles may appear. Example: early injury to kidney tubule cells during reduced blood supply.

• 2. Fatty change (steatosis): fat droplets, mainly triglycerides, accumulate inside cells because fat processing or transport is disturbed. It commonly affects the liver. Examples: fatty liver associated with alcohol use, obesity or metabolic disease.

• Other cell changes: the cell may show membrane blebs, loss of microvilli, swelling of mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum, and clumping of nuclear chromatin. These changes may reverse when the cause is removed.

• Important terminology: hydropic change is another name for marked cellular swelling. Hyaline change describes a glassy, pink appearance caused by protein material, and mucoid change refers to mucin-rich material; these are not usually listed as the two main standard patterns of reversible cell injury. “Dysarrhythmia change” is not a standard pathology term and may be a misheard or misspelled term, so it should be checked against the class notes.

Q40. Explain hyaline change, mucoid change and the two types of mucin.

• Hyaline change: “Hyaline” describes a smooth, glassy, pink appearance under the microscope, usually due to protein accumulation. It is a descriptive appearance, not one single disease or a standard main category of reversible cell injury. Example: hyaline arteriolosclerosis in small blood vessels.

• Mucoid change: refers to accumulation or increased production of mucus or mucin-like ground substance. The exact meaning depends on the tissue and textbook context.

• Epithelial mucin: mucin produced by epithelial cells, such as goblet cells in the respiratory and intestinal tracts. It mixes with water to form mucus that lubricates and protects surfaces.

• Connective-tissue mucin: mucin-like, glycosaminoglycan-rich material in the extracellular matrix of connective tissue. Excess accumulation may be seen in certain disorders, such as myxoedema.

Q41. Explain irreversible cell injury, autolysis, necrosis and apoptosis.

• Irreversible cell injury: severe damage from which a cell cannot recover. Major features include inability to restore mitochondrial function, severe membrane damage and serious nuclear changes.

• Autolysis: breakdown of cells by their own digestive enzymes, especially after death when normal control and circulation have stopped. It contributes to postmortem tissue breakdown. Autolysis is not usually classified as a separate regulated form of cell death alongside necrosis and apoptosis.

• Necrosis: uncontrolled cell death caused by severe injury. Cells often swell, membranes rupture and cell contents leak out, usually causing inflammation in surrounding tissue. Example: coagulative necrosis in the heart after a myocardial infarction.

• Apoptosis: regulated, programmed cell death. The cell shrinks, its DNA is fragmented, and small membrane-bound bodies are removed by other cells; inflammation is usually minimal. Examples: removal of cells during embryonic development and removal of severely DNA-damaged cells.

Q42. Describe the five commonly taught morphological patterns of necrosis.

• 1. Coagulative necrosis: tissue architecture remains recognisable for a time because proteins are denatured. Commonly follows loss of blood supply in solid organs, except the brain. Example: myocardial infarction.

• 2. Liquefactive necrosis: enzymes digest dead tissue into a liquid or thick fluid. Example: an abscess caused by bacterial infection, or an infarct in the brain.

• 3. Caseous necrosis: dead tissue becomes soft, crumbly and cheese-like in appearance. Example: tuberculosis; it is often found within granulomas.

• 4. Fat necrosis: damaged fat cells release lipids that may combine with calcium to form chalky deposits (saponification). Example: acute pancreatitis causing injury to nearby fat.

• 5. Fibrinoid necrosis: immune-mediated or severe vascular injury causes bright pink, fibrin-like material to build up in vessel walls. Example: some forms of vasculitis and severe hypertension.

• Note: gangrenous necrosis is a clinical term often used for extensive tissue death, especially in a limb; dry gangrene is mainly coagulative, while wet gangrene includes infection and liquefaction. It is commonly discussed separately from the five patterns above.

Q43. Explain cell death in a tumour exposed to a chemotherapy agent.

• Many chemotherapy medicines damage DNA or interfere with DNA replication, cell division or other essential cell functions in rapidly dividing tumour cells.

• If the damage is too severe to repair, the cell may activate apoptosis through internal signalling pathways. The cell shrinks, its chromatin condenses, DNA fragments and apoptotic bodies form; these are cleared by other cells with little inflammation.

• Some chemotherapy agents can also cause other forms of cell death, including necrotic or regulated non-apoptotic pathways. The outcome depends on the drug, dose, tumour type and cell condition; not every chemotherapy-induced tumour-cell death is apoptosis.

• Example: a DNA-damaging drug may trigger DNA-damage sensors and the p53 pathway in a susceptible tumour cell, leading to activation of caspases and apoptosis.

UNIT II · 12 HOURS

Integumentary System, Skeletal System and Joints

Q1. What is the integumentary system?

The integumentary system consists mainly of the skin and its appendages, including hair, nails and skin glands. It protects the body, helps regulate temperature, provides sensation and contributes to vitamin D production.

Q2. Describe the layers of the skin.

The epidermis is the outer epithelial layer. The dermis beneath it contains connective tissue, blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles and glands. The hypodermis or subcutaneous tissue lies under the skin and contains connective tissue and variable amounts of fat; it is not usually counted as a layer of the skin proper.

Q3. State the functions of skin.

Skin provides a protective barrier, reduces water loss, helps regulate body temperature, detects touch and other sensations, contributes to immune defence and participates in vitamin D synthesis.

Q4. What is psoriasis?

Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease that can cause well-defined, red or darker plaques with scaling. It is not contagious. Severity and appearance vary, and assessment by a qualified clinician helps guide treatment.

Q5. What is dermatitis?

Dermatitis means inflammation of the skin. It can cause itching, redness, dryness, scaling or blisters. Causes include irritants, allergens and other skin conditions. Management depends on the cause and severity.

Q6. What is leprosy?

Leprosy, also called Hansen disease, is a chronic infection caused by Mycobacterium leprae or related bacteria. It mainly affects skin and peripheral nerves. Early diagnosis and multidrug treatment can cure the infection and help prevent disability. It is not spread by casual touch.

Q7. Describe the basic stages of wound healing.

Wound healing is commonly described as haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. Clotting helps stop bleeding; immune cells remove debris and microbes; new tissue and blood vessels form; and collagen is reorganized over time. The stages overlap and healing depends on wound type and the person's health.

Q8. Name the main divisions of the skeleton.

The axial skeleton includes the skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum. The appendicular skeleton includes the bones of the limbs and the shoulder and pelvic girdles.

Q9. Classify bones by shape.

Long bones include the femur; short bones include many wrist bones; flat bones include the sternum and some skull bones; irregular bones include vertebrae; and sesamoid bones include the patella. Pneumatic bones contain air-filled spaces, for example some skull bones.

Q10. State the functions of the skeletal system.

The skeleton supports the body, protects organs, provides attachment for muscles, acts as levers for movement, stores minerals such as calcium and phosphate, and contains marrow involved in blood-cell formation.

Q11. Differentiate between axial and appendicular skeleton.

The axial skeleton forms the central axis of the body and mainly supports and protects the head, neck and trunk. The appendicular skeleton includes the limbs and their girdles and is mainly involved in movement.

Q12. What are the main parts of a typical long bone?

A long bone has a shaft called the diaphysis, ends called epiphyses and transitional metaphyses. It contains compact and spongy bone, a medullary cavity in many long bones, periosteum on the outer surface except at articular areas, and endosteum lining internal surfaces.

Q13. What is skeletal muscle?

Skeletal muscle is striated muscle usually attached to bones by tendons. It produces voluntary movement, maintains posture, stabilizes joints and contributes to heat production.

Q14. Explain the basic mechanism of skeletal muscle contraction.

A nerve signal triggers release of acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction. The muscle action potential spreads along the membrane and into T-tubules, causing calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binds troponin, allowing actin and myosin to interact. ATP powers cross-bridge cycling. Calcium is then pumped back into storage, allowing relaxation.

Q15. What is the neuromuscular junction?

The neuromuscular junction is the synapse between a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle fibre. The motor neuron releases acetylcholine, which binds to receptors on the muscle membrane and initiates a muscle action potential.

Q16. Classify joints structurally.

Fibrous joints: bones are joined by fibrous connective tissue. Cartilaginous joints: bones are joined by cartilage. Synovial joints: have a joint cavity containing synovial fluid and usually permit substantial movement.

Q17. Classify joints functionally.

Synarthrosis: essentially immovable. Amphiarthrosis: slightly movable. Diarthrosis: freely movable; most synovial joints fall into this group.

Q18. What is rheumatoid arthritis?

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory disease that commonly affects joints symmetrically and can cause pain, swelling, stiffness and joint damage. It may also affect other organs. Early clinical assessment and appropriate treatment are important.

Q19. What is osteoporosis?

Osteoporosis is a condition in which bone strength is reduced, increasing the risk of fractures. It may develop without obvious symptoms until a fracture occurs. Age, hormonal changes, some medicines and other risk factors can contribute.

Q20. What is gout?

Gout is an inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of monosodium urate crystals in joints and other tissues, usually in the setting of elevated urate levels. It can cause sudden severe joint pain, redness and swelling. Diagnosis and treatment require clinical assessment.

UNIT III · 12 HOURS

Body Fluids, Blood, Lymphatic System, Inflammation and Haematological Diseases

Q1. What are body fluids?

Body fluids are water-based fluids containing dissolved substances. Total body water is divided into intracellular fluid inside cells and extracellular fluid outside cells, including interstitial fluid and plasma. They transport substances and help maintain temperature, pH and electrolyte balance.

Q2. State the major functions of blood.

Blood transports oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, hormones and waste products; helps regulate pH and temperature; protects against infection; and prevents blood loss through clotting.

Q3. What are the main components of blood?

Blood consists of plasma and formed elements. Plasma contains water, proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones and waste products. Formed elements include red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.

Q4. What is haemopoiesis?

Haemopoiesis is the production of blood cells. In adults, it occurs mainly in red bone marrow. It produces red blood cells, several types of white blood cells and platelets from blood-forming stem cells.

Q5. What is haemoglobin?

Haemoglobin is an iron-containing protein in red blood cells. It binds oxygen in the lungs and releases it to tissues; it also carries some carbon dioxide and contributes to acid–base buffering.

Q6. Explain blood coagulation in simple terms.

Coagulation is part of haemostasis. After vessel injury, platelets form an initial plug and coagulation factors activate in a sequence that generates thrombin. Thrombin converts fibrinogen into fibrin, which strengthens the clot. The clot is later remodelled and removed as healing progresses.

Q7. What are ABO blood groups?

ABO blood groups depend on A and B antigens on red blood cells. Group A has A antigen; group B has B antigen; group AB has both; and group O has neither A nor B. Plasma antibodies and compatibility must be considered when transfusing blood.

Q8. What is the Rh factor?

The Rh blood group system includes several antigens; the D antigen is commonly used to describe someone as Rh-positive or Rh-negative. Rh compatibility matters in transfusion and pregnancy because antibodies may develop after exposure to incompatible red blood cells.

Q9. What is blood transfusion?

Blood transfusion is the administration of blood or a blood component to a patient. It requires appropriate clinical indication, patient and component identification, compatibility testing, consent where applicable, monitoring and procedures to manage reactions.

Q10. What is the lymphatic system?

The lymphatic system includes lymph, lymphatic vessels, lymph nodes and lymphoid organs such as the spleen and thymus. It returns excess tissue fluid to the blood, transports absorbed dietary fats from the intestine and supports immune defence.

Q11. How is lymph formed and circulated?

Some fluid filters out of blood capillaries into tissues. Much returns to the blood capillaries, while excess enters lymphatic capillaries and is called lymph. Lymph travels through vessels and nodes and eventually returns to the venous circulation. Muscle movement, breathing and valves help its flow.

Q12. Define inflammation.

Inflammation is a protective response of vascularized tissues to injury or infection. It helps remove the cause of damage, clear dead cells and start repair. Excessive or persistent inflammation can also damage tissue.

Q13. Name the classic signs of inflammation.

The classic signs are redness, heat, swelling, pain and reduced function. They arise from changes in blood flow, increased vascular permeability and the actions of inflammatory mediators.

Q14. Differentiate between acute and chronic inflammation.

Acute inflammation usually starts rapidly and is characterized by fluid movement and recruitment of white blood cells, especially neutrophils. Chronic inflammation is longer-lasting and often involves macrophages and lymphocytes, tissue destruction and attempts at repair.

Q15. Name important mediators of inflammation.

Examples include histamine, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, cytokines, chemokines, complement components and bradykinin. These mediators influence blood vessels, immune-cell movement, pain, fever and other parts of the inflammatory response.

Q16. What is tissue repair?

Tissue repair restores tissue after injury through regeneration of surviving cells, formation of connective tissue scar, or a combination of both. The outcome depends on tissue type, injury severity, blood supply, infection and other factors.

Q17. What is iron-deficiency anaemia?

Iron-deficiency anaemia occurs when insufficient iron limits haemoglobin production. It may cause tiredness, weakness, pallor or breathlessness. Causes can include inadequate intake, poor absorption or blood loss; the underlying cause should be assessed rather than assuming diet is the only reason.

Q18. What is megaloblastic anaemia?

Megaloblastic anaemia is a type of anaemia caused by impaired DNA synthesis, often due to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. Red-cell precursors become abnormally large. The cause matters because vitamin B12 deficiency can also cause neurological problems.

Q19. What is sickle cell anaemia?

Sickle cell anaemia is an inherited disorder involving haemoglobin S. Under certain conditions, red blood cells can become sickle-shaped, break down early and obstruct small blood vessels, causing anaemia and episodes of pain or organ injury.

Q20. What is thalassaemia?

Thalassaemia is a group of inherited disorders in which production of alpha or beta globin chains is reduced. It can cause anaemia of varying severity. Diagnosis and management depend on the type and severity.

Q21. What is hereditary spherocytosis?

Hereditary spherocytosis is an inherited red-cell membrane disorder that can make red blood cells sphere-shaped and more likely to be destroyed in the spleen. It can cause haemolytic anaemia, jaundice and an enlarged spleen in some patients.

Q22. What is haemophilia?

Haemophilia is an inherited bleeding disorder caused by deficiency or dysfunction of a clotting factor, most commonly factor VIII (haemophilia A) or factor IX (haemophilia B). It can cause prolonged bleeding and bleeding into joints or muscles.

Q23. Differentiate between anaemia and haemophilia.

Anaemia is a reduced ability of blood to carry oxygen, often due to low haemoglobin or red-cell mass. Haemophilia is a clotting disorder that causes a tendency to bleed. They affect different aspects of blood function.

UNIT IV · 12 HOURS

Peripheral Nervous System and Special Senses

Q1. What is the peripheral nervous system?

The peripheral nervous system (PNS) includes nerves and ganglia outside the brain and spinal cord. It carries sensory information to the central nervous system and sends motor commands to muscles and glands.

Q2. Classify the peripheral nervous system functionally.

The sensory or afferent division carries information toward the central nervous system. The motor or efferent division carries commands away from it. The motor division includes the somatic nervous system and the autonomic nervous system.

Q3. Differentiate between sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems.

The sympathetic division supports responses to challenge or activity, such as increasing heart rate and dilating pupils. The parasympathetic division supports rest-and-digest functions, such as slowing heart rate and promoting digestive activity. Their effects depend on the organ and receptor.

Q4. What are spinal nerves?

Spinal nerves arise from the spinal cord through dorsal sensory roots and ventral motor roots. Humans usually have 31 pairs: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal pair. Most are mixed nerves carrying both sensory and motor fibres.

Q5. What are cranial nerves?

Cranial nerves are 12 pairs of nerves associated with the brain. They carry sensory, motor or mixed information for functions involving the head and neck, and some also serve internal organs. Examples include the optic nerve for vision and the vagus nerve for several parasympathetic functions.

Q6. Describe the main parts of the eye.

The cornea and lens focus light. The iris controls pupil size. The aqueous humour and vitreous body help maintain the eye's shape and optical properties. The retina contains photoreceptors that convert light into neural signals, which travel through the optic nerve.

Q7. What is myopia?

Myopia, or short-sightedness, is a refractive error in which distant objects appear blurred because the eye focuses their images in front of the retina under relaxed accommodation. It is commonly corrected with appropriate concave lenses or other prescribed methods.

Q8. What is cataract?

A cataract is clouding of the eye's lens that can cause blurred vision, glare and reduced contrast. It commonly develops with age but may have other causes. Assessment by an eye-care professional determines appropriate management.

Q9. What is glaucoma?

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases that damage the optic nerve, often associated with raised intraocular pressure but not always. It may cause gradual vision loss. Early detection and treatment can help prevent progression.

Q10. What are the main parts of the ear?

The outer ear collects sound; the middle ear contains the tympanic membrane and ossicles that transmit vibrations; and the inner ear contains the cochlea for hearing and vestibular structures for balance.

Q11. What is otitis externa?

Otitis externa is inflammation or infection of the external ear canal. It can cause ear pain, itching, discharge and tenderness, especially when the outer ear is moved. Evaluation helps distinguish it from middle-ear disease.

Q12. What is otitis media?

Otitis media is inflammation or infection of the middle ear behind the eardrum. Symptoms may include ear pain, fever or reduced hearing. Diagnosis and treatment depend on age, severity and clinical findings.

Q13. What is vertigo?

Vertigo is the false sensation that a person or their surroundings are moving or spinning. It can arise from inner-ear or neurological conditions. New severe vertigo, especially with weakness, difficulty speaking, severe headache or other neurological signs, requires urgent medical assessment.

Q14. What is the function of the nose?

The nose filters, warms and humidifies inhaled air and contains receptors for smell. The olfactory system detects odours and sends signals to the brain.

Q15. What is anosmia?

Anosmia is loss of the sense of smell. Causes may include nasal obstruction, viral infections, head injury or neurological conditions. Persistent or sudden unexplained loss should be assessed according to clinical guidance.

Q16. What is the function of the tongue?

The tongue helps taste, move food during chewing, form a bolus, assist swallowing and produce speech sounds. Taste receptors detect basic taste qualities, while smell also contributes strongly to flavour perception.

UNIT V · 12 HOURS

Cardiovascular System

Q1. What are the main functions of the cardiovascular system?

The cardiovascular system circulates blood to deliver oxygen, nutrients and hormones, remove carbon dioxide and waste, transport immune cells and help regulate temperature, pH and fluid balance.

Q2. Describe the location and chambers of the heart.

The heart lies in the thoracic cavity between the lungs, behind the sternum and slightly left of the midline. It has four chambers: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium and left ventricle. Valves help keep blood moving in one direction.

Q3. Explain the pathway of blood through the heart.

Blood returning from the body enters the right atrium through the venae cavae, passes through the tricuspid valve to the right ventricle and is pumped through the pulmonary valve to the lungs. Oxygenated blood returns to the left atrium through pulmonary veins, passes through the mitral valve to the left ventricle and leaves through the aortic valve into the aorta and body.

Q4. Differentiate between arteries, veins and capillaries.

Arteries carry blood away from the heart and generally have thicker, more elastic walls. Veins carry blood toward the heart and often contain valves. Capillaries have very thin walls, usually one endothelial cell layer, allowing exchange of gases, nutrients and waste. Pulmonary vessels are important exceptions to the usual oxygen-content pattern.

Q5. What is coronary circulation?

Coronary circulation supplies the heart muscle itself. The coronary arteries branch from the aorta and deliver oxygenated blood to the myocardium; cardiac veins drain blood into the coronary sinus and then the right atrium.

Q6. What is the cardiac conduction system?

The main components are the sinoatrial (SA) node, atrioventricular (AV) node, atrioventricular bundle (bundle of His), right and left bundle branches, and Purkinje fibres. This system coordinates the electrical activation and contraction of the heart.

Q7. How is heartbeat regulated by the autonomic nervous system?

Sympathetic activity generally increases heart rate, conduction and contractility. Parasympathetic activity, mainly through the vagus nerve, generally slows the SA node and AV conduction. Regulation also involves hormones, reflexes and the heart's own pacemaker activity.

Q8. Define cardiac cycle.

The cardiac cycle is the sequence of events in one heartbeat, including atrial systole, ventricular systole and relaxation (diastole). These phases coordinate filling and ejection of blood through the chambers and valves.

Q9. Define cardiac output and give its formula.

Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute. Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume. For example, 70 beats/min × 70 mL/beat = 4,900 mL/min, or about 4.9 L/min.

Q10. What is blood pressure?

Blood pressure is the force exerted by circulating blood against arterial walls. Systolic pressure is the higher pressure during ventricular contraction; diastolic pressure is the lower pressure during ventricular relaxation. Interpretation depends on proper measurement and clinical context.

Q11. What is pulse?

Pulse is the pressure wave felt in an artery as the heart ejects blood. It can be assessed for rate, rhythm and strength. Pulse rate often approximates heart rate, but they may differ in some arrhythmias.

Q12. What is an electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG)?

An ECG records electrical potential changes associated with heart activity at the body surface. The P wave usually represents atrial depolarization, the QRS complex ventricular depolarization, and the T wave ventricular repolarization. An ECG must be interpreted with symptoms and other clinical information.

Q13. What is hypertension?

Hypertension is persistently elevated arterial blood pressure. It often has no obvious symptoms but increases the risk of stroke, heart disease, kidney disease and other complications. Diagnosis requires appropriate repeated measurements and clinical assessment.

Q14. What are cardiac arrhythmias?

Cardiac arrhythmias are abnormalities in heart rate, rhythm or electrical conduction. They may be too fast, too slow or irregular. Symptoms can include palpitations, dizziness, fainting or breathlessness, although some arrhythmias cause no symptoms.

Q15. What is congestive heart failure?

Heart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot fill or pump adequately to meet the body's needs without elevated filling pressures. It can cause breathlessness, fatigue and fluid accumulation, such as swelling in the legs or fluid in the lungs.

Q16. What is ischaemic heart disease?

Ischaemic heart disease occurs when blood supply to the heart muscle is insufficient, often because coronary arteries are narrowed by atherosclerosis. It can cause angina or myocardial infarction.

Q17. Differentiate between angina and myocardial infarction.

Angina is chest discomfort caused by temporary myocardial ischaemia without death of heart muscle. Myocardial infarction occurs when prolonged ischaemia causes death of heart muscle cells. New, severe or persistent chest pain is an emergency and needs immediate medical help.

Q18. What is atherosclerosis?

Atherosclerosis is a disease in which plaques containing lipids, inflammatory cells and fibrous tissue build up in the inner walls of arteries. Plaques can narrow arteries or rupture and trigger clot formation, leading to events such as heart attack or stroke.

Q19. What is coronary artery disease?

Coronary artery disease is disease of the arteries that supply the heart muscle, commonly due to atherosclerotic plaque. It can reduce blood flow and lead to angina, heart attack, heart failure or arrhythmias.

Q20. Name major modifiable risk factors for cardiovascular disease.

Examples include tobacco use, high blood pressure, unhealthy blood lipid levels, diabetes, physical inactivity, an unhealthy diet and harmful alcohol use. Risk is also affected by age, family history and other factors. A healthcare professional can help assess individual risk.
