UNIT I · 9 HOURS

Introduction to the Profession of Pharmacy

Topics in this unit

History and development of pharmacy in India; pharmacy education, industries and organizations; roles of pharmacists; pharmacopoeias; and prescription writing and handling.

Q1. What is pharmacy?

Pharmacy is the health science concerned with the preparation, quality, storage, supply and proper use of medicines. It connects pharmaceutical science with patient care.

Q2. Give a brief account of the development of pharmacy in India.

Traditional systems such as Ayurveda used medicinal substances for a long time. Modern pharmacy developed with the growth of organized drug manufacturing, formal pharmacy education, drug standards and professional regulation. Pharmacy education and pharmaceutical industries expanded after independence. The Pharmacy Act, 1948 provided for regulation of the profession and registration of pharmacists. The Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and its rules regulate important aspects of drugs and cosmetics in India.

Q3. What is the importance of pharmacy education?

Pharmacy education teaches students about medicines, their preparation, quality, effects, safe use and patient counselling. It prepares trained people for community, hospital, industry, research, quality control and regulatory work.

Q4. What are the main areas of work for a pharmacist?

A pharmacist may work in: (1) retail or community pharmacy, supplying medicines and counselling patients; (2) hospital pharmacy, managing medicines for hospital services; (3) clinical pharmacy, supporting safe and effective medicine use; (4) pharmaceutical industry, manufacturing and quality assurance; (5) research and development; and (6) regulatory affairs, education and public health.

Q5. State the responsibilities of a community pharmacist.

The community pharmacist checks prescriptions, supplies the correct medicine, explains dose and timing, advises about storage and possible precautions, checks for obvious prescription problems, maintains required records and refers a patient to a prescriber when needed. A pharmacist should not change prescribed treatment without proper authorization.

Q6. What is hospital pharmacy?

Hospital pharmacy is the branch of pharmacy that manages the selection, purchase, storage, preparation and distribution of medicines in a hospital. It also supports medication safety, inventory control and communication with healthcare professionals.

Q7. What is clinical pharmacy?

Clinical pharmacy focuses on optimizing medicine use for individual patients. Clinical pharmacists review medication regimens, identify possible medicine-related problems, provide medicine information and work with the healthcare team to support safe treatment.

Q8. What is a pharmacopoeia?

A pharmacopoeia is an official book or collection of standards for medicines and pharmaceutical substances. It may specify identity, purity, strength, tests, assay, storage and other quality requirements.

Q9. Explain IP, BP, USP and Ph. Eur.

IP: Indian Pharmacopoeia, the official compendium of drug standards in India. BP: British Pharmacopoeia. USP: United States Pharmacopeia. Ph. Eur.: European Pharmacopoeia. These publications set recognized quality standards within their respective jurisdictions or systems.

Q10. What are the main contents of the Indian Pharmacopoeia?

It contains general notices and requirements, general analytical methods, standards or monographs for drug substances and preparations, reagents and other relevant quality information. The exact organization depends on the edition.

Q11. What is a pharmacopoeial monograph?

A monograph is an official standard for a particular substance or preparation. It commonly gives the name, definition, identification tests, purity tests, assay, storage conditions and other specifications.

Q12. What is a prescription?

A prescription is a direction from an authorized prescriber to a pharmacist for preparing or supplying medicine for a patient. It also communicates how the medicine is to be used.

Q13. List the common parts of a prescription.

Common parts include: (1) date; (2) patient details; (3) superscription, traditionally marked by ℞; (4) inscription, listing the medicine and strength; (5) subscription, giving instructions to the dispenser when relevant; (6) signa or directions for the patient; and (7) prescriber details, signature and registration information. The exact format can vary by law and practice.

Q14. How should a pharmacist handle a prescription?

Check that it is legally valid and readable; verify the patient, medicine, strength, dose, route, frequency and duration; check for possible errors or interactions within available information; contact the prescriber if clarification is needed; dispense and label correctly; counsel the patient; and maintain required records. Never guess an unclear dose.

Q15. Give examples of Latin prescription terms.

Traditional terms include p.o. (by mouth), b.i.d. (twice daily), t.i.d. (three times daily), q.i.d. (four times daily), p.r.n. (when required), and stat (immediately). Abbreviations can be misunderstood, so clear plain-language directions are preferred where possible.

Q16. Differentiate between a pharmacopoeia and a formulary.

A pharmacopoeia mainly sets official quality standards for substances and preparations. A formulary generally provides information and recommendations about medicines selected for use in a healthcare system, institution or practice.

UNIT II · 9 HOURS

Introduction to Pharmaceutical Calculations and Dosage Forms

Topics in this unit

Metric weights and measures; alligation; proof spirit; isotonic and dilute solutions; ratio and percentage strength; scientific notation; posology; routes and dosage forms; active pharmaceutical ingredients and excipients.

Q1. What is the metric system?

The metric system is a decimal system of measurement. Common units include kilogram (kg), gram (g), milligram (mg), litre (L) and millilitre (mL). For example, 1 g = 1,000 mg and 1 L = 1,000 mL.

Q2. What is alligation?

Alligation is a calculation method used to find the proportions in which ingredients of different strengths or concentrations must be mixed to obtain a desired strength. The units and basis of the strengths must be the same before applying the method.

Q3. Explain alligation with an example.

To prepare a 10% mixture from 5% and 20% ingredients, use the differences: 20 − 10 = 10 parts of the 5% ingredient, and 10 − 5 = 5 parts of the 20% ingredient. The ratio is therefore 10:5, or 2:1. This gives an average concentration of 10%.

Q4. What is proof spirit?

Proof spirit is a historical way of expressing alcohol strength. The exact definition differs by country. Under the British system, proof spirit is approximately 57.1% alcohol by volume at the reference temperature. Always state which system is being used in calculations.

Q5. Define percentage strength and give its types.

Percentage strength expresses the amount of ingredient in 100 parts of preparation. % w/w means grams per 100 g; % w/v means grams per 100 mL of final preparation; % v/v means millilitres per 100 mL of final preparation.

Q6. How much solute is needed to make 200 mL of a 5% w/v solution?

A 5% w/v solution contains 5 g per 100 mL. For 200 mL: (5 g ÷ 100 mL) × 200 mL = 10 g. Dissolve 10 g and add vehicle to make the final volume 200 mL.

Q7. What is ratio strength?

Ratio strength expresses the amount of ingredient as one part in a stated number of parts of final preparation, such as 1:1,000. For a w/v ratio, 1:1,000 commonly means 1 g in 1,000 mL. The basis—w/w, w/v or v/v—must be specified when needed.

Q8. Convert 1:1,000 w/v into percentage strength.

1:1,000 w/v means 1 g in 1,000 mL. In 100 mL, the amount is 0.1 g. Therefore, the strength is 0.1% w/v.

Q9. What is scientific notation?

Scientific notation writes a number as a value between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of 10. For example, 0.00025 = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ and 45,000 = 4.5 × 10⁴. It makes very large and very small values easier to read.

Q10. What is an isotonic solution?

An isotonic solution has approximately the same effective osmotic pressure as the body fluid with which it is compared. Tonicity is important for preparations such as injections and eye drops because unsuitable tonicity may cause irritation or damage. Formulation must follow the applicable standards.

Q11. What is dilution?

Dilution decreases concentration by adding more solvent or vehicle. When the amount of solute remains constant, the relationship is C₁V₁ = C₂V₂, where C is concentration and V is volume, provided compatible concentration units and the appropriate volume basis are used.

Q12. Example: How much of a 10% solution is needed to make 100 mL of a 2% solution?

Use C₁V₁ = C₂V₂. V₁ = (2 × 100) ÷ 10 = 20 mL. Measure 20 mL of the 10% solution and add suitable vehicle to make a final volume of 100 mL.

Q13. What is posology?

Posology is the branch of pharmacy concerned with the study and determination of medicine doses. Dose selection depends on the medicine, indication, age, body weight, body surface area, organ function and other patient-specific factors.

Q14. Name factors that affect dose.

Factors may include age, body weight, body surface area, route of administration, severity of disease, kidney and liver function, tolerance, interactions, dosage form and the medicine's safety profile.

Q15. What are Clark's and Young's rules?

They are historical formulae used in teaching to estimate a child's dose from an adult dose using body weight (Clark's rule) or age (Young's rule). They are not reliable substitutes for current, medicine-specific pediatric dosing guidance. In practice, use an authoritative dosing reference and a qualified prescriber's directions.

Q16. What is body surface area (BSA) dosing?

BSA dosing expresses a dose per square metre of body surface area. A common formula for estimated BSA is the Mosteller formula: BSA (m²) = √[height (cm) × weight (kg) ÷ 3600]. It is used only when appropriate for the medicine and clinical situation.

Q17. What is a dosage form? Give examples.

A dosage form is the physical form in which a medicine is prepared for administration. Examples include tablets, capsules, powders, solutions, syrups, suspensions, emulsions, ointments, creams, gels and suppositories.

Q18. List common routes of administration.

Oral, sublingual, buccal, topical, transdermal, inhalational, rectal, vaginal, ophthalmic, otic, nasal and parenteral routes such as intravenous, intramuscular and subcutaneous administration.

Q19. Differentiate between an active pharmaceutical ingredient and an excipient.

An active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) is the substance responsible for the intended pharmacological action. An excipient is an ingredient added to help manufacture, stabilize, preserve, deliver or improve the acceptability of a preparation. Excipients are selected for their intended function and safety.

Q20. What are the ideal characteristics of an excipient?

An excipient should be suitable for its intended use, compatible with the API and other ingredients, sufficiently stable, of appropriate quality, and safe at the intended exposure. It should support the required performance of the dosage form.

UNIT III · 9 HOURS

Solid Dosage Forms

Topics in this unit

Powders and their types; tablets and pills; capsules and capsule sizes; advantages, disadvantages, excipients and general methods of preparation.

Q1. What are powders in pharmacy?

Pharmaceutical powders are dry, finely divided mixtures of one or more substances intended for internal or external use. They may be supplied as bulk powders or divided into individual doses.

Q2. Classify pharmaceutical powders.

Powders may be classified as bulk powders and divided powders; powders for internal or external use; and special types such as dusting powders, effervescent powders, efflorescent powders, hygroscopic powders and eutectic mixtures.

Q3. What are dusting powders?

Dusting powders are finely divided powders intended to be applied to the skin. They should be appropriately fine, free from irritating particles and manufactured with suitable microbiological quality. Powders for open wounds or body cavities require special controls.

Q4. What are effervescent powders?

Effervescent powders contain substances such as an acid and a carbonate or bicarbonate that react in water to release carbon dioxide. They are generally dissolved or dispersed in water before use. Moisture protection is important during storage.

Q5. What are efflorescent powders?

Efflorescent substances contain water of crystallization and may lose some of it when exposed to air, becoming damp or changing form. This can affect powder flow and stability. Suitable formulation and packaging help manage the problem.

Q6. What are hygroscopic powders?

Hygroscopic powders absorb moisture from the surrounding air. They may become damp, sticky or difficult to handle. They need suitable packaging, storage conditions and, where appropriate, a compatible absorbent.

Q7. What is a eutectic mixture?

A eutectic mixture is a mixture of substances that, at a particular composition, melts at a lower temperature than either pure component. Some powders may liquefy when ingredients are mixed. Separate mixing followed by use of a suitable inert absorbent may help, if compatible with the formulation.

Q8. State advantages and disadvantages of powders.

Advantages: flexible dosing in suitable cases, often relatively easy to prepare, and generally faster dissolution than some intact solid forms. Disadvantages: unpleasant taste may be difficult to mask; moisture-sensitive ingredients may be unstable; dose measurement can be inaccurate with bulk powders; and they are unsuitable for some drugs or patients.

Q9. What are the general steps in powder preparation?

Check ingredient identity and quantities, weigh accurately, reduce particle size when needed, sieve if appropriate, mix to uniformity using a suitable method, divide into doses if required, package in a compatible container and label with directions and storage requirements.

Q10. Define a tablet.

A tablet is a solid unit dosage form containing one or more active ingredients, usually prepared by compression and sometimes by moulding. It may contain excipients to support manufacturing, stability, disintegration, dissolution and appearance.

Q11. Name common types of tablets.

Examples include uncoated tablets, film-coated tablets, sugar-coated tablets, enteric-coated tablets, chewable tablets, dispersible tablets, effervescent tablets, orally disintegrating tablets, sublingual tablets, buccal tablets and modified-release tablets.

Q12. What are the advantages and disadvantages of tablets?

Advantages: convenient, portable, accurate unit doses, good stability for many medicines and economical large-scale production. Disadvantages: some patients have difficulty swallowing; some drugs are hard to compress or dissolve; onset may be slower than certain liquid or parenteral forms; and not every tablet may be crushed or split safely.

Q13. Name common tablet excipients and their functions.

Diluents add bulk; binders help particles stick together; disintegrants help tablets break apart; lubricants reduce friction during compression and ejection; glidants improve powder flow; and coatings may protect the tablet, mask taste or modify release. One excipient can sometimes perform more than one function.

Q14. Describe the general methods of tablet preparation.

Direct compression: suitable powders or granules are blended and compressed directly. Wet granulation: a liquid binder is used to form granules, which are dried, blended and compressed. Dry granulation: powders are compacted without a granulating liquid, then milled into granules and compressed. The method depends on material properties and product requirements.

Q15. What is a pill?

A pill is a traditional solid oral dosage form made as a small rounded mass. Modern standardized tablets and capsules have largely replaced pills in routine pharmaceutical practice.

Q16. What is a capsule?

A capsule is a dosage form in which medicine is enclosed in a shell, commonly made from gelatin or another suitable material. Capsules may contain powders, granules, pellets or, in suitable designs, liquids or semisolids.

Q17. Differentiate between hard and soft capsules.

Hard capsules commonly have two pieces—a body and a cap—and are often filled with powders or granules. Soft capsules have a one-piece flexible shell and are commonly used for compatible liquids or semisolids. The shell material and fill must be compatible.

Q18. What are capsule sizes?

Capsule sizes are standardized by numbers. For common hard capsules, size 000 is among the largest and size 5 among the smallest. The actual fill mass depends on capsule volume, material density and formulation; size alone does not determine the dose.

Q19. State advantages and disadvantages of capsules.

Advantages: can mask unpleasant taste, are convenient, provide unit doses and may be easier to swallow than some tablets. Disadvantages: shells can be sensitive to unsuitable humidity; some fills and ingredients are incompatible; capsules may cost more to manufacture; and certain capsules must not be opened or altered.

Q20. Name common capsule excipients.

Depending on the product, the fill may contain diluents, lubricants, glidants, wetting agents, stabilizers or other functional excipients. Shells may contain gelatin or another polymer, water, colourants and approved opacifiers. Only compatible, suitable-grade ingredients should be used.

UNIT IV · 9 HOURS

Monophasic and Biphasic Liquids

Topics in this unit

Preparations for internal use; aromatic waters, syrups, elixirs and linctuses; preparations for external use and body cavities; official preparations; suspensions and emulsions.

Q1. What is a liquid dosage form?

A liquid dosage form is a pharmaceutical preparation in which the medicine is dissolved, dispersed or emulsified in a liquid vehicle. Liquids can be useful for patients who have difficulty swallowing solid dosage forms.

Q2. Differentiate between monophasic and biphasic liquids.

A monophasic liquid is a single, uniform phase, such as a true solution. A biphasic liquid contains two phases, such as solid particles dispersed in a liquid (suspension) or droplets of one liquid dispersed in another (emulsion).

Q3. What is an aromatic water?

An aromatic water is a clear, saturated aqueous preparation containing volatile aromatic substances. Depending on the formulation, it may be used as a flavouring vehicle or for another specified pharmaceutical purpose. It should be prepared and stored according to the applicable formula and standard.

Q4. What is a syrup?

A syrup is a concentrated aqueous preparation containing sugar or a suitable sugar substitute, with or without medicinal ingredients and flavouring agents. Sweetness and viscosity can improve acceptability, but high sugar content is not suitable for every patient or product.

Q5. Describe general methods of syrup preparation.

Common approaches include dissolving ingredients with heat when appropriate, dissolving ingredients without heat, adding a prepared medicated or flavoured liquid to syrup base, or percolation where specified. The chosen method must protect heat-sensitive ingredients and produce the required final volume and quality.

Q6. What is an elixir?

An elixir is a clear, sweetened, flavoured hydroalcoholic oral liquid. Alcohol can help dissolve certain ingredients, so compatibility and precipitation must be considered when an elixir is diluted or mixed.

Q7. What is a linctus?

A linctus is a viscous oral liquid, traditionally used for symptomatic relief of cough. It is generally taken in small amounts and swallowed slowly to coat the throat. Directions depend on the specific product.

Q8. Name liquid preparations used externally or in body cavities.

Examples in the syllabus include liniments, lotions, throat paints, applications, gargles, mouthwashes, enemas, eye drops, ear drops, nasal drops and tinctures. Their composition, sterility requirements, route and labelling differ. For example, ophthalmic preparations require suitable quality and sterility controls.

Q9. Differentiate between a liniment and a lotion.

Liniments are generally applied to the skin with rubbing, where appropriate. Lotions are liquid preparations applied externally without vigorous rubbing. Neither should be used on damaged skin or for other purposes unless the product directions permit it.

Q10. What is a gargle? How is it used?

A gargle is a liquid preparation used in the throat or mouth. It is used according to the label, and should not be swallowed unless the product is specifically intended for swallowing.

Q11. What is a mouthwash?

A mouthwash is a liquid used to rinse the mouth for a specified purpose, such as cleansing or delivering an ingredient. The user should follow the product directions, including whether it must be spat out.

Q12. What are official preparations?

Official preparations are preparations described in an official pharmacopoeia or other recognized official compendium. Their identity, ingredients, strength, method of preparation and quality requirements should comply with the applicable monograph or standard.

Q13. Define a suspension.

A suspension is a biphasic liquid dosage form in which insoluble solid particles are dispersed in a liquid vehicle. The particles may settle on standing, so the label commonly directs the user to shake the container before measuring a dose.

Q14. What are flocculated and deflocculated suspensions?

In a flocculated suspension, particles form loose aggregates called flocs and usually settle relatively quickly, often forming a loose sediment that is easier to redisperse. In a deflocculated suspension, particles remain more separate, may settle more slowly, but can form a compact cake that is difficult to redisperse.

Q15. State the advantages and disadvantages of suspensions.

Advantages: can deliver poorly soluble drugs in liquid form, may be easier to swallow and can allow flexible dosing when properly formulated. Disadvantages: sedimentation may occur; uniform dosing requires shaking; physical stability and microbial quality need control; and the preparation may be less convenient to transport than a solid form.

Q16. What ingredients may be used in a suspension?

Depending on the formula, ingredients may include the active substance, vehicle, wetting agent, suspending agent, flocculating agent, buffer, preservative, sweetener and flavour. Each ingredient must be compatible and suitable for the intended route.

Q17. Describe the general preparation of a suspension.

Reduce the drug to a suitable particle size, wet the powder if needed, prepare the vehicle and excipients, disperse the particles uniformly, bring to final volume, mix well, and fill into a suitable container. Evaluate sedimentation, redispersibility, dose uniformity and other required quality attributes.

Q18. Define an emulsion.

An emulsion is a biphasic system in which one immiscible liquid is dispersed as droplets in another liquid. An emulsifying agent helps form and stabilize the dispersion.

Q19. What are the main types of emulsions?

Oil-in-water (O/W): oil droplets are dispersed in water. Water-in-oil (W/O): water droplets are dispersed in oil. The type affects feel, washability, use and the choice of emulsifier.

Q20. Name common emulsifying agents.

Examples include suitable natural gums, certain proteins, non-ionic surfactants, anionic surfactants and finely divided solids such as some clays. Selection depends on the emulsion type, route, pH, compatibility and product requirements.

Q21. How can the type of emulsion be identified?

Common teaching tests include dilution, dye solubility and electrical conductivity. An O/W emulsion can generally be diluted with water and often conducts electricity better; a W/O emulsion is more readily diluted with oil. A water-soluble dye colours the continuous phase. Interpret tests together and follow the prescribed laboratory method.

Q22. Describe the general preparation of an emulsion.

Select compatible oil, water and emulsifier; use an appropriate emulsification method; combine phases under suitable mixing conditions; add heat-sensitive or volatile ingredients at the correct stage; adjust to final quantity; and evaluate appearance, droplet distribution and physical stability. The specific method depends on the formula.

Q23. Differentiate between a suspension and an emulsion.

A suspension disperses solid particles in a liquid. An emulsion disperses droplets of one liquid in another immiscible liquid. Both are biphasic systems and require attention to physical stability, but their formulation needs are different.

UNIT V · 9 HOURS

Semisolid Dosage Forms

Topics in this unit

Definitions and classification; advantages and disadvantages; ointment bases and excipients; preparation of ointments, pastes, creams and gels; suppositories and pessaries; ideal bases, displacement value and preparation methods.

Q1. What are semisolid dosage forms?

Semisolid dosage forms are preparations with a consistency between liquids and solids. They are commonly applied to the skin or certain mucosal surfaces. Examples include ointments, creams, pastes and gels.

Q2. Classify semisolid dosage forms.

Common classes include ointments, creams, pastes and gels. The choice depends on the intended site, drug properties, desired release, skin condition and product requirements.

Q3. State advantages and disadvantages of semisolid preparations.

Advantages: can deliver medicine locally, protect or soften the skin, and allow application to a selected area. Disadvantages: may be greasy or messy, can be difficult to measure precisely, may be affected by temperature or storage, and may cause irritation in some users.

Q4. What is an ointment?

An ointment is a semisolid preparation intended for application to the skin or certain mucosal surfaces. It may be medicated or non-medicated and is formulated with a suitable base.

Q5. Classify ointment bases.

Common classes are: (1) hydrocarbon bases, which are greasy and occlusive; (2) absorption bases, which can incorporate some aqueous solution; (3) water-removable bases, often oil-in-water emulsion bases; and (4) water-soluble bases, which contain water-soluble materials and no oleaginous base.

Q6. What are the properties of an ideal ointment base?

An ideal base should be non-irritating, stable, compatible with the drug, easy to apply and remove when required, have suitable consistency, release the drug appropriately and meet the needs of the intended site of use.

Q7. Describe the general methods of ointment preparation.

Incorporation: mix ingredients into the base using suitable spatulation or levigation. Fusion: melt compatible base ingredients, mix them uniformly and cool while stirring. The chosen method depends on heat sensitivity, solubility, particle size and the properties of the base.

Q8. What is levigation?

Levigation is the process of reducing grittiness by triturating a powder with a small amount of a suitable liquid or base to form a smooth paste before incorporation into the main base. The levigating agent must be compatible with the formulation.

Q9. What is a cream?

A cream is a semisolid emulsion intended for application to the skin or certain mucosal surfaces. Creams may be oil-in-water or water-in-oil, giving them different feel, washability and performance.

Q10. What is a paste?

A paste is a semisolid preparation containing a relatively high proportion of finely dispersed solid material. It is usually stiffer than an ointment and may provide a protective layer on the skin.

Q11. What is a gel?

A gel is a semisolid system in which a liquid is held within a three-dimensional network formed by a suitable gelling agent. Gels may be aqueous or non-aqueous and are selected according to the intended application and drug compatibility.

Q12. Name common excipients used in semisolid dosage forms.

Depending on the product, these may include bases or vehicles, emulsifying agents, gelling agents, humectants, preservatives, antioxidants, buffers, stabilizers and suitable penetration or viscosity modifiers. Their selection must account for compatibility and safety.

Q13. What are suppositories?

Suppositories are solid or semisolid unit dosage forms designed for insertion into a body cavity, where they melt, soften or dissolve to release the medicine. Rectal suppositories are commonly shaped for rectal insertion.

Q14. What are pessaries?

Pessaries are dosage forms intended for insertion into the vagina. They may be designed to melt, soften or dissolve after administration and release the active ingredient locally or, for suitable products, for absorption.

Q15. Name types of suppository bases.

Examples include fatty bases such as hard fat and water-soluble or water-miscible bases such as polyethylene glycol mixtures and glycerinated gelatin for suitable uses. Base selection depends on melting or dissolution behaviour, drug compatibility, release and intended site.

Q16. What are the properties of an ideal suppository base?

An ideal base should be non-toxic and non-irritating, compatible with the drug, stable during storage, easy to mould, release the drug appropriately and melt, soften or dissolve as intended at body temperature or in body fluids.

Q17. State advantages and disadvantages of suppositories.

Advantages: useful when oral administration is not possible, can provide local action and may avoid some gastrointestinal exposure. Disadvantages: acceptance may be poor, absorption can be variable, storage may be temperature-sensitive and administration can be inconvenient.

Q18. What is displacement value?

Displacement value is a formulation calculation used in suppository preparation to estimate how much base is displaced by a given mass of drug. It helps calculate the amount of base required to fill a mould while accounting for the volume occupied by the drug. The exact convention and formula must match the method taught in the course.

Q19. Describe the general methods of suppository preparation.

Common methods include hand rolling, compression and fusion/moulding. In fusion, the base is melted under suitable conditions, the drug is incorporated uniformly, the mixture is poured into calibrated moulds, allowed to set, removed and checked. Heat-sensitive ingredients require appropriate handling.

Q20. What is the difference between an ointment, cream, paste and gel?

Ointments are semisolid preparations based on an appropriate ointment base and are often greasy. Creams are semisolid emulsions. Pastes contain a high proportion of dispersed solids and are generally stiff. Gels use a network-forming agent to hold a liquid phase.
