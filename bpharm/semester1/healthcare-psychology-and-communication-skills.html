UNIT I · 3 HOURS

Introduction to Psychology in Healthcare

Topics: Definition, scope and relevance of psychology in health sciences; clinical, health, behavioural and developmental psychology; sensation, perception and attention; learning and memory; reinforcement in health behaviour change; emotion and motivation.

Q1. Define psychology.

Psychology is the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes. It studies how people think, feel, learn, remember and act.

Q2. What is healthcare psychology, and why is it important?

Healthcare psychology applies psychological knowledge to health, illness and healthcare. It helps healthcare workers understand patients' feelings and behaviour, communicate clearly, support treatment adherence, manage stress and improve the patient experience.

Q3. State the scope of psychology in health sciences.

Its scope includes understanding health behaviour, supporting prevention, explaining responses to illness, improving communication, assisting behaviour change, understanding stress and coping, supporting rehabilitation and helping healthcare teams provide patient-centred care.

Q4. Explain clinical psychology.

Clinical psychology focuses on the assessment and psychological care of emotional, behavioural and mental health difficulties. Clinical psychologists may use interviews, psychological tests and evidence-based psychological interventions within their professional training and scope.

Q5. What is health psychology?

Health psychology studies how biological, psychological and social factors affect health, illness and healthcare. It considers habits such as sleep, exercise, diet, smoking, stress management and medicine-taking behaviour.

Q6. What is behavioural psychology?

Behavioural psychology studies observable actions and how they are learned or changed through experience, consequences and the environment. Its principles can help design routines that support healthy behaviour.

Q7. What is developmental psychology?

Developmental psychology studies changes in physical, cognitive, emotional and social functioning across the lifespan, from infancy through old age. It helps healthcare workers adapt explanations and care to a person's developmental stage.

Q8. Differentiate between sensation and perception.

Sensation is the detection of information by sensory receptors, such as hearing a sound or feeling pressure. Perception is the process of organizing and interpreting that sensory information, such as recognizing the sound as a warning alarm.

Q9. What is attention? Why is it important in healthcare?

Attention is the process of focusing mental resources on selected information. It matters when patients listen to medicine instructions, understand risks, complete forms or remember follow-up advice. Noise, pain, anxiety and fatigue can reduce attention.

Q10. Define learning and memory.

Learning is a relatively lasting change in knowledge or behaviour due to experience or practice. Memory is the ability to encode, retain and retrieve information. Repetition, meaningful explanations and practice can help patients remember health instructions.

Q11. What is reinforcement?

Reinforcement is a consequence that increases the likelihood of a behaviour occurring again. Positive reinforcement adds a desirable consequence; negative reinforcement removes an unpleasant condition. Negative reinforcement is not the same as punishment.

Q12. How can reinforcement support a health behaviour change?

A person may use a simple tracking chart and give themselves a healthy reward after meeting a realistic activity goal. Praise for progress, reminders and a supportive environment can also strengthen a routine. The reward should be safe and appropriate.

Q13. Define emotion and motivation.

Emotion is a feeling state involving subjective experience and often bodily and behavioural responses. Motivation is the set of processes that initiate, direct and sustain goal-oriented behaviour.

Q14. How can emotions affect healthcare?

Fear or anxiety may make it harder to understand information or attend appointments. Hope and trust may help a patient participate in care. Healthcare workers should acknowledge feelings respectfully, provide clear information and avoid judging patients.

Q15. Explain the relevance of motivation in patient care.

Motivation can influence whether a person follows a treatment plan, attends follow-up visits or makes lifestyle changes. Healthcare workers can support motivation by listening to the person's goals, explaining benefits clearly, setting achievable steps and recognizing progress.

UNIT II · 3 HOURS

Developmental and Behavioural Psychology

Topics: Human developmental stages and healthcare needs; personality theories and patient interaction styles; psychological factors affecting illness perception and recovery; anxiety, depression and somatization; coping, resilience and stress management.

Q1. What are the main stages of human development?

A broad classification is infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood and older adulthood. Each stage may involve different physical, cognitive, emotional and social needs. Individual development varies, so age alone should not be used to make assumptions about a patient.

Q2. Why should healthcare communication change with developmental stage?

Children may need simple words and support from a caregiver; adolescents may value privacy and respectful involvement in decisions; adults may prefer collaborative discussion; and some older adults may need larger print, extra time or hearing support. Communication should be adapted to the individual, not based only on age.

Q3. What is personality?

Personality refers to relatively enduring patterns of thinking, feeling and behaving that distinguish one person from another. It can influence how someone responds to illness, uncertainty and healthcare interactions.

Q4. Briefly explain major approaches to personality.

Trait approach: describes patterns using traits such as conscientiousness or sociability. Psychodynamic approach: emphasizes unconscious processes and early experiences. Humanistic approach: emphasizes personal meaning, growth and choice. Social-cognitive approach: considers learning, beliefs, expectations and the environment. These are theoretical approaches, not labels to diagnose a patient.

Q5. How can personality affect patient interaction?

Some patients may ask many questions, while others may be quiet or want more time before deciding. A healthcare worker should avoid stereotyping, check the patient's preferences, explain options clearly and invite questions without pressure.

Q6. What is illness perception?

Illness perception is how a person understands and interprets a health problem—for example, its cause, seriousness, expected duration and effect on life. It can influence distress, help-seeking and treatment behaviour.

Q7. Name psychological factors that can affect illness and recovery.

Stress, fear, mood, beliefs about illness, expectations, coping skills, social support, past experiences, health literacy and confidence in managing treatment may affect how a person experiences illness and engages with care. These factors do not mean the illness is imaginary.

Q8. Define anxiety.

Anxiety is a state of worry, fear or apprehension, often accompanied by physical symptoms such as a racing heart, muscle tension or restlessness. Some anxiety is a normal response to uncertainty, but persistent or severe anxiety may require assessment by a qualified professional.

Q9. Define depression in a psychological context.

Depression can refer to a low mood, but depressive disorder involves a persistent pattern of symptoms that may include low mood or loss of interest, changes in sleep or appetite, low energy, difficulty concentrating, feelings of worthlessness or thoughts of death. Diagnosis should be made by a qualified health professional.

Q10. What is somatization?

Somatization describes the experience or expression of psychological distress through physical symptoms. Physical symptoms are real to the person and should be assessed respectfully. Healthcare workers should not assume a symptom is psychological without appropriate medical assessment.

Q11. What is coping?

Coping refers to thoughts and actions used to manage demands or stress. Problem-focused coping attempts to address the problem; emotion-focused coping aims to manage the emotional response. Both may be useful depending on the situation.

Q12. What is resilience?

Resilience is the capacity to adapt or recover when facing difficulty. It does not mean a person never feels upset. Social support, practical resources, flexible problem-solving and access to care can help build resilience.

Q13. List practical stress-management techniques.

Examples include slow breathing, regular physical activity as appropriate, adequate sleep, planning tasks, taking breaks, relaxation practice, maintaining social connections and seeking professional support when stress becomes difficult to manage.

Q14. How can a pharmacist support a patient who is anxious about medicine?

Listen without interruption, ask what worries the patient, explain the purpose and correct use of the medicine in plain language, discuss common and serious warning signs accurately, check understanding and refer medicine-specific concerns to the prescriber when needed. Do not dismiss concerns or promise that side effects cannot occur.

Q15. Differentiate between problem-focused and emotion-focused coping.

Problem-focused coping changes or manages the source of stress—for example, arranging transport to an appointment. Emotion-focused coping manages feelings—for example, relaxation or talking with a trusted person. They can be combined.

UNIT III · 3 HOURS

Foundations of Health Communication

Topics: Elements and models of communication; interpersonal, group, mass and telehealth communication; barriers; active listening, questioning, empathy, culturally appropriate and inclusive communication.

Q1. Define communication.

Communication is the process of exchanging information, ideas or feelings between people. Effective healthcare communication helps patients understand information, share concerns and take part in decisions.

Q2. Name the main elements of communication.

Common elements are sender, message, encoding, channel, receiver, decoding, feedback and context. Noise or other barriers can interfere with the message at any stage.

Q3. Explain a basic communication model.

A sender forms and sends a message through a channel. The receiver interprets it and may give feedback. The sender checks the feedback to see whether the message was understood. In healthcare, asking a patient to explain the instructions in their own words is one way to check understanding.

Q4. What is interpersonal communication?

Interpersonal communication is direct communication between people, such as a pharmacist explaining a medicine to a patient. It can include spoken words, writing, facial expressions, gestures and tone of voice.

Q5. What is group communication?

Group communication occurs among several people, such as a healthcare team meeting or a patient education session. It requires clear roles, respectful turn-taking, accurate information and a shared understanding of the goal.

Q6. What is mass communication?

Mass communication delivers information to a large audience through channels such as radio, television, newspapers, posters or public websites. Health messages should be accurate, accessible and suitable for the intended audience.

Q7. What is telehealth communication?

Telehealth communication uses digital technologies, such as video calls, telephone calls or secure messaging, to provide healthcare information or services at a distance. Privacy, identity verification, clear audio, documentation and a plan for urgent situations are important.

Q8. List barriers to effective healthcare communication.

Barriers include language differences, medical jargon, hearing or vision difficulties, low health literacy, noise, time pressure, fear, pain, cultural misunderstandings, poor privacy, technology problems and assumptions made by either person.

Q9. What is active listening?

Active listening means giving full attention, avoiding unnecessary interruption, noticing verbal and non-verbal cues, asking clarifying questions and summarizing what the person has said. It shows respect and helps prevent misunderstanding.

Q10. What is empathy in healthcare?

Empathy is the effort to understand another person's feelings and point of view and to respond respectfully. For example: “It sounds as though managing these medicines feels overwhelming. Let us go through them one at a time.” Empathy does not require agreeing with every belief.

Q11. What types of questions can improve communication?

Open questions invite a fuller answer, such as “What concerns do you have about this medicine?” Closed questions check a specific fact, such as “Have you taken today's dose?” Both types are useful when used appropriately.

Q12. What is the teach-back method?

Teach-back asks a patient to explain important instructions in their own words. It checks how clearly the healthcare worker explained the information; it is not a test of the patient. If the answer is unclear, explain the information again in a different way and recheck.

Q13. What is non-verbal communication?

Non-verbal communication includes facial expressions, posture, gestures, eye contact, personal space and tone of voice. These cues can support or contradict spoken words. Their meaning varies across individuals and cultures, so they should not be interpreted rigidly.

Q14. How can healthcare communication be culturally appropriate and inclusive?

Use respectful language, ask about communication preferences, avoid stereotypes, arrange a qualified interpreter when needed, use accessible formats, respect cultural practices where safe and relevant, and include the patient in decisions. Do not assume a family member should interpret sensitive information.

Q15. Why is plain language important?

Plain language makes information easier to understand and remember. Use familiar words, short sentences, clear steps and specific timing. Explain necessary medical terms and invite questions rather than relying on jargon.

UNIT IV · 3 HOURS

Professional Communication in Healthcare Settings

Topics: Communication with patients, caregivers and interdisciplinary teams; difficult news and emotional situations; confidentiality and consent; patient records, reports and discharge summaries; technology and digital communication tools.

Q1. What is professional communication in healthcare?

Professional communication is clear, respectful, accurate and appropriate information-sharing between healthcare workers, patients and caregivers. It supports safety, trust, teamwork and continuity of care.

Q2. How should a healthcare worker communicate with a patient?

Introduce yourself and your role, confirm the patient's identity appropriately, protect privacy, ask about the patient's needs, use plain language, listen carefully, explain options and next steps, check understanding and document relevant information.

Q3. How should healthcare workers communicate with caregivers?

With the patient's permission and in line with applicable law and policy, explain relevant care information clearly, invite questions, clarify the caregiver's role and respect the patient's preferences and privacy. A caregiver should not automatically receive all confidential information.

Q4. What is interdisciplinary communication?

Interdisciplinary communication is the exchange of relevant information among professionals from different healthcare disciplines. It supports coordinated care, reduces duplication and helps identify medicine-related or other patient-safety concerns.

Q5. What makes a healthcare handover effective?

A good handover is timely, structured, accurate and focused on relevant facts, current concerns, treatment, outstanding tasks and follow-up. The sender should allow questions and confirm that the receiver understands key actions.

Q6. How should difficult news be communicated?

Choose a suitable private setting, find out what the person already understands, ask how much detail they want, use clear and honest language, pause to allow emotion, acknowledge feelings and explain the next steps. The person delivering the news must work within their professional role and coordinate with the responsible clinician.

Q7. How should an emotionally charged situation be handled?

Remain calm, use a respectful tone, listen without arguing, acknowledge the concern, keep appropriate personal space and focus on immediate needs and safe next steps. If there is a threat to safety, follow the workplace's emergency procedures and seek help.

Q8. Define confidentiality.

Confidentiality is the duty to protect private information obtained through professional care and not disclose it without a valid basis. Information should be shared only with authorized people for legitimate purposes, subject to applicable law, consent and safety-related exceptions.

Q9. What is informed consent?

Informed consent is a voluntary decision made after receiving and understanding relevant information about a proposed intervention, including its purpose, important benefits, risks and alternatives. The legal requirements and who may obtain consent depend on the procedure, capacity and applicable rules.

Q10. What are the principles of good healthcare documentation?

Records should be accurate, clear, timely, relevant, legible and factual. They should identify the author and date as required, distinguish reported information from observations, record important decisions and follow institutional policy for corrections and access.

Q11. What is a patient report?

A patient report is a structured record or communication about a patient's condition, care or progress. It should include the relevant findings, actions taken, response, outstanding concerns and follow-up, according to the purpose of the report.

Q12. What is a discharge summary?

A discharge summary communicates key information when a patient leaves a healthcare facility. It commonly includes the reason for admission, relevant diagnoses, important treatment, medicine list and changes, condition at discharge, follow-up plan and warning signs or instructions.

Q13. What are the benefits and risks of digital communication in healthcare?

Benefits: faster information exchange, easier follow-up, improved access and better coordination. Risks: privacy breaches, wrong-recipient messages, incomplete context, technical failures and unclear records. Use approved systems, verify recipients and avoid sharing sensitive information through insecure channels.

Q14. What is professional etiquette in digital communication?

Use approved channels, identify yourself, keep messages concise and respectful, check the recipient, avoid unnecessary patient identifiers, follow response-time policies, document important clinical communications and do not assume a message has been read unless confirmation is available.

Q15. Why are confidentiality and consent important?

They protect patient dignity, autonomy and trust. Patients are more likely to share relevant information when they know it will be handled appropriately. Healthcare workers must follow professional codes, organizational policies and applicable law.

UNIT V · 3 HOURS

Health Psychology and Behavioural Interventions

Topics: Health belief models and illness behaviour; psychosomatic illness and mind–body connection; behaviour change theories such as CBT and TTM; treatment adherence; psychological first aid and crisis communication; mental health promotion and stigma reduction.

Q1. What is health behaviour?

Health behaviour is an action that can affect health, such as taking medicine as directed, attending screening, exercising, using protective equipment or seeking medical help when needed.

Q2. Explain the Health Belief Model.

The Health Belief Model suggests that health actions can be influenced by perceived susceptibility (chance of getting a condition), perceived severity (how serious it seems), perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action and self-efficacy (confidence in performing the action). It is a framework for understanding behaviour, not a complete explanation of every person's choices.

Q3. What is illness behaviour?

Illness behaviour includes how people notice symptoms, interpret them, decide whether to seek help and respond to advice or treatment. It may be influenced by culture, past experience, access to care, beliefs, stress and social support.

Q4. Explain the mind–body connection.

Mental and physical processes influence each other. Stress can affect sleep, muscle tension, digestion and other bodily processes, while physical illness can affect mood and thinking. This does not mean physical symptoms are imaginary; new or concerning symptoms require appropriate assessment.

Q5. What are psychosomatic illnesses or symptoms?

The term is often used for physical symptoms or conditions in which psychological processes, such as stress, can contribute to or worsen symptoms. Psychological and biological factors can act together. Assessment should consider physical causes as well as emotional and social factors.

Q6. What is cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)?

CBT is a structured psychological treatment that examines links among thoughts, feelings and behaviours. It may help people identify unhelpful thinking patterns and practise more useful coping behaviours. It is provided by appropriately trained professionals and is not simply “thinking positively.”

Q7. What is the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) of behaviour change?

TTM describes stages that people may move through when changing a behaviour: precontemplation (not considering change), contemplation (thinking about change), preparation (planning), action (making the change) and maintenance (working to sustain it). People may move between stages rather than follow a perfectly straight path.

Q8. How can a healthcare worker use TTM to support a patient?

First ask how the patient feels about the change. Provide information without pressure when they are not ready; explore pros and barriers when they are considering it; help plan small steps when ready; support practical action; and help identify ways to maintain progress and respond to setbacks.

Q9. Define treatment adherence.

Treatment adherence is the extent to which a person's actions—such as taking medicines, following agreed lifestyle steps and attending appointments—match the plan agreed with the healthcare professional. It is better supported through shared decisions than blame.

Q10. What factors can reduce medicine adherence?

Possible factors include cost, side effects, complex schedules, difficulty understanding instructions, forgetfulness, doubts about benefit, fear, stigma, poor access, cultural beliefs and lack of support. Ask the patient about the actual barrier before suggesting a solution.

Q11. How can a pharmacist help improve adherence?

Explain the purpose and use of each medicine, simplify information, use a schedule or reminder where appropriate, check understanding with teach-back, discuss concerns without judgment, identify access problems and coordinate with the prescriber when the regimen or side effects need review.

Q12. What is psychological first aid (PFA)?

Psychological first aid is humane, supportive and practical help for someone experiencing serious distress after a crisis. It focuses on safety, dignity, listening, meeting immediate needs and connecting people with support; it is not a replacement for professional mental healthcare when that is needed. The World Health Organization describes PFA guidance for supporting people after distressing events. WHO guide for field workers.

Q13. Explain the “Look, Listen, Link” approach to psychological first aid.

Look: check safety, urgent basic needs and signs of serious distress. Listen: approach respectfully, ask what the person needs and listen without pressuring them to speak. Link: help them access practical support, reliable information, services and trusted people. Adapt help to the person's culture and circumstances.

Q14. What should a healthcare worker do during crisis communication?

Prioritize immediate safety, speak calmly and clearly, give accurate information, acknowledge distress, avoid speculation and false promises, explain practical next steps, protect privacy and connect the person to appropriate professional or emergency support. Do not force someone to describe a traumatic event.

Q15. What is mental health promotion?

Mental health promotion aims to strengthen mental well-being and protective factors. Examples include supportive relationships, reducing isolation, sleep and activity routines, coping skills, safe environments, access to care and public education that encourages people to seek help early.

Q16. What is mental health stigma?

Stigma is negative stereotyping, prejudice or discrimination directed at people because of mental health difficulties. It can discourage people from seeking support or speaking honestly about their needs.

Q17. How can communication reduce mental health stigma?

Use respectful, person-first language; share accurate information; avoid jokes and labels; listen to lived experiences; correct myths calmly; protect privacy; and present seeking help as a reasonable step. Do not define a person only by a diagnosis.

Q18. Differentiate between health promotion and treatment.

Health promotion supports well-being and reduces risk across individuals or communities. Treatment addresses a particular health condition or problem. They can work together, but health promotion does not replace needed clinical assessment or treatment.
