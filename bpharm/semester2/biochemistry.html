UNIT I — Enzymology, Vitamins and Clinical Chemistry (10 hours)
Q1. Define an enzyme. State its important properties.

An enzyme is a biological catalyst, usually a protein, that increases the rate of a biochemical reaction without being consumed overall. Enzymes lower activation energy, are generally highly specific, work best within suitable temperature and pH ranges, and may require cofactors. They do not change the overall free-energy difference or equilibrium position of a reaction.

Q2. What are apoenzyme, cofactor, coenzyme and holoenzyme?

An apoenzyme is the protein portion of an enzyme without its required non-protein component. A cofactor is a non-protein component needed for activity, often a metal ion or an organic coenzyme. A coenzyme is a small organic helper molecule, often derived from a vitamin. The active complete enzyme with its required component is the holoenzyme.

Q3. Explain enzyme nomenclature and the IUBMB classification.

Enzymes are commonly named by adding “-ase” to the substrate or reaction, such as lactase or dehydrogenase. The IUBMB EC system classifies enzymes by the reaction they catalyse. The major classes are oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases and, in the expanded modern system, translocases. EC numbers identify the reaction class and specific enzyme activity.

Q4. What is the active site of an enzyme?

The active site is the region where the substrate binds and the chemical reaction is facilitated. Its shape and chemical properties help determine substrate specificity. Binding can involve hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, hydrophobic effects and temporary covalent interactions.

Q5. Explain the lock-and-key and induced-fit models.

The lock-and-key model pictures the active site as already complementary to the substrate. The induced-fit model states that substrate binding can change the enzyme's shape to improve binding and catalysis. Induced fit better represents the flexibility of many enzymes.

Q6. What is enzyme kinetics?

Enzyme kinetics studies how reaction rate changes with substrate concentration, enzyme concentration, temperature, pH and inhibitors. It helps compare enzyme activity and understand how medicines or disease affect biochemical reactions.

Q7. State the Michaelis–Menten equation and define its terms.

For a simple enzyme reaction under Michaelis–Menten assumptions, v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]). Here v is the initial reaction rate, Vmax is the maximum rate at substrate saturation, [S] is substrate concentration and Km is the substrate concentration at which v equals half of Vmax. A lower Km often indicates higher apparent substrate affinity in the relevant simple system, but Km is not always a direct binding constant.

Q8. What is a Michaelis plot?

A Michaelis–Menten plot graphs initial reaction velocity (v) against substrate concentration [S]. The curve rises and approaches Vmax as the enzyme becomes saturated. Km is read at the substrate concentration corresponding to half of Vmax.

Q9. Explain the Lineweaver–Burk plot and its limitation.

The Lineweaver–Burk plot graphs 1/v against 1/[S]. For the Michaelis–Menten equation, the y-intercept is 1/Vmax and the x-intercept is −1/Km; the slope is Km/Vmax. It is useful for teaching inhibition patterns but reciprocal transformation magnifies errors at low substrate concentrations, so nonlinear fitting is usually preferred for accurate parameter estimation.

Q10. What are enzyme inhibitors? Explain competitive and non-competitive inhibition.

Enzyme inhibitors reduce enzyme activity. In ideal competitive inhibition, inhibitor competes at the active site; apparent Km increases while Vmax remains unchanged, and sufficiently high substrate concentration can overcome the inhibition. In ideal pure non-competitive inhibition, inhibitor binds at a separate site to enzyme and enzyme–substrate complex; Vmax decreases while Km remains unchanged. Real mixed inhibitors can affect both parameters.

Q11. Distinguish reversible and irreversible enzyme inhibition.

Reversible inhibitors bind through interactions that can dissociate, so activity may return when the inhibitor is removed. Irreversible inhibitors inactivate an enzyme persistently, often through covalent modification or very tight binding. The distinction depends on the inhibitor and reaction conditions.

Q12. How is enzyme activity regulated?

Regulation can occur through enzyme induction or repression, allosteric regulation, covalent modification such as phosphorylation, substrate availability, product inhibition, compartmentalisation and degradation of the enzyme. These mechanisms help cells coordinate metabolism with their needs.

Q13. Explain allosteric regulation.

An allosteric regulator binds at a site distinct from the active site and changes enzyme activity by altering its conformation. An allosteric activator increases activity; an allosteric inhibitor reduces it. Allosteric enzymes often show sigmoidal rather than simple hyperbolic substrate-response curves.

Q14. What are isoenzymes? Give examples and uses.

Isoenzymes are different molecular forms that catalyse the same reaction but differ in structure, tissue distribution or properties. Examples include lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) isoenzymes and creatine kinase (CK) isoenzymes. Their patterns can assist clinical assessment, but modern diagnosis often uses more specific tests and must consider timing and clinical context.

Q15. State the therapeutic and diagnostic uses of enzymes.

Therapeutic enzymes include some clot-dissolving agents, digestive enzyme preparations and enzyme-replacement therapies for selected inherited disorders. Diagnostic enzymes or enzyme-linked methods can measure analytes; changes in enzymes such as ALT, AST, ALP or lipase may support assessment of organ injury. Enzyme results must be interpreted with symptoms and other investigations.

Q16. List the main factors affecting enzyme activity.

Important factors include temperature, pH, substrate concentration, enzyme concentration, product concentration, cofactors, ionic strength and inhibitors or activators. Extreme temperature or pH can alter the enzyme's structure and reduce activity.

Q17. Explain digestion and absorption of carbohydrates, proteins and lipids.

Carbohydrates are broken down by amylases and brush-border enzymes into absorbable monosaccharides. Proteins are digested by gastric and pancreatic proteases and intestinal peptidases into amino acids and small peptides. Bile salts emulsify dietary fat; pancreatic lipase forms fatty acids and monoacylglycerols, which are absorbed and largely packaged into chylomicrons. Most nutrients are absorbed in the small intestine.

Q18. Distinguish macronutrients and micronutrients.

Macronutrients—carbohydrates, proteins and fats—are needed in relatively large amounts and provide energy or structural material. Micronutrients—vitamins and minerals—are required in smaller amounts for enzyme function, regulation, tissue structure and other physiological processes. Both groups are essential for health.

Q19. Summarise the major vitamins, their functions and deficiency disorders.

A: Vitamin A supports vision and epithelial tissues; deficiency can cause night blindness. B1 (thiamine): carbohydrate metabolism; deficiency can cause beriberi or Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome. B2 (riboflavin): FAD/FMN coenzymes; deficiency may cause cheilosis and glossitis. B3 (niacin): NAD/NADP; deficiency causes pellagra. B6: amino-acid metabolism; deficiency can cause dermatitis, anaemia or neurological symptoms. B9 (folate) and B12: DNA synthesis and red-cell formation; deficiency can cause megaloblastic anaemia, with B12 deficiency also causing neurological injury. C: collagen synthesis and antioxidant roles; deficiency causes scurvy. D: calcium and phosphate homeostasis; deficiency causes rickets or osteomalacia. E: antioxidant protection; severe deficiency can cause neurological problems. K: clotting-factor activation; deficiency can cause bleeding.

Q20. Why are minerals important? Give examples.

Iron is needed for haemoglobin; iodine for thyroid hormones; calcium and phosphorus for bones and teeth; sodium and potassium for fluid balance and nerve impulses; magnesium for many enzymes; and zinc for numerous enzymes and normal healing. Deficiency or excess can cause illness, so supplementation should be appropriate to need.

Q21. What are liver function tests (LFTs)?

LFT panels commonly include ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), bilirubin, albumin and sometimes prothrombin time/INR. ALT and AST can indicate hepatocellular injury; ALP and GGT may support assessment of cholestasis; bilirubin relates to heme breakdown and liver/biliary handling; albumin and INR can help assess synthetic function, though each has non-liver influences.

Q22. What are renal function tests?

Common assessments include serum creatinine with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), blood urea, electrolytes and urinalysis including urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio when indicated. Creatinine-based eGFR estimates filtration and can be affected by muscle mass and other factors. Kidney function is interpreted using trends and the full clinical picture.

Q23. What is ELISA? State its principle and uses.

ELISA means enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. It uses specific antigen–antibody binding and an enzyme-linked detection system that produces a measurable signal, often a colour change. Depending on the design, it can detect or quantify antibodies, antigens, hormones or other analytes in diagnostic and research laboratories.

UNIT II — Biomolecules, Bioenergetics and Carbohydrate Metabolism (10 hours)
Q1. Define biomolecules and name the major groups.

Biomolecules are chemical compounds involved in living systems. Major groups include carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids and smaller molecules such as vitamins, metabolites and signalling compounds. They provide structure, energy, information storage and regulation.

Q2. What are carbohydrates?

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds that yield them on hydrolysis. They include monosaccharides such as glucose, disaccharides such as sucrose and lactose, and polysaccharides such as glycogen and starch. They are important energy sources and also have structural and recognition roles.

Q3. Define lipids, proteins and nucleic acids.

Lipids are a diverse group of relatively water-insoluble molecules including triacylglycerols, phospholipids and cholesterol. Proteins are chains of amino acids that fold into structures supporting catalysis, transport, signalling and tissue function. Nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, are nucleotide polymers that store, transmit and express genetic information.

Q4. Define bioenergetics and free energy.

Bioenergetics studies energy changes in living systems. Gibbs free-energy change (ΔG) predicts the thermodynamic direction of a process at constant temperature and pressure: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Here ΔH is enthalpy change, T is absolute temperature and ΔS is entropy change. A negative ΔG indicates a thermodynamically favourable process under the stated conditions, but does not mean it is fast.

Q5. Explain ATP and other energy-rich compounds.

ATP transfers usable chemical energy by coupling its hydrolysis to cellular processes. GTP supplies energy in processes such as translation and signalling. Other compounds, including phosphocreatine and certain activated intermediates, help transfer energy or chemical groups. “High-energy” refers to a favourable free-energy change of hydrolysis, not simply to a bond that releases energy by breaking alone.

Q6. What is redox potential?

Redox potential describes the tendency of a chemical species to accept or donate electrons under specified conditions. Electron transfer from a donor to an acceptor can release free energy when the overall redox potential difference is favourable. Cells use redox carriers such as NAD⁺/NADH and FAD/FADH₂ in metabolism.

Q7. What is glycolysis? State its site and outcome.

Glycolysis is the pathway that converts one glucose molecule into two pyruvate molecules in the cytosol. Under the usual aerobic accounting, it produces a net 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose. In cells relying on anaerobic glycolysis, pyruvate can be reduced to lactate to regenerate NAD⁺.

Q8. What is the citric acid cycle (TCA cycle)?

The TCA cycle occurs mainly in the mitochondrial matrix in eukaryotic cells. Acetyl-CoA is oxidised, generating reduced coenzymes (NADH and FADH₂), GTP or ATP, and CO₂. These reduced coenzymes deliver electrons to the electron transport chain. The cycle also supplies intermediates for biosynthesis.

Q9. Explain gluconeogenesis.

Gluconeogenesis produces glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors such as lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acids. It occurs mainly in the liver and, during prolonged fasting, also in the kidney. It maintains blood glucose for tissues that depend on it, and it uses bypass reactions because glycolysis contains irreversible steps.

Q10. What is the hexose monophosphate (HMP) shunt?

The HMP shunt, also called the pentose phosphate pathway, occurs in the cytosol. Its oxidative phase produces NADPH, which supports reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defence, and its pathway supplies ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis. NADPH is especially important for maintaining reduced glutathione in red blood cells.

Q11. Explain glycogen metabolism.

Glycogenesis stores glucose as glycogen, mainly in liver and skeletal muscle. Glycogenolysis breaks glycogen down to release glucose-1-phosphate and related products. Liver glycogen helps maintain blood glucose; muscle glycogen mainly fuels muscle contraction because skeletal muscle lacks significant glucose-6-phosphatase activity for releasing free glucose into blood.

Q12. How is blood glucose regulated?

Insulin generally lowers blood glucose by promoting glucose uptake in responsive tissues and supporting glycogen and fat synthesis. Glucagon raises blood glucose mainly by stimulating hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. Adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone also affect glucose availability during stress or fasting.

Q13. Describe metabolism in fed, fasting and prolonged-starvation states.

In the fed state, insulin supports glucose use and storage as glycogen and fat. During early fasting, the liver uses glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis to maintain blood glucose. As fasting continues, gluconeogenesis remains important and fat breakdown increases; the liver produces ketone bodies, which the brain can increasingly use during prolonged starvation. Protein breakdown is moderated as ketone use rises, although severe prolonged starvation remains dangerous.

Q14. What is diabetes mellitus? Briefly explain its biochemical basis.

Diabetes mellitus is a group of disorders characterised by persistent hyperglycaemia due to inadequate insulin secretion, impaired insulin action, or both. Type 1 commonly involves autoimmune loss of pancreatic beta cells; type 2 involves insulin resistance with progressive beta-cell dysfunction. Chronic hyperglycaemia can damage blood vessels, nerves, kidneys, eyes and other tissues.

Q15. What are common biochemical tests used in diabetes assessment?

Tests include fasting plasma glucose, random plasma glucose in appropriate circumstances, oral glucose tolerance testing and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c). Diagnostic cut-offs and confirmation rules should follow current clinical guidelines and account for conditions that affect the reliability of HbA1c or glucose measurements.

Q16. Explain biological oxidation and the electron transport chain (ETC).

Biological oxidation transfers electrons from nutrients to electron carriers. In mitochondria, NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to the ETC in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electron flow through complexes helps pump protons across the membrane, creating an electrochemical gradient used for ATP synthesis.

Q17. What is oxidative phosphorylation?

Oxidative phosphorylation is ATP production driven by the proton gradient generated by electron transport. Protons flow back through ATP synthase, coupling that movement to ADP and inorganic phosphate forming ATP. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor in the usual aerobic chain and is reduced to water.

Q18. What are oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction?

Oxidative stress occurs when reactive species production exceeds antioxidant and repair capacity. Mitochondrial dysfunction can reduce energy production, alter redox balance and increase cellular stress. These processes are implicated in multiple diseases, but the mechanisms and clinical significance differ by condition and tissue.

UNIT III — Lipid Metabolism (7 hours)
Q1. Classify lipids and state their functions.

Lipids include simple lipids such as triacylglycerols, complex lipids such as phospholipids and glycolipids, and derived lipids such as fatty acids, cholesterol and steroid hormones. They store energy, form cell membranes, insulate organs, support signalling and help absorb fat-soluble vitamins.

Q2. What are lipoproteins and why are they needed?

Lipoproteins are particles that transport hydrophobic lipids through blood. Their core contains triacylglycerols and cholesteryl esters, surrounded by phospholipids, free cholesterol and apolipoproteins. Different lipoproteins carry dietary or liver-derived lipids and participate in lipid delivery and return transport.

Q3. Compare chylomicrons, VLDL, LDL and HDL.

Chylomicrons transport dietary triacylglycerols from the intestine. VLDL carries mainly liver-produced triacylglycerols. LDL delivers cholesterol to tissues and higher LDL-cholesterol is associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk. HDL participates in cholesterol transport back toward the liver and other pathways; HDL function is complex, so a high HDL value is not a guarantee against cardiovascular disease.

Q4. Explain beta-oxidation of fatty acids.

Beta-oxidation breaks fatty acyl-CoA into acetyl-CoA units through repeated cycles of oxidation, hydration, oxidation and thiolysis. It generates NADH and FADH₂, which support ATP production. Long-chain fatty acids are generally activated and transported into mitochondria using the carnitine shuttle before oxidation.

Q5. What is de novo fatty-acid synthesis?

De novo synthesis makes fatty acids, mainly in the cytosol, from acetyl-CoA-derived units. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase produces malonyl-CoA, and fatty-acid synthase builds the chain using reducing power from NADPH. The pathway is regulated by nutritional and hormonal state and is generally more active when energy is abundant.

Q6. Compare beta-oxidation and fatty-acid synthesis.

Beta-oxidation degrades fatty acids, mainly in mitochondria, producing acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH₂. Fatty-acid synthesis builds fatty acids in the cytosol, uses acetyl-CoA/malonyl-CoA and consumes NADPH. Their enzymes, carriers, cellular locations and regulation differ, preventing a simple futile cycle.

Q7. What are ketone bodies? When are they produced?

The principal ketone bodies are acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetone. The liver produces them from acetyl-CoA when fatty-acid oxidation is high, especially during fasting, prolonged exercise or uncontrolled insulin deficiency. Ketone bodies can fuel extrahepatic tissues, but excessive accumulation can cause dangerous ketoacidosis, particularly in diabetes.

Q8. Explain ketone-body utilisation.

Extrahepatic tissues convert acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate into acetyl-CoA for energy production. The liver produces ketone bodies but cannot use them efficiently itself because it lacks the key transferase enzyme (SCOT/thiophorase) required for their utilisation.

Q9. What is cholesterol and why is it important?

Cholesterol is a membrane component and a precursor for steroid hormones, bile acids and vitamin D. The body synthesises cholesterol, mainly in the liver, and also obtains it from food. Excess atherogenic lipoprotein cholesterol can contribute to cholesterol deposition in artery walls and atherosclerosis.

Q10. What is a lipid profile?

A lipid profile commonly reports total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-cholesterol and LDL-cholesterol, with some laboratories also reporting non-HDL cholesterol or calculated values. Results help assess cardiovascular risk alongside age, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, medical history and other factors.

Q11. Define hyperlipidaemia and hypercholesterolaemia.

Hyperlipidaemia means elevated levels of one or more blood lipids or lipoproteins. Hypercholesterolaemia specifically refers to elevated cholesterol, often LDL-cholesterol. Causes can be genetic, dietary, metabolic or secondary to conditions or medicines; assessment identifies the pattern and underlying cause.

Q12. What are lipid storage diseases?

Lipid storage diseases are inherited metabolic disorders in which defects in enzymes or related proteins cause particular lipids to accumulate in cells. Examples include Gaucher disease and Tay–Sachs disease. The accumulated material and affected organs determine the clinical features.

Q13. Explain atherosclerosis.

Atherosclerosis is a chronic disease of medium and large arteries involving retention of atherogenic lipoproteins, inflammation and plaque formation in the arterial wall. Plaques can narrow arteries or rupture and trigger thrombosis, contributing to heart attack, stroke and peripheral arterial disease.

Q14. What is fatty liver disease?

Fatty liver refers to excess fat accumulation in liver cells. It may be associated with metabolic dysfunction, alcohol exposure, medicines or other causes. Some people develop inflammation and fibrosis. Evaluation depends on risk factors, clinical assessment and appropriate investigations; the term and classification used may vary with current guidelines.

Q15. Explain the biochemical relationship between obesity and lipid metabolism.

Obesity often involves excess energy storage in adipose tissue and may be associated with insulin resistance, increased fatty-acid flux to the liver, higher triglycerides and altered lipoproteins. Risk depends on many factors and is not determined by body weight alone.

UNIT IV — Amino Acids and Protein Metabolism (10 hours)
Q1. Classify amino acids and state their physiological roles.

Amino acids may be classified by side-chain properties (non-polar, polar, acidic, basic or aromatic) and by nutritional requirement (essential, non-essential or conditionally essential). They build proteins and also serve as precursors for neurotransmitters, hormones, nucleotides, heme and other compounds. Essential amino acids must be obtained from the diet in adequate amounts.

Q2. Describe the levels of protein structure.

Primary: amino-acid sequence. Secondary: local structures such as alpha helices and beta sheets. Tertiary: overall three-dimensional fold of one polypeptide. Quaternary: arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits. Structure determines function and can be altered by mutations, denaturation or chemical changes.

Q3. What are plasma proteins? Give their functions.

Major plasma proteins include albumin, immunoglobulins, transport proteins, complement proteins and coagulation factors. Albumin helps maintain colloid osmotic pressure and transports many substances. Immunoglobulins contribute to immunity; clotting proteins support haemostasis. Many are synthesised by the liver, while immunoglobulins are produced by plasma cells.

Q4. Define transamination.

Transamination transfers an amino group from an amino acid to an alpha-keto acid, commonly alpha-ketoglutarate, forming a new amino acid and a new keto acid. Aminotransferases such as ALT and AST catalyse these reactions and usually require pyridoxal phosphate derived from vitamin B6.

Q5. Distinguish oxidative and non-oxidative deamination.

Deamination removes an amino group. Oxidative deamination, such as the glutamate dehydrogenase reaction, releases ammonia while oxidising the substrate. Non-oxidative deamination removes an amino group without an oxidation step, as occurs with some amino acids through specific enzymes. Ammonia is toxic and must be handled safely by metabolism.

Q6. What is decarboxylation of amino acids?

Decarboxylation removes the carboxyl group as CO₂. It can form biologically active amines; for example, histidine can form histamine and glutamate can form GABA. Many amino-acid decarboxylases require pyridoxal phosphate.

Q7. Explain the urea cycle and its importance.

The urea cycle converts toxic ammonia and nitrogen from amino-acid metabolism into urea, which is carried in blood to the kidneys and excreted in urine. Its reactions occur across mitochondria and cytosol in liver cells. Defects can cause hyperammonaemia, which can be life-threatening and requires urgent medical care.

Q8. What is the fate of amino-acid carbon skeletons?

After nitrogen removal, carbon skeletons enter central metabolism as pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, acetoacetate or TCA-cycle intermediates. Glucogenic amino acids can contribute to glucose synthesis; ketogenic amino acids form acetyl-CoA or acetoacetate. Leucine and lysine are exclusively ketogenic; several amino acids are both glucogenic and ketogenic.

Q9. What is phenylketonuria (PKU)?

PKU is usually caused by deficient phenylalanine hydroxylase activity or, less commonly, problems with its cofactor system. Phenylalanine accumulates and can damage brain development if untreated. Newborn screening and early, carefully supervised dietary management help prevent complications.

Q10. What is albinism?

Albinism is a group of inherited conditions involving reduced or absent melanin production or distribution. Some forms involve tyrosinase or other melanin-pathway proteins. Features can include light pigmentation and vision problems; sun protection and ophthalmic support may be important.

Q11. Explain alkaptonuria and tyrosinemia briefly.

Alkaptonuria is commonly due to homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase deficiency, causing homogentisic acid accumulation, dark urine on standing and later connective-tissue problems. Tyrosinemias are inherited disorders of tyrosine metabolism; the type determines the enzyme defect and severity, and some forms can cause serious liver and kidney disease. Diagnosis and treatment require specialist care.

Q12. What are branched-chain amino acids and why are they important?

Leucine, isoleucine and valine are branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs). Their catabolism involves branched-chain aminotransferase and the branched-chain alpha-ketoacid dehydrogenase complex. Deficiency of the latter causes maple syrup urine disease, in which branched-chain amino acids and their ketoacids accumulate and can cause neurological injury.

Q13. Describe neurotransmitters derived from amino acids.

Tryptophan is a precursor of serotonin (5-HT) and melatonin. Tyrosine is a precursor of dopamine, noradrenaline and adrenaline through successive enzymatic steps. Glutamate can be converted to GABA, an inhibitory neurotransmitter. These pathways depend on enzymes, cofactors and regulation, and are important in nervous-system function.

Q14. What is heme and how is it degraded?

Heme is an iron-containing porphyrin group in haemoglobin and other proteins. During red-cell breakdown, heme oxygenase converts heme to biliverdin, releasing iron and carbon monoxide; biliverdin is reduced to bilirubin. Unconjugated bilirubin travels bound to albumin to the liver, where it is conjugated and excreted in bile. Intestinal metabolism produces derivatives that are excreted mainly in stool and partly in urine.

Q15. Define jaundice and name its broad biochemical categories.

Jaundice is yellow discoloration of skin and sclera due to elevated bilirubin. Broad categories include pre-hepatic (for example, increased bilirubin production from haemolysis), hepatic (impaired uptake, conjugation or secretion due to liver disease), and post-hepatic or obstructive (impaired bile flow). Bilirubin fractions and other tests help distinguish causes; jaundice requires clinical assessment.

Q16. Why is ammonia detoxification important?

Ammonia is neurotoxic, particularly when it accumulates in blood. The liver converts much of the nitrogen load into urea; other tissues can temporarily incorporate ammonia into glutamine. Severe liver dysfunction or inherited urea-cycle defects can cause hyperammonaemia and neurological emergencies.

UNIT V — Nucleic Acid Metabolism and Genetic Information Transfer (8 hours)
Q1. What are nucleotides? How do they differ from nucleosides?

A nucleoside consists of a nitrogenous base attached to a pentose sugar. A nucleotide is a nucleoside with one or more phosphate groups. Nucleotides are building blocks of DNA and RNA and also function in energy transfer (ATP/GTP), signalling (cAMP) and coenzymes.

Q2. Name the purine and pyrimidine bases.

Purines are adenine and guanine. Pyrimidines are cytosine, thymine and uracil. DNA uses A, G, C and T; RNA generally uses A, G, C and U.

Q3. Outline purine nucleotide synthesis and degradation.

Purine nucleotides can be built de novo from smaller precursors on a ribose-phosphate framework or recovered through salvage pathways. Purine degradation in humans ultimately produces uric acid. Excess uric acid or reduced excretion can lead to hyperuricaemia, although not everyone with hyperuricaemia develops gout.

Q4. What is gout and how is it related to uric acid?

Gout is an inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of monosodium urate crystals in joints or tissues. Hyperuricaemia increases the likelihood of crystal formation but is not sufficient on its own to diagnose gout. Diagnosis and management depend on the clinical picture and, when needed, crystal identification and appropriate investigations.

Q5. Outline pyrimidine nucleotide metabolism.

Pyrimidine synthesis builds the ring from precursors and then attaches it to activated ribose phosphate to form nucleotides. Pyrimidine nucleotides are used for DNA and RNA synthesis and other cellular processes. Their synthesis and degradation are regulated to maintain nucleotide balance.

Q6. Describe the organisation of the mammalian genome.

Mammalian nuclear DNA is packaged with histone and other proteins into chromatin and organised into chromosomes. Genes include coding regions and regulatory sequences; much of the genome is non-coding but can have structural or regulatory functions. Mitochondria also contain a small genome. Gene expression depends on chromatin state and regulatory elements.

Q7. Explain the central dogma of molecular biology.

The central dogma describes the usual flow of genetic information from DNA to RNA by transcription and from messenger RNA to protein by translation. DNA replication copies DNA. Some biological systems, such as retroviruses, use reverse transcription from RNA to DNA, but protein sequence information is not ordinarily transferred back into nucleic acid.

Q8. What is DNA replication?

DNA replication copies the genome before cell division. It is semiconservative: each new double helix contains one parental strand and one newly synthesised strand. DNA polymerases add nucleotides in the 5′ to 3′ direction using a template; primase, helicase, ligase and other proteins coordinate the process.

Q9. What is transcription?

Transcription synthesises RNA from a DNA template using RNA polymerase. In eukaryotes, many protein-coding genes are transcribed by RNA polymerase II. The initial RNA is processed, including capping, splicing and polyadenylation for many messenger RNAs, before export and translation.

Q10. What is translation?

Translation reads codons in messenger RNA at ribosomes to build a polypeptide. Transfer RNAs deliver amino acids, and ribosomal machinery catalyses peptide-bond formation. Translation proceeds through initiation, elongation and termination; the new polypeptide then folds and may be modified.

Q11. State the main properties of the genetic code.

The genetic code is read in triplets called codons. It is degenerate (many amino acids have more than one codon), generally unambiguous (a codon specifies one amino acid or stop signal), non-overlapping and read in a defined frame. AUG commonly serves as a start codon; UAA, UAG and UGA are stop codons in the standard code.

Q12. Give examples of inhibitors of transcription or translation.

Several antibiotics act on bacterial ribosomes or RNA polymerase, for example tetracyclines and macrolides inhibit aspects of bacterial translation, while rifampicin inhibits bacterial RNA polymerase. Some toxins inhibit eukaryotic protein synthesis, such as diphtheria toxin through modification of elongation factor 2. Selectivity and toxicity depend on the target and drug; these are educational examples, not instructions for use.

Q13. What types of DNA damage occur?

DNA damage can include base modification, oxidation, deamination, alkylation, mismatched bases, single- or double-strand breaks, crosslinks and damage caused by ultraviolet or ionising radiation. If not repaired accurately, damage can produce mutations, cell dysfunction or cell death.

Q14. Name major DNA repair mechanisms.

Proofreading and mismatch repair correct errors made during replication. Base-excision repair removes damaged bases. Nucleotide-excision repair removes bulky lesions such as many UV-induced photoproducts. Direct reversal repairs certain chemical modifications. Double-strand breaks can be repaired by homologous recombination or non-homologous end joining. Each pathway has specific strengths and limitations.

Q15. Give examples of disorders associated with defective DNA repair.

Xeroderma pigmentosum involves defects in nucleotide-excision repair and causes marked sensitivity to UV light and increased skin-cancer risk. Some forms of Lynch syndrome involve mismatch-repair defects and increase risks of certain cancers. Ataxia-telangiectasia involves ATM-related DNA-damage response defects. These examples show why DNA repair is important for genome stability.

Q16. Why is DNA repair clinically important?

DNA repair preserves genetic information, limits mutations and supports normal cell function. Repair defects can contribute to inherited disorders and cancer susceptibility. Some cancer therapies exploit repair weaknesses or cause DNA damage, but treatment choice depends on tumour biology and specialist assessment.
