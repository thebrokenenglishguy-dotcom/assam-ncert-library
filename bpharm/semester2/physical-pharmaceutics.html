UNIT I — Solubility, Distribution Phenomenon and Buffers (9 hours)
Q1. Define solubility.

Solubility is the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a specified amount of solvent at a stated temperature and pressure to form a stable saturated solution. It is commonly expressed as concentration, such as g/L, mol/L or mass per mass of solvent.

Q2. What is a saturated solution?

A saturated solution contains the maximum equilibrium amount of a solute under the stated conditions. An unsaturated solution can dissolve more solute, while a supersaturated solution temporarily contains more dissolved solute than the equilibrium amount and may crystallise when disturbed.

Q3. Explain solute–solvent interactions.

Dissolution depends on interactions among solute particles, solvent molecules and solute–solvent pairs. Important interactions include ion–dipole forces, hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole forces and dispersion forces. The general principle “like dissolves like” means substances with similar polarity often mix well, but it is only a guide, not an absolute rule.

Q4. What factors affect the solubility of solids in liquids?

Important factors include temperature, solute and solvent chemistry, pH for ionisable compounds, salt formation, crystal form or polymorphism, common-ion effects and complex formation. Particle size usually changes the rate of dissolution more than the equilibrium solubility, although very small particles can show increased apparent solubility.

Q5. What factors affect the solubility of gases in liquids?

Gas solubility is affected by gas and solvent nature, temperature and gas pressure. For many gases, solubility increases as the partial pressure above the liquid increases and decreases as temperature rises. Chemical reaction between gas and solvent can change this behaviour.

Q6. State Henry’s law.

At constant temperature, the concentration of a gas dissolved in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid within the law’s applicable range. One common form is C = kH P, where C is dissolved concentration, P is partial pressure and kH is a constant under the chosen convention. Check the definition and units of the constant used.

Q7. State Raoult’s law.

For an ideal solution, the partial vapour pressure of a volatile component equals its mole fraction in the liquid multiplied by the vapour pressure of the pure component: pᵢ = xᵢ pᵢ°. For a solution containing a non-volatile solute, the solvent vapour pressure is reduced relative to the pure solvent.

Q8. What is the effect of temperature on solubility?

For many solids, solubility increases with temperature when dissolution is endothermic and may decrease when it is exothermic. Gas solubility in liquids commonly decreases as temperature rises. The actual relationship depends on the thermodynamics of the particular system.

Q9. What is intrinsic solubility?

Intrinsic solubility is the equilibrium solubility of the unionised form of an ionisable compound under specified conditions. Total or apparent solubility may be higher when the compound ionises, forms salts or complexes, or exists in other soluble forms.

Q10. How does pH affect the solubility of weak acids and weak bases?

Weak acids generally become more ionised and often more soluble as pH rises above their pKa. Weak bases generally become more ionised and often more soluble as pH falls below their pKa. The exact result also depends on salt formation, solid form, common ions and possible precipitation.

Q11. What is the distribution or partition coefficient?

The partition coefficient describes how a solute distributes between two immiscible phases at equilibrium. For the same molecular form, P = concentration in organic phase / concentration in aqueous phase. A logP value is often used to describe lipophilicity of the neutral form; logD includes all forms present at a stated pH.

Q12. What is the pharmaceutical significance of partition coefficient?

It helps estimate lipophilicity and can influence membrane permeation, drug absorption, distribution, extraction and formulation behaviour. However, it does not alone predict a drug’s performance; ionisation, molecular size, transporters, dissolution and biological factors also matter.

Q13. What is critical solution temperature (CST)?

Critical solution temperature is the temperature at which two partially miscible liquids become completely miscible in all proportions at the relevant pressure. A system with an upper CST becomes fully miscible above that temperature; a system with a lower CST becomes fully miscible below it. Some systems show both limits.

Q14. Give pharmaceutical applications of CST.

CST helps explain miscibility and phase behaviour of solvent mixtures used in extraction, liquid formulations and solvent selection. Temperature-dependent phase separation can affect product appearance, uniformity and stability, so the phase diagram and storage conditions may need consideration.

Q15. Define a buffer solution.

A buffer solution resists a large change in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. It commonly contains a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid.

Q16. Explain how an acidic buffer works.

In a weak-acid/conjugate-base buffer, the conjugate base consumes added hydrogen ions, while the weak acid consumes added hydroxide ions. For example, in an acetic acid/acetate buffer: acetate reacts with added H⁺ to form acetic acid; acetic acid reacts with OH⁻ to form acetate and water.

Q17. Write the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.

For a weak-acid buffer, pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). For a weak-base/conjugate-acid buffer, pOH = pKb + log([B]/[BH⁺]), followed by pH = pKw − pOH at the stated temperature. Concentrations are often approximated by activities in introductory calculations.

Q18. What factors affect buffer pH?

The pKa of the buffering pair, the ratio of conjugate base to weak acid, temperature, ionic strength and dilution can affect measured pH. In the ideal Henderson–Hasselbalch approximation, the ratio sets pH while the total concentration mainly affects capacity.

Q19. Define buffer capacity.

Buffer capacity (β) is the amount of strong acid or strong base required to change the pH of one litre of buffer by one pH unit. A common expression is β = dB/d(pH), where dB is the amount of strong base added per litre; sign conventions may differ. Capacity is generally greatest near pH = pKa and increases with total buffer concentration.

Q20. What factors influence buffer capacity?

Buffer capacity depends mainly on total buffer concentration and the ratio of the two buffer components. It is greatest when the weak acid and conjugate base are present in similar amounts, approximately at pH = pKa. Very dilute buffers have low capacity even if their pH is correctly adjusted.

Q21. What are pharmaceutical and biological applications of buffers?

Buffers help maintain pH in injections, eye drops, oral liquids, topical products, analytical reagents and biological systems. Formulation buffers must be selected carefully because excessive buffer capacity can irritate tissues or interfere with physiological pH adjustment.

Q22. How can pH be measured?

Electrometric measurement uses a pH meter with a suitable electrode system and calibration buffers. Colorimetric methods use indicators that change colour over a pH range. A calibrated pH meter is usually more precise for quantitative pharmaceutical work, while indicators are useful for approximate measurements or visual endpoints.

Q23. What is an indicator?

An acid–base indicator is a weak acid or weak base whose ionised and unionised forms have different colours. It changes colour over a characteristic pH transition range. The indicator should be chosen so that its transition range suits the intended measurement or titration endpoint.

Q24. Outline the general procedure for preparing a buffer.

Choose a suitable buffer pair with a pKa near the target pH; calculate the component ratio; weigh or measure the components; dissolve in part of the required solvent; adjust pH if needed; make up to final volume; mix and verify the pH using a calibrated meter. Use pharmaceutical-grade ingredients and the formulation’s validated instructions.

Q25. Solve a simple buffer pH example.

If a weak acid has pKa = 4.76 and the conjugate-base/acid concentration ratio is 1, then pH = 4.76 + log(1) = 4.76, because log(1) = 0. If the ratio is 10, pH = 4.76 + 1 = 5.76, under the Henderson–Hasselbalch approximation.

UNIT II — Interfacial Phenomena (8 hours)
Q1. What is an interface?

An interface is the boundary between two phases, such as liquid–air, liquid–liquid or solid–liquid. Molecules at an interface experience a different environment from molecules in the bulk phase, giving rise to interfacial energy and distinctive adsorption behaviour.

Q2. Define surface tension.

Surface tension is force per unit length acting along a liquid surface, or equivalently surface free energy per unit area for many simple systems. Its SI unit is N/m; it is often reported as mN/m. It arises because surface molecules experience unbalanced intermolecular forces.

Q3. Define interfacial tension.

Interfacial tension is the force per unit length or free energy per unit area at the boundary between two immiscible phases, such as oil and water. Lowering interfacial tension can help disperse one liquid in another, although stable emulsification also requires suitable interfacial films and formulation design.

Q4. What is surface free energy?

Surface free energy is the work required to create a unit area of new surface under specified conditions. It explains why many liquids tend to minimise surface area and why powders, droplets and bubbles can have significant surface-energy contributions.

Q5. Name methods for measuring surface or interfacial tension.

Common methods include capillary rise, drop weight or drop count, Du Noüy ring, Wilhelmy plate and pendant-drop methods. The appropriate method depends on sample properties, required accuracy, instrument availability and whether surface or interfacial tension is being measured.

Q6. What is the spreading coefficient?

For liquid 1 spreading over liquid 2 in the presence of air, a commonly used expression is S = γ₂ − (γ₁ + γ₁₂), where γ₂ is the surface tension of liquid 2, γ₁ is that of liquid 1, and γ₁₂ is the liquid–liquid interfacial tension. If S is positive, complete spreading is thermodynamically favoured in this simplified description.

Q7. What are surface-active agents (surfactants)?

Surfactants are molecules with both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions. They adsorb at interfaces and often reduce surface or interfacial tension. Depending on their type and concentration, they can act as wetting agents, detergents, emulsifiers, solubilisers or foaming agents.

Q8. Classify surfactants.

Surfactants are commonly classified as anionic, cationic, nonionic and amphoteric (zwitterionic). Their charge, compatibility, irritation potential and interaction with other ingredients differ, so the choice depends on route of administration and formulation purpose.

Q9. What is HLB?

HLB means hydrophilic–lipophilic balance. It is an empirical index used to help select surfactants for emulsification and related formulation tasks. In general, relatively higher-HLB surfactants are more hydrophilic and often favour oil-in-water emulsions; lower-HLB surfactants are more lipophilic and often favour water-in-oil systems. Required HLB and compatibility must also be considered.

Q10. Explain detergency.

Detergency is the removal of dirt or oily material from a surface. Surfactants can improve wetting, loosen soil, reduce interfacial tension and help disperse oily material in the wash liquid. Detergency depends on surfactant type, concentration, temperature, agitation and the nature of the soil and surface.

Q11. What are monolayers at liquid surfaces?

A monolayer is a single molecular layer of an adsorbed substance at a surface. Amphiphilic molecules may orient their hydrophilic groups toward water and hydrophobic groups toward air or oil. Surface pressure and area per molecule can be studied to understand film behaviour.

Q12. Define adsorption and distinguish it from absorption.

Adsorption is accumulation of molecules at a surface or interface. Absorption is penetration of a substance into the bulk of another material. Adsorption is a surface phenomenon, while absorption involves the bulk phase; both can occur in the same system.

Q13. What is activated charcoal, and why does it adsorb substances?

Activated charcoal is carbon processed to have a large internal surface area and porous structure. It can adsorb many molecules from liquids or gases. In medicine it is used in selected poisoning cases under professional guidance, but it does not bind every toxin and is not suitable for every exposure.

Q14. What is wetting?

Wetting describes how a liquid contacts and spreads over a solid surface. It is commonly assessed using the contact angle: a smaller contact angle generally indicates better wetting. Wetting is important for powder dispersion, tablet dissolution, granulation and suspension preparation.

Q15. Define contact angle.

The contact angle is the angle measured through the liquid at the line where solid, liquid and surrounding fluid meet. A low angle indicates stronger wetting; a high angle indicates poorer wetting. Surface roughness, contamination and measurement conditions affect the result.

Q16. What is adsorption of surfactants at an interface?

Surfactant molecules accumulate at a surface or interface with their hydrophilic and hydrophobic portions oriented according to the phases present. This adsorption can lower interfacial free energy and influence wetting, emulsification, foaming and solubilisation. Above the critical micelle concentration, additional surfactant increasingly forms micelles in bulk solution.

Q17. What is an electrical double layer?

A charged surface in a liquid attracts counter-ions and repels co-ions. The surface charge and surrounding ionic region form an electrical double layer. Its structure influences particle interactions, dispersion stability and electrophoretic behaviour.

Q18. What is zeta potential?

Zeta potential is the electrical potential at the slipping plane between a particle moving with its surrounding liquid layer and the bulk liquid. It is used as an indicator of electrostatic interactions in dispersions. A large magnitude can support electrostatic stabilisation in some systems, but stability also depends on steric effects, solvent, concentration and formulation.

Q19. How do electrolytes affect zeta potential and dispersion stability?

Electrolytes can compress the electrical double layer and reduce the magnitude of zeta potential. This may lower electrostatic repulsion and encourage aggregation or flocculation. The effect depends on ion concentration and valency; some systems are stabilised mainly by steric rather than electrostatic mechanisms.

UNIT III — Colloidal and Coarse Dispersions (12 hours)
Q1. What is a colloidal dispersion?

A colloidal dispersion contains one substance dispersed in another with particles typically in the approximate nanometre-to-submicrometre range, often described broadly as about 1 nm to 1 µm depending on the convention. Colloids may show distinctive optical, kinetic and electrical behaviour.

Q2. Classify colloidal systems by affinity for the dispersion medium.

Lyophilic colloids have strong affinity for the medium and are often relatively stable; lyophobic colloids have less affinity and may require stabilisation; association colloids form structures such as micelles above a suitable concentration. The terms depend on the solvent—for water, “hydrophilic” and “hydrophobic” are often used.

Q3. What are lyophilic colloids?

Lyophilic colloids have strong interactions with the dispersion medium. Examples include some polymer or gum solutions. They are often stabilised by solvation and can be comparatively reversible, but their behaviour still depends on pH, electrolytes, temperature and polymer properties.

Q4. What are lyophobic colloids?

Lyophobic colloids have relatively weak affinity for the dispersion medium and may be easily destabilised unless protected by charge or a stabilising layer. Examples include some sols of inorganic particles. They may be prepared by dispersion or condensation methods.

Q5. What are association colloids?

Association colloids form when amphiphilic molecules aggregate into structures such as micelles above a characteristic concentration called the critical micelle concentration (CMC). These structures can incorporate poorly water-soluble substances and are important in detergency and pharmaceutical solubilisation.

Q6. Explain the Tyndall effect.

The Tyndall effect is the scattering of light by dispersed particles, making the path of a light beam visible in a colloidal system. It can help distinguish many colloidal dispersions from true molecular solutions, though it is not a definitive identification method on its own.

Q7. Describe Brownian movement.

Brownian movement is the random motion of small particles caused by continual collisions with molecules of the surrounding medium. It can oppose sedimentation for sufficiently small particles and is one of the kinetic properties of colloids.

Q8. What are the electrical properties of colloids?

Colloidal particles can carry surface charge and form an electrical double layer. Related phenomena include electrophoresis (movement of particles in an electric field), electro-osmosis (movement of liquid relative to a charged surface), streaming potential and sedimentation potential.

Q9. How do particle size and shape affect colloids?

Size and shape affect scattering, Brownian movement, sedimentation, viscosity, surface area, adsorption and stability. Smaller particles generally have higher surface area per unit mass. Non-spherical particles may orient or flow differently from spherical particles.

Q10. What is peptization?

Peptization is conversion of a freshly formed precipitate into a colloidal dispersion by adding a small amount of a suitable electrolyte or peptizing agent. The agent helps particles acquire sufficient surface charge or stabilisation to remain dispersed.

Q11. What is protective action in colloids?

A protective colloid is a lyophilic material that adsorbs on lyophobic particles and helps prevent aggregation. Protection may result from a solvated layer and steric repulsion. Examples in formulation science include selected polymers and gums, chosen for compatibility and intended use.

Q12. Give pharmaceutical applications of colloidal systems.

Colloidal systems are used in suspensions, emulsions, gels, sols, micellar solubilisation, some drug-delivery systems and diagnostic products. Their performance depends on particle size, surface chemistry, viscosity, stability, route of administration and product quality requirements.

Q13. What is a coarse dispersion?

A coarse dispersion contains relatively large dispersed particles or droplets, as in many suspensions and emulsions. Such systems can separate under gravity and require suitable particle-size control, viscosity, interfacial stabilisation and storage instructions.

Q14. Define suspension and emulsion.

A suspension is a dispersion of insoluble solid particles in a liquid. An emulsion is a dispersion of one liquid as droplets in another immiscible liquid, usually stabilised by an emulsifying agent or suitable interfacial film.

Q15. State Stokes’ law for sedimentation.

For a small spherical particle settling slowly in a dilute Newtonian fluid under laminar conditions, the terminal settling velocity is v = d²(ρp − ρm)g / (18η), where d is particle diameter, ρp particle density, ρm medium density, g gravitational acceleration and η viscosity. The assumptions matter; concentrated or non-spherical systems may not follow this equation.

Q16. What factors affect sedimentation rate?

According to Stokes’ law, sedimentation velocity increases with the square of particle diameter and density difference, and decreases with medium viscosity. Particle shape, concentration, aggregation, Brownian movement, flocculation and non-Newtonian flow can also affect real pharmaceutical suspensions.

Q17. What is flocculation?

Flocculation is the loose aggregation of dispersed particles into clusters called flocs. Flocculated suspensions often sediment faster but may form a loose sediment that is easier to redisperse. Controlled flocculation aims to avoid a hard cake while maintaining acceptable appearance and dose uniformity.

Q18. What is deflocculation?

In a deflocculated suspension, particles remain more separate and may sediment slowly, but they can pack closely and form a compact, difficult-to-redisperse cake. The best state depends on the formulation; controlled flocculation is often used to improve redispersibility.

Q19. What is controlled flocculation?

Controlled flocculation is deliberate adjustment of particle interactions using suitable electrolytes, polymers or surfactants to produce loose, redispersible flocs. The goal is a physically stable suspension with acceptable sedimentation, redispersion and dose uniformity.

Q20. What is the DLVO theory?

DLVO theory explains the stability of many colloidal dispersions as a balance between attractive van der Waals forces and repulsive electrical double-layer forces. The energy barrier affects whether particles remain separated or aggregate. Steric, hydration and other non-DLVO forces can also be important.

Q21. What is the difference between monomolecular and multimolecular adsorption?

Monomolecular adsorption forms approximately one molecular layer on a surface, while multimolecular adsorption involves multiple layers or more complex accumulation. The actual adsorption model depends on the material, concentration, surface and experimental conditions; not all pharmaceutical adsorption follows a simple multilayer model.

Q22. Explain film formation in emulsion stabilisation.

An emulsifier can form an interfacial film around droplets. A strong, coherent film can resist droplet contact, coalescence and rupture. Film properties depend on emulsifier type, concentration, interfacial packing, temperature and other formulation ingredients.

Q23. What is solid-particle adsorption in emulsions?

Fine solid particles can adsorb at an oil–water interface and stabilise droplets by forming a physical barrier. This is known as Pickering stabilisation. Particle wettability, size, shape and concentration affect whether an oil-in-water or water-in-oil emulsion is favoured.

Q24. Name the physical instabilities of emulsions.

Important physical instabilities include creaming, flocculation, coalescence, cracking or breaking, and phase inversion. Some changes may be reversible, while cracking is generally irreversible without reformulation.

Q25. Distinguish creaming, coalescence and cracking.

Creaming is upward or downward movement of droplets due to density differences without droplets merging. Coalescence is merging of droplets into larger droplets. Cracking/breaking is major irreversible separation of the emulsion into bulk phases.

Q26. What is phase inversion?

Phase inversion occurs when an oil-in-water emulsion changes to water-in-oil, or vice versa. It can be triggered by changes in phase-volume ratio, temperature, electrolyte concentration or emulsifier properties. It can dramatically alter product consistency and performance.

Q27. How can suspensions and emulsions be stabilised?

Possible measures include selecting suitable particle or droplet size, using compatible wetting agents or emulsifiers, adjusting viscosity, controlling flocculation, choosing suitable polymers, controlling pH and electrolytes, and using appropriate manufacturing and storage conditions. Stability should be tested over the intended shelf life.

UNIT IV — Rheological Studies (8 hours)
Q1. Define rheology.

Rheology is the study of how materials deform and flow when forces are applied. In pharmacy it helps evaluate liquids, suspensions, emulsions, creams, gels, ointments and powders during manufacture, dispensing and use.

Q2. Define viscosity.

Viscosity is resistance to flow. Dynamic viscosity is commonly represented by η and has SI units of Pa·s. Kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by density and has SI units of m²/s.

Q3. Define shear stress and shear rate.

Shear stress is tangential force per unit area, commonly measured in Pa. Shear rate describes the velocity gradient between adjacent fluid layers and is measured in s⁻¹. The relationship between shear stress and shear rate defines the flow behaviour of a material.

Q4. What is a Newtonian system?

A Newtonian fluid has constant viscosity at a given temperature and pressure, independent of shear rate. It follows τ = η γ̇, where τ is shear stress, η is viscosity and γ̇ is shear rate. Examples include water and many simple low-molecular-weight liquids under ordinary conditions.

Q5. What are non-Newtonian systems?

Non-Newtonian materials do not show a constant ratio of shear stress to shear rate under all conditions. Their apparent viscosity may change with shear rate or time. Many pharmaceutical suspensions, emulsions, polymer solutions, creams and gels show non-Newtonian behaviour.

Q6. Explain pseudoplastic flow.

Pseudoplastic or shear-thinning materials show decreasing apparent viscosity as shear rate increases. Polymer solutions and many structured dispersions show this behaviour. It can help a cream or suspension flow during application or pouring while remaining thicker at rest.

Q7. Explain dilatant flow.

Dilatant or shear-thickening materials show increasing apparent viscosity as shear rate increases. It can occur in highly concentrated suspensions of solid particles. Strong shear can make the material harder to stir or pump.

Q8. What is plastic flow and yield value?

A plastic material requires a minimum stress, called yield stress or yield value, before sustained flow begins. Above that value, the material flows. Yield stress can help a suspension resist settling or an ointment retain its shape, but excessive yield stress can make dispensing difficult.

Q9. What is thixotropy?

Thixotropy is time-dependent shear thinning: under constant shear, viscosity or structure decreases with time, and it rebuilds gradually after the shear is removed. It is useful in products that should flow when shaken or spread but recover structure during storage.

Q10. What is negative thixotropy (rheopexy)?

Negative thixotropy, often called rheopexy, describes a time-dependent increase in viscosity or structure during continued shear under specified conditions. It is less common than thixotropy. Terminology and measurement protocols should be stated carefully because time-dependent flow behaviour can be complex.

Q11. What is a thixotropic loop?

When shear stress is plotted against shear rate during an increasing and then decreasing shear cycle, a hysteresis loop may appear because the material structure changes with time. The loop area can provide a comparative indication of thixotropic breakdown and recovery, provided the test protocol is controlled.

Q12. What are bulges and spurs in rheological curves?

Bulges or spurs are irregular features that may appear on rheograms of complex, time-dependent materials during a shear cycle. They can reflect structural breakdown and rebuilding, delayed response or measurement artefacts. Their interpretation requires the instrument geometry, test conditions and sample history.

Q13. Name instruments used to measure viscosity.

Common instruments include capillary viscometers, falling-ball viscometers, rotational viscometers and rheometers. Single-point instruments measure under a particular condition, while multi-point instruments measure at several shear rates and are more useful for characterising non-Newtonian flow.

Q14. Distinguish single-point and multi-point viscosity measurements.

A single-point measurement gives apparent viscosity at one specified shear condition. A multi-point test measures stress or viscosity across a range of shear rates, helping identify Newtonian, shear-thinning, shear-thickening or yield-stress behaviour. Results must report temperature, geometry, speed/shear rate and sample preparation.

Q15. What is viscoelasticity?

Viscoelastic materials show both viscous flow and elastic recovery. Their response depends on time, deformation rate and the balance between energy dissipation and storage. Many gels, polymer systems, creams and biological materials are viscoelastic.

Q16. What is psycho-rheology?

Psycho-rheology studies how rheological properties relate to human sensory perception, such as thickness, creaminess, spreadability, stickiness and mouthfeel. It connects instrument measurements with how a patient or consumer experiences a product.

Q17. State applications of rheology in pharmacy.

Rheology is used to optimise mixing, pumping, filling, coating, suspension stability, cream spreadability, gel extrusion, syringeability, pourability, patient acceptability and quality control. It can help ensure a product behaves consistently during manufacture and use.

UNIT V — Micromeritics (8 hours)
Q1. Define micromeritics.

Micromeritics is the science of small particles and the properties of particulate systems, including particle size, size distribution, shape, surface area, porosity, density and powder flow. These properties affect manufacturing and dosage-form performance.

Q2. Why is particle size important in pharmacy?

Particle size can influence dissolution rate, content uniformity, suspension stability, absorption, sedimentation, powder flow, mixing, filtration and product appearance. Smaller particles often dissolve faster because they provide greater surface area, but they may also agglomerate or flow poorly.

Q3. What is particle-size distribution?

Particle-size distribution describes the range and relative proportions of particle sizes in a sample. A powder may be described by number-, length-, area- or volume-based distributions. The basis must be stated because these distributions can differ substantially.

Q4. Name methods for measuring particle size.

Methods include sieving, optical microscopy, sedimentation, laser diffraction, dynamic light scattering for suitable small particles, and electrical sensing-zone methods. Each method measures a particular equivalent size and has limitations related to shape, concentration and sample preparation.

Q5. Explain sieve analysis.

Sieve analysis passes a powder through a stack of sieves with progressively smaller openings. The mass retained on each sieve is measured to estimate the size distribution. It is simple and useful for many relatively coarse powders but less suitable for very fine, cohesive or irregularly shaped particles.

Q6. What is particle shape, and why does it matter?

Particle shape describes morphology, such as spherical, needle-like, plate-like, fibrous or irregular. Shape affects packing, surface area, flow, friction, compaction, dissolution and filtration. Two powders with the same nominal size can behave differently if their shapes differ.

Q7. Define specific surface area.

Specific surface area is the total surface area per unit mass or volume of a material, often expressed as m²/g. For ideal non-porous spherical particles of uniform diameter, specific surface area per mass increases as particle diameter decreases. Real powders may have roughness and internal porosity.

Q8. Name methods for determining surface area.

Methods include gas adsorption (for example, BET analysis), air-permeability methods and calculations from particle-size and density data under simplifying assumptions. Gas adsorption can measure accessible internal and external surface area, while permeability methods infer surface area from resistance to fluid flow.

Q9. What is bulk density?

Bulk density is the mass of a powder divided by the bulk volume it occupies, including the spaces between particles: bulk density = mass / unsettled bulk volume. It depends on particle packing and the measurement method.

Q10. What is tapped density?

Tapped density is the mass of a powder divided by the volume after a specified tapping procedure has reduced the void spaces. It is generally equal to or higher than bulk density. The procedure and endpoint should follow the applicable compendial method.

Q11. Define Carr’s compressibility index.

Carr’s index estimates powder compressibility from bulk and tapped density: Carr’s index (%) = [(tapped density − bulk density) / tapped density] × 100. Lower values often suggest better flow, while higher values suggest greater packing change and possible poorer flow. Interpretation depends on the powder and test method.

Q12. Define Hausner ratio.

Hausner ratio is tapped density / bulk density. A ratio close to 1 generally indicates less change during tapping and often better flow, while a larger ratio suggests greater cohesiveness or compressibility. It is an indirect flow indicator, not a complete prediction of behaviour in every machine.

Q13. What are powder-flow properties?

Powder flow describes how particles move relative to one another and through equipment. It depends on particle size and shape, surface roughness, moisture, electrostatic charge, density, cohesion, and environmental conditions. Poor flow can cause inconsistent die filling and content variation.

Q14. How is angle of repose used to assess powder flow?

A powder is allowed to form a cone, and the angle between the cone surface and horizontal is measured. For a simple cone, tan θ = height / radius. A smaller angle often suggests easier flow, but funnel geometry, method and powder cohesion affect the result.

Q15. How can powder flow be improved?

Possible methods include optimising particle-size distribution, using suitable granulation, reducing excessive fines, controlling moisture and electrostatic charge, improving particle shape, adding compatible glidants, and modifying equipment or process conditions. Any excipient or process change must be validated for the formulation.

Q16. What is a powder-flow tester?

A powder-flow tester is an instrument that characterises flow under defined conditions. Depending on the instrument, it may measure flow through an orifice, angle of repose, shear-cell properties, bulk/tapped density or dynamic flow energy. Advanced testing can assess how flow changes with consolidation, aeration and equipment conditions.

Q17. What is porosity?

Porosity is the fraction or percentage of a material’s bulk volume occupied by void spaces. It influences powder packing, liquid penetration, compaction, dissolution and tablet properties. Porosity can be estimated from bulk and true density when the assumptions are suitable.

Q18. Give pharmaceutical applications of micromeritics.

Micromeritics supports powder blending, granulation, tablet compression, capsule filling, suspension design, inhalation products, dissolution testing, filtration, packaging and quality control. Particle attributes are often critical material attributes that must be controlled to achieve reproducible products.
