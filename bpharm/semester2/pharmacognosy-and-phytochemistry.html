UNIT I — Metabolic Pathways and Biogenetic Studies (10 hours)
Q1. Define pharmacognosy and phytochemistry.

Pharmacognosy is the study of crude drugs and useful substances obtained from natural sources, including plants, animals, microbes and minerals. Phytochemistry is the study of chemical compounds produced by plants, including their isolation, structure, properties and biological activity.

Q2. What are primary and secondary metabolites?

Primary metabolites are directly involved in growth, development and reproduction, such as sugars, amino acids, fatty acids and nucleotides. Secondary metabolites are not directly required for basic growth in every condition but help with defence, signalling and ecological interactions. Examples include alkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, tannins and glycosides.

Q3. What is a biosynthetic or biogenetic pathway?

A biosynthetic pathway is a series of enzyme-controlled reactions that converts a starting substance into one or more products in a living organism. Biogenetic studies investigate how natural products are formed and which precursors contribute atoms or structural units to them.

Q4. Explain the shikimic acid pathway and its importance.

The shikimate pathway converts central carbohydrate-metabolism intermediates into chorismate, a precursor of the aromatic amino acids phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan in plants and many microorganisms. These amino acids feed the biosynthesis of several natural products, including phenylpropanoids, many alkaloids and other aromatic compounds. Animals do not possess this pathway.

Q5. What is the acetate pathway?

The acetate pathway refers broadly to natural-product biosynthesis from acetate-derived units, commonly through acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA. Repeated condensation of acetate/malonate units can form polyketide chains that cyclise or undergo modification to give compounds such as some phenolics, anthraquinones and other secondary metabolites.

Q6. How do amino acid pathways contribute to secondary metabolites?

Amino acids act as precursors for many nitrogen-containing natural products. For example, tryptophan contributes to indole alkaloids, tyrosine and phenylalanine contribute to several alkaloids and phenolic compounds, and ornithine or lysine can contribute to particular alkaloid groups. Enzymes modify these precursors through reactions such as decarboxylation, oxidation, methylation and coupling.

Q7. Give examples linking pathways to natural-product classes.

The shikimate pathway supplies aromatic amino acids and many phenylpropanoid precursors; acetate/malonate metabolism contributes to polyketides; amino acids contribute to many alkaloids. Terpenoids are mainly assembled from the isoprene units IPP and DMAPP, generated through the mevalonate and/or methylerythritol phosphate pathways, depending on the organism and compartment.

Q8. What is the use of radioactive isotopes in biogenetic studies?

A labelled precursor containing a radioactive isotope can be supplied to a biological system, and researchers can track where the label appears in the final natural product. This helps identify precursor–product relationships and possible pathways. Such studies require specialised facilities, radiation controls and authorised procedures.

Q9. What is an isotope tracer?

An isotope tracer is an atom of an element that can be followed through a biological or chemical process because it has a detectable isotope label. Radioactive labels can be detected through their emissions; stable isotopes such as carbon-13 can be followed using methods such as mass spectrometry or NMR.

Q10. What are pathway-prediction tools?

Pathway-prediction tools use information about genes, enzymes, known reactions and chemical structures to suggest possible biosynthetic routes. They help researchers form hypotheses, prioritise experiments and connect genomic data with metabolite data. Predictions need experimental confirmation.

Q11. What is CRISPR/Cas9, and how can it support natural-product research?

CRISPR/Cas9 is a genome-editing system guided by RNA that can target a selected DNA sequence. In properly authorised research, it can help investigate the function of biosynthetic genes or modify a pathway to study changes in metabolite production. It is a research tool, not a direct identification test for a crude drug.

Q12. Why are biogenetic studies important in pharmacy?

They help explain how medicinal natural products are formed, identify possible biosynthetic enzymes and precursors, support sustainable production, guide metabolic engineering and help discover related compounds. Understanding pathways can also explain variation in constituent levels among species, tissues and growing conditions.

UNIT II — Primary Metabolites and Their Pharmacognostic Study (12 hours)
Q1. What are primary metabolites used as crude drugs?

They are naturally derived substances such as carbohydrates, proteins, enzymes, fats, fixed oils and waxes. They may be used as excipients, demulcents, emollients, nutritional ingredients or sources of specialised preparations. Their quality depends on source, processing, purity and intended use.

Q2. What is acacia? Mention its source and uses.

Acacia gum is a dried gummy exudate obtained from certain Acacia/Senegalia species, commonly including Senegalia senegal. It contains complex polysaccharides and glycoprotein fractions. It is used as an emulsifying and suspending agent, binder and demulcent in suitable pharmaceutical formulations and in food products.

Q3. What is agar?

Agar is a dried gelatinous substance obtained from certain red algae, especially species of Gelidium and Gracilaria. Its main polysaccharide fractions include agarose and agaropectin. It forms gels and is used as a culture medium, thickener and stabilising or suspending material in selected applications.

Q4. What is tragacanth?

Tragacanth is a dried gummy exudate from certain Astragalus species. It contains water-swellable polysaccharides and forms a viscous mucilage. It has been used as a suspending agent, thickener, emulsifying aid and pharmaceutical excipient.

Q5. Describe honey as a natural product.

Honey is a sugar-rich substance made by honeybees from floral nectar or related plant secretions. It mainly contains fructose and glucose, with water, minor sugars, organic acids, enzymes and trace constituents. It is used as a sweetening agent and in some topical products; medical-grade honey is distinct from ordinary food honey and must meet specific quality requirements.

Q6. What is gelatin? State its source and uses.

Gelatin is a protein mixture produced by partial hydrolysis of collagen from animal connective tissues. It can form gels and films. Pharmaceutical uses include capsules, some tablet coatings, stabilisation and selected haemostatic materials. Its source and processing are important for quality and religious or dietary acceptability.

Q7. What is casein?

Casein is the major family of phosphoproteins in milk. It can be separated from milk by controlled acidification or other suitable methods. It has nutritional and food applications and has been used in some pharmaceutical or formulation contexts, although its suitability depends on the product and the possibility of milk-protein allergy.

Q8. What are proteolytic enzymes?

Proteolytic enzymes (proteases) catalyse the breakdown of proteins by hydrolysing peptide bonds. They have different substrate preferences and conditions for activity. Examples in the syllabus include papain, bromelain, serratiopeptidase, urokinase, streptokinase and pepsin; their origins and clinical roles are not identical.

Q9. Give the source and uses of papain and bromelain.

Papain is a protease obtained from the latex of papaya (Carica papaya). Bromelain is a mixture of proteolytic enzymes obtained from pineapple (Ananas comosus). They are used in selected food, research and industrial applications; medicinal claims and topical uses depend on the specific approved preparation and evidence.

Q10. Distinguish serratiopeptidase, urokinase and streptokinase.

Serratiopeptidase is a proteolytic enzyme preparation historically promoted for anti-inflammatory purposes, but evidence and regulatory acceptance vary. Urokinase is a human enzyme that activates plasminogen to plasmin. Streptokinase is a bacterial protein that forms an activating complex with plasminogen. Urokinase and streptokinase are fibrinolytic agents used in selected clinical settings under professional supervision, not interchangeable over-the-counter enzymes.

Q11. What is pepsin?

Pepsin is an acidic protease produced in the stomach from pepsinogen. It begins protein digestion by breaking peptide bonds, particularly under acidic gastric conditions. Pharmaceutical or diagnostic use requires a defined preparation and suitable quality control.

Q12. What is castor oil?

Castor oil is a fixed oil obtained from seeds of Ricinus communis. It is rich in ricinoleic-acid-containing triglycerides. It has been used as a stimulant laxative in appropriate circumstances and as an ingredient in some topical or industrial formulations. Castor seeds contain the highly toxic protein ricin; crude seeds are not safe to consume.

Q13. Describe olive oil and its uses.

Olive oil is a fixed oil obtained from the fruit of Olea europaea. It is rich in oleic acid and also contains minor constituents such as tocopherols and phenolics, depending on grade and processing. It is used as a food oil and in selected emollient or formulation applications.

Q14. What is cocoa butter?

Cocoa butter is the fat obtained from seeds of Theobroma cacao. It contains triglycerides that give it a melting range close to body temperature. It has traditionally been used as a suppository base and in creams and cosmetic products, although polymorphism and softening can affect formulation performance.

Q15. What is wool fat (lanolin)?

Wool fat, or lanolin, is a purified wax-like material recovered from sheep's wool grease. It contains wax esters, sterol esters and related lipids. It acts as an emollient and water-absorbing base in topical preparations. Sensitivity can occur in some people, so product quality and patient history matter.

Q16. What is beeswax?

Beeswax is a wax produced by honeybees. It contains long-chain wax esters and other lipids. It is used to increase consistency and structure in ointments, creams, balms and other formulations.

Q17. What are identification tests for primary metabolites?

Tests are selected according to the substance: iodine can indicate starch; Benedict's or Fehling's reagent can detect reducing sugars; Biuret reagent detects peptide bonds; ninhydrin can indicate many free amino acids; Sudan dyes can stain lipids. These are preliminary tests and do not replace validated identity, purity and assay methods.

Q18. What details are included in a pharmacognostic study?

A pharmacognostic profile usually includes biological source and accepted name, family, geographical distribution, cultivation or collection, macroscopical and microscopical characters, chemical constituents, identification tests, therapeutic uses, adulterants, quality standards and commercial applications.

UNIT III — Secondary Metabolites and Crude Drugs (12 hours)
Q1. Classify important secondary metabolites.

Important groups include alkaloids, volatile oils, tannins, resins, glycosides, phenylpropanoids, flavonoids, iridoids, other terpenoids and naphthoquinones. Classification can be based on chemical structure, biosynthetic origin or major pharmacological properties.

Q2. What are alkaloids? Give general characteristics.

Alkaloids are a diverse group of naturally occurring nitrogen-containing compounds, many of which are basic and physiologically active. Many are derived from amino acids, but not every nitrogen-containing natural product is classified as an alkaloid. Examples in this syllabus include constituents from Vinca, Rauwolfia and opium.

Q3. Write a pharmacognostic note on Vinca.

Vinca refers in this context mainly to Catharanthus roseus (Madagascar periwinkle), a plant in the Apocynaceae family. Its indole alkaloids include vincristine and vinblastine, important anticancer medicines that interfere with microtubule function. These are potent prescription medicines with serious toxicities and are used only under specialist care.

Q4. Write a pharmacognostic note on Rauwolfia.

Rauvolfia serpentina is a medicinal plant in the Apocynaceae family; its roots contain indole alkaloids, including reserpine. Reserpine affects monoamine storage and has been used as an antihypertensive and antipsychotic, but it can cause important adverse effects and is not a casual self-treatment. Correct botanical identity and alkaloid content are key quality considerations.

Q5. What is opium? Mention its important constituents.

Opium is the dried latex obtained from the unripe capsules of the opium poppy, Papaver somniferum. It contains alkaloids such as morphine, codeine, thebaine, papaverine and noscapine. Some are controlled substances and can cause dependence, respiratory depression or other serious harm; this is a pharmacognosy study topic, not a recommendation for unsupervised use.

Q6. What are volatile oils?

Volatile oils, also called essential oils, are aromatic mixtures that evaporate readily and are commonly obtained from plant materials by distillation or other suitable methods. Many contain terpenes, terpenoids or phenylpropanoids. Their composition can vary with species, plant part, climate, harvest and processing.

Q7. Give the source and major constituents of lemongrass oil.

Lemongrass oil is commonly obtained from leaves of Cymbopogon species, especially C. flexuosus or C. citratus. Citral (a mixture of geranial and neral) is a major constituent in many grades. It is used mainly in fragrance, flavour and related products; concentrated oil can irritate skin and must be used appropriately.

Q8. Give the source and major constituents of clove.

Clove consists of dried flower buds of Syzygium aromaticum (family Myrtaceae). Its volatile oil is rich in eugenol, with other constituents such as eugenyl acetate and beta-caryophyllene. Clove oil has flavouring and traditional dental uses, but concentrated oil can irritate tissues and may be toxic if swallowed in excessive amounts.

Q9. Give the source and major constituents of cinnamon.

Cinnamon bark is obtained from species of Cinnamomum (family Lauraceae), with the exact species depending on the commercial type. Cinnamaldehyde is a major constituent of many bark oils; eugenol may be prominent in some leaf oils. Species identity matters because composition and coumarin content can differ.

Q10. Give the source and major constituents of fennel.

Fennel consists of the dried ripe fruits of Foeniculum vulgare (family Apiaceae). Its volatile oil commonly contains anethole and fenchone, with proportions depending on variety and source. It is used as a flavouring and in traditional carminative preparations.

Q11. What are tannins? Name the syllabus examples.

Tannins are polyphenolic compounds that bind and precipitate proteins. They are often grouped as hydrolysable tannins and condensed tannins. The syllabus examples are myrobalans, catechu and pomegranate. Their astringent effect is related to protein binding, but excessive use can affect nutrient or drug absorption.

Q12. What are myrobalans, catechu and pomegranate used to illustrate?

They are natural sources rich in tannins or related polyphenols. Myrobalans commonly refers to fruits of Terminalia species; catechu is obtained from plant material such as Acacia catechu; pomegranate rind from Punica granatum contains tannins and other phenolics. Exact constituents vary by species, plant part and preparation.

Q13. What are resins? Give examples from the syllabus.

Resins are complex, often amorphous plant exudates or extracts that are generally insoluble in water but soluble in certain organic solvents. Guggul is an oleo-gum-resin obtained from Commiphora wightii; asafoetida is an oleo-gum-resin from Ferula species. Their composition includes resinous components and volatile or gum fractions, depending on the drug.

Q14. What are glycosides?

Glycosides contain a sugar portion (glycone) linked to a non-sugar portion (aglycone or genin). Hydrolysis can separate these parts. The properties and biological activity often depend strongly on the aglycone. Examples listed here include senna, liquorice and digitalis, although their major active constituents belong to different chemical subgroups.

Q15. Write a note on senna.

Senna consists of leaflets and/or fruits of Senna alexandrina (older names include Cassia angustifolia and C. acutifolia). Its stimulant-laxative constituents include sennosides, which are anthraquinone-related glycosides. Senna is used for short-term constipation in suitable patients; prolonged or inappropriate use can cause diarrhoea and electrolyte disturbances.

Q16. Write a note on liquorice.

Liquorice root is obtained from Glycyrrhiza glabra and related accepted sources. Glycyrrhizin (glycyrrhizic acid) and flavonoids are important constituents. It is used as a flavouring and in certain traditional preparations. Excessive glycyrrhizin intake can cause sodium retention, low potassium and elevated blood pressure, and may interact with medicines.

Q17. Write a note on digitalis.

Digitalis drugs are obtained from leaves of certain Digitalis species and contain cardiac glycosides, including digoxin-related compounds depending on the species and preparation. These compounds affect the sodium–potassium ATPase and cardiac function. They have a narrow therapeutic index, so correct standardisation and medical monitoring are essential.

Q18. What are phenylpropanoids and flavonoids? Give the listed examples.

Phenylpropanoids are natural products with a C₆–C₃-derived framework, while flavonoids have a characteristic C₆–C₃–C₆ skeleton. Green tea contains catechins and other polyphenols; Ginkgo leaves contain flavonoids and terpene lactones; flaxseed contains lignans and fixed oil. These drugs have different constituents and should not be treated as chemically interchangeable.

Q19. What are iridoids and other terpenoids? Give the examples listed.

Iridoids are a class of monoterpenoid-derived natural products often found as glycosides. Gentian root contains bitter secoiridoid glycosides that contribute to its bitter taste and traditional digestive-stimulant use. Artemisia species contain varied terpenoids; some species contain artemisinin-related compounds, but species and plant parts must be identified accurately.

Q20. What are naphthoquinones?

Naphthoquinones are compounds containing a quinone system fused to a naphthalene-like ring framework. They can have biological activity, and some can be irritant or toxic. Identification requires appropriate chemical or chromatographic evidence rather than appearance alone.

Q21. Name general identification tests for secondary metabolites.

Preliminary tests include Dragendorff's or Mayer's reagents for many alkaloids; ferric chloride for some phenolics and tannins; foam tests for saponins; Shinoda or aluminium chloride-based tests for certain flavonoids; and suitable tests for glycosides or terpenoids. These tests are presumptive and can give false positives, so chromatographic or spectroscopic confirmation may be required.

Q22. What information should a crude-drug profile include?

It should describe accepted biological source and family, geographical distribution, cultivation or collection of the source, part used, macroscopical and microscopical characters, chemical constituents, specific identification tests, therapeutic uses, commercial applications, adulterants and relevant quality standards.

UNIT IV — Extraction Methods for Medicinal Plants (12 hours)
Q1. What is extraction in pharmacognosy?

Extraction is the process of transferring desired constituents from plant material into a suitable solvent or fluid. The method depends on the plant matrix, target compounds, solvent polarity, heat sensitivity, particle size, moisture, time and intended product.

Q2. What is an infusion?

Infusion is a simple method in which a suitable solvent, commonly hot or cold water, is brought into contact with a plant material for a limited period and then separated. It is generally used for softer plant parts and constituents that can be extracted without prolonged boiling.

Q3. What is decoction?

Decoction involves boiling or simmering plant material in water for a specified time, then separating the liquid. It is commonly suited to harder materials such as roots or bark and to water-soluble, heat-stable constituents. It is unsuitable for compounds that degrade readily with heat.

Q4. Explain digestion.

Digestion is maceration carried out with gentle heat to improve extraction rate. It can help when mild warming increases solubility or diffusion, but the temperature must be controlled to avoid degrading heat-sensitive constituents.

Q5. What is maceration?

In maceration, suitably prepared plant material is soaked in a solvent for a defined period, often with occasional mixing. The liquid is then separated and the marc may be pressed or re-extracted. It is simple but can be time-consuming and may be less efficient than methods with continuous solvent renewal.

Q6. Explain percolation.

Percolation passes solvent slowly through a packed bed of prepared plant material in a percolator. Fresh solvent contacts the material as the extract drains, supporting mass transfer. Correct moistening, packing, flow rate and particle size are important to avoid channeling or incomplete extraction.

Q7. What is reflux extraction?

Reflux extraction heats a solvent to boiling while a condenser returns solvent vapour to the vessel. It allows extraction at an elevated, relatively constant solvent temperature with less solvent loss. It is not suitable for all heat-sensitive substances or flammable solvents without appropriate equipment and controls.

Q8. Explain distillation in medicinal-plant processing.

Distillation separates volatile components based on volatility. Steam or hydrodistillation is commonly used to obtain essential oils from suitable plant materials. The vapour is condensed and the oil is separated from the aqueous distillate. It is different from extracting non-volatile compounds into a solvent.

Q9. What is Soxhlet extraction?

Soxhlet extraction repeatedly washes a solid sample with condensed hot solvent in an apparatus that siphons the extract back to the boiling flask. It can be efficient for exhaustive extraction of suitable compounds, but repeated heating may damage thermolabile constituents and the method can consume time and energy.

Q10. What is successive solvent extraction?

Successive solvent extraction uses a series of solvents, often arranged from lower to higher polarity or selected by target chemistry, on the same plant material. This separates broad groups of constituents into different fractions. Solvent choice and sequence influence the composition of each fraction.

Q11. What is supercritical-fluid extraction?

Supercritical-fluid extraction uses a fluid above its critical temperature and pressure, commonly carbon dioxide, where it has useful gas-like diffusivity and liquid-like solvating power. Pressure and co-solvents can tune selectivity. It can reduce organic-solvent residues, but equipment is costly and polar compounds may need a modifier.

Q12. Explain microwave-assisted extraction.

Microwave-assisted extraction uses microwave energy to heat suitable solvent and plant moisture, which can speed matrix disruption and mass transfer. It may reduce extraction time and solvent use. Temperature, power and vessel design require control to prevent degradation or pressure hazards.

Q13. Explain ultrasonic-assisted extraction.

Ultrasonic-assisted extraction uses sound waves to produce cavitation in a liquid. Bubble formation and collapse can improve solvent penetration and release constituents from plant tissue. It may shorten extraction time, but performance depends on frequency, power, solvent, temperature and sample properties.

Q14. What is enzyme-assisted extraction?

Enzyme-assisted extraction uses enzymes such as cellulases or pectinases to break down parts of the plant cell wall and improve release of target compounds. It can operate under relatively mild conditions, but enzyme specificity, pH, temperature and cost need consideration.

Q15. What is pressurized-liquid extraction?

Pressurized-liquid extraction uses a solvent at elevated temperature and pressure to keep it liquid and improve penetration and solubility. It can provide rapid and reproducible extraction with lower solvent volumes, but requires specialised equipment and optimisation for the target compounds.

Q16. Compare conventional and modern extraction methods.

Conventional methods such as maceration, infusion, decoction and percolation are often simpler and less expensive but may take longer or use more solvent. Modern methods such as supercritical-fluid, microwave, ultrasound, enzyme-assisted and pressurised-liquid extraction can improve speed, selectivity or solvent efficiency, but may need specialised equipment and method validation.

Q17. What factors affect extraction efficiency?

Important factors are solvent type and polarity, particle size, solvent-to-drug ratio, temperature, time, agitation, pH, moisture, pressure (where relevant), plant age and storage. A good method maximises recovery of the desired constituents while limiting degradation and unwanted impurities.

UNIT V — Isolation, Identification and Characterization Techniques (14 hours)
Q1. Distinguish extraction, isolation, identification and characterization.

Extraction transfers constituents from plant material into a solvent. Isolation separates a target compound from a mixture. Identification determines what the compound is, usually by comparison with reliable references. Characterization establishes its chemical and physical properties and structure using complementary evidence.

Q2. What is chromatography?

Chromatography separates mixture components through their different distributions between a stationary phase and a mobile phase. Differences in adsorption, partitioning, polarity, size or other interactions cause compounds to move at different rates.

Q3. What is planar chromatography?

Planar chromatography separates compounds on a flat stationary phase, commonly a thin layer of adsorbent coated on a plate or paper. The mobile phase travels across the surface by capillary action. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) is a common planar technique used for screening and identification.

Q4. Explain the principle of TLC.

In thin-layer chromatography, a small sample spot is placed near the base of a coated plate. A suitable solvent rises through the stationary phase and carries compounds at different rates. The separated spots are visualised under suitable light or with a detection reagent. Results depend on the stationary phase, solvent system and sample.

Q5. Define the retention factor (Rf) in TLC.

Rf = distance travelled by the centre of the compound spot from the origin ÷ distance travelled by the solvent front from the origin. Under specified conditions Rf is between 0 and 1. It is not a universal constant: solvent composition, plate, temperature and other conditions affect it, so comparisons require matched conditions.

Q6. What is column chromatography?

Column chromatography packs a stationary phase into a column and passes a mobile phase through it. Components separate because they interact differently with the stationary phase and solvent. Fractions are collected as compounds elute and can be analysed to identify the desired fraction.

Q7. What is preparative TLC?

Preparative TLC uses a thicker stationary-phase layer and a larger sample to separate compounds for recovery. The band containing the desired compound is located, removed from the plate and eluted with a suitable solvent. Recovery and purity depend on loading, resolution and handling.

Q8. Explain flash chromatography.

Flash chromatography is a form of column chromatography in which mobile phase is driven through the column under positive pressure. It can speed up separation compared with gravity flow. It is widely used to purify compound mixtures, with solvent gradients often used to improve separation.

Q9. What are phytochemical tests?

Phytochemical tests are preliminary chemical reactions used to indicate classes of compounds, such as alkaloids, tannins, flavonoids, saponins, glycosides, resins or terpenoids. Because different substances can produce similar reactions, positive results are screening evidence and should be confirmed with more specific methods.

Q10. What are chromatographic identification techniques?

They include TLC, HPTLC, HPLC and gas chromatography, among others. A sample may be compared with a reference standard by retention behaviour and detector response. Matching a single retention value alone is usually insufficient for definitive identification; orthogonal evidence improves confidence.

Q11. What are spectroscopic techniques used in phytochemistry?

UV-visible spectroscopy can provide information about chromophores; infrared spectroscopy helps identify functional groups; mass spectrometry provides molecular-mass and fragmentation information; and NMR spectroscopy provides detailed information about atomic environments and molecular structure. Often several methods are combined to characterise a natural product.

Q12. What is HPTLC?

High-performance thin-layer chromatography (HPTLC) uses carefully controlled plates, sample application, solvent development and densitometric or image-based detection. It can generate a reproducible chromatographic fingerprint of a plant extract and compare batches or samples against reference material.

Q13. What is fingerprinting of medicinal plants?

Fingerprinting creates a characteristic analytical pattern of multiple constituents from a plant material or extract, often using TLC/HPTLC or other chromatographic methods. It helps assess identity, consistency, batch-to-batch variation, adulteration and stability. A fingerprint is most useful when supported by validated methods and reference samples.

Q14. What are phytochemical markers or reference standards?

A phytochemical marker is a defined constituent used to help identify a material or assess its quality and consistency. A reference standard is a well-characterised substance used for comparison or measurement. Markers may be active constituents, characteristic constituents or analytical indicators; a marker is not automatically the only compound responsible for therapeutic activity.

Q15. Why are markers important in herbal-drug quality control?

Markers help confirm identity, estimate constituent levels, compare batches, monitor manufacturing and detect some forms of substitution or adulteration. Reliable quality assessment may also require botanical authentication, contaminant testing, microbial limits, pesticide/heavy-metal testing and evaluation of the whole chemical profile.

Q16. How are alkaloids screened and analysed?

Preliminary screening can use suitable alkaloid reagents, followed by separation using TLC/HPTLC, HPLC or other validated methods. Quantification may use a calibrated detector response against an appropriate reference standard. Extraction and analysis depend on the alkaloid's basicity, polarity, stability and the plant matrix.

Q17. How are glycosides and saponins screened?

Glycosides may be screened using tests suited to the sugar or aglycone class, with chromatographic confirmation where appropriate. Saponins may give persistent foam in a preliminary froth test because of their surface-active properties. Neither test alone establishes the identity or quantity of a specific compound.

Q18. How are tannins, resins and flavonoids screened?

Ferric chloride may indicate some phenolic compounds including tannins; flavonoids can be screened by selected colour reactions or aluminium chloride-based methods; resins may be assessed through solubility and appropriate chemical or chromatographic tests. These tests are presumptive and should be interpreted alongside controls and confirmatory analysis.

Q19. How are phenolics and steroids analysed?

Phenolics can be assessed using suitable colourimetric assays, chromatography and spectroscopic methods; total-phenolic assays estimate a broad response rather than identify every molecule. Steroids and sterol-like compounds can be screened using class reactions and then separated and characterised using chromatography, mass spectrometry or NMR as appropriate.

Q20. What is the importance of isolation and characterization in pharmacy?

These techniques help discover active compounds, establish chemical identity, evaluate purity, standardise herbal preparations, detect adulteration, investigate biological activity and support reproducible research. Proper validation and reference standards are important because natural materials can vary considerably.
