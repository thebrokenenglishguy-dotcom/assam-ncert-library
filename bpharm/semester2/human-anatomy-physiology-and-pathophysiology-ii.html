UNIT I — Nervous System (14 hours)
Q1. Describe the organisation of the nervous system.

The nervous system has two main divisions. The central nervous system (CNS) consists of the brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) includes nerves and ganglia outside the CNS. Functionally, the PNS includes sensory pathways that carry information to the CNS and motor pathways that carry commands to muscles and glands. The autonomic nervous system regulates many involuntary functions.

Q2. What is a neuron? Name its main parts.

A neuron is a specialised cell that receives and transmits information. Its main parts are the cell body (soma), dendrites and axon. Dendrites usually receive signals, the cell body maintains the cell, and the axon conducts electrical signals toward terminals that communicate with other cells.

Q3. What is neuroglia? State its functions.

Neuroglia are supporting cells of nervous tissue. Astrocytes help maintain the chemical environment and support the blood–brain barrier; oligodendrocytes form myelin in the CNS; Schwann cells form myelin in the PNS; microglia provide immune surveillance and phagocytosis; and ependymal cells line the brain ventricles and central canal and are associated with cerebrospinal-fluid circulation.

Q4. Classify neurons by structure and function.

Structurally, neurons may be multipolar, bipolar or pseudounipolar. Functionally, they are sensory (afferent), motor (efferent) or interneurons. Sensory neurons carry information from receptors toward the CNS; motor neurons carry commands away from the CNS; interneurons connect and process signals within neural circuits.

Q5. What is a nerve fibre? Explain myelinated and unmyelinated fibres.

A nerve fibre generally refers to an axon and its coverings. Myelinated fibres have insulating myelin interrupted by nodes of Ranvier, allowing rapid saltatory conduction from node to node. Unmyelinated fibres lack this thick myelin sheath and usually conduct impulses more slowly.

Q6. Define resting membrane potential.

Resting membrane potential is the voltage difference across a neuron's membrane when it is not producing an action potential. It results from unequal ion distributions, selective membrane permeability—especially to potassium—and the activity of ion pumps such as Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. A typical neuron is around −70 mV, though the value varies by cell.

Q7. Explain an action potential.

When a sufficient depolarising stimulus reaches threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open and sodium enters, rapidly depolarising the membrane. Sodium channels then inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels allow potassium to leave, repolarising the membrane. A brief after-hyperpolarisation may follow. Refractory periods help ensure one-way propagation and limit firing frequency.

Q8. What is a nerve impulse? How does it travel?

A nerve impulse is a propagated action potential. In an unmyelinated axon, neighbouring membrane regions depolarise sequentially. In a myelinated axon, the action potential is regenerated mainly at nodes of Ranvier, making conduction faster and more energy-efficient.

Q9. What are sensory receptors? Give examples.

Sensory receptors detect changes inside or outside the body and convert them into electrical signals. Mechanoreceptors detect touch, pressure or stretch; thermoreceptors detect temperature; nociceptors detect potentially damaging stimuli; photoreceptors detect light; and chemoreceptors detect chemical changes such as oxygen, carbon dioxide or taste molecules.

Q10. Define synapse and distinguish chemical and electrical synapses.

A synapse is a junction through which one cell communicates with another. At a chemical synapse, a presynaptic terminal releases neurotransmitter into a synaptic cleft; the transmitter binds receptors on the target cell. Electrical synapses use gap junctions that allow direct current flow between cells and can transmit signals very rapidly.

Q11. Name important neurotransmitters and their general roles.

Acetylcholine acts at neuromuscular junctions and in autonomic pathways. Glutamate is the major excitatory transmitter in much of the CNS; GABA is a major inhibitory transmitter in the brain. Dopamine contributes to movement, motivation and reward; serotonin affects mood, sleep and other functions; noradrenaline contributes to alertness and autonomic regulation. Effects depend on receptor type and location.

Q12. What protects the central nervous system?

The skull protects the brain and the vertebral column protects the spinal cord. Three meninges—dura mater, arachnoid mater and pia mater—surround the CNS. Cerebrospinal fluid cushions the brain and spinal cord, supports buoyancy and helps maintain the chemical environment. The blood–brain barrier restricts passage of many substances from blood into brain tissue.

Q13. Describe the ventricles and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

The ventricular system includes two lateral ventricles, the third ventricle and the fourth ventricle, connected by channels. CSF is produced mainly by the choroid plexuses, flows through ventricles and around the brain and spinal cord in the subarachnoid space, and is returned to venous blood through arachnoid granulations and other drainage pathways. CSF cushions the CNS and transports some nutrients and waste products.

Q14. State the major functions of the cerebrum.

The cerebrum supports conscious sensation, voluntary movement, language, memory, reasoning, planning, emotion and complex behaviour. The cerebral cortex is divided into lobes with specialised but interacting functions: frontal areas are important in planning and motor control, parietal areas in somatic sensation and spatial processing, temporal areas in hearing and memory, and occipital areas in vision.

Q15. State the functions of the brainstem and cerebellum.

The brainstem—midbrain, pons and medulla—contains pathways and nuclei involved in alertness, cranial-nerve functions, breathing, cardiovascular regulation and other vital processes. The cerebellum coordinates movement, balance, posture, timing and motor learning; it helps make movements accurate rather than initiating most voluntary movement by itself.

Q16. Describe the spinal cord and its afferent and efferent pathways.

The spinal cord lies within the vertebral canal and contains central grey matter surrounded by white-matter tracts. Afferent sensory fibres enter through dorsal roots; efferent motor fibres leave through ventral roots. Ascending tracts carry sensory information toward the brain, while descending tracts carry motor commands toward spinal circuits.

Q17. What is a reflex arc?

A reflex arc is the neural pathway underlying a reflex. It includes a receptor, sensory neuron, integration centre in the spinal cord or brainstem, motor neuron and effector such as a muscle or gland. Reflexes provide rapid, often automatic responses; some involve interneurons, while a simple stretch reflex can be monosynaptic.

Q18. Explain the pathophysiology of epilepsy.

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder involving a tendency to have recurrent unprovoked seizures. Seizures arise from abnormal, excessive or synchronised neuronal activity and may be focal or generalised. Causes include genetic factors, structural brain injury, infection and other conditions; in some people the cause remains unknown. Diagnosis and treatment depend on seizure type and clinical evaluation.

Q19. What is Parkinson's disease?

Parkinson's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder associated with loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra and changes in basal-ganglia circuits. Typical motor features include bradykinesia, rigidity and resting tremor; balance problems and non-motor symptoms can also occur. It is not simply a normal part of ageing.

Q20. Define stroke and distinguish ischemic and haemorrhagic stroke.

Stroke is acute neurological injury caused by interruption of blood supply or bleeding in or around the brain. Ischemic stroke results from blocked blood flow; haemorrhagic stroke results from a ruptured blood vessel. Sudden facial droop, arm weakness, speech difficulty, vision loss or severe imbalance requires emergency services because prompt assessment is time-critical.

Q21. What is migraine?

Migraine is a neurological disorder causing recurrent attacks of headache, often with nausea and sensitivity to light or sound; some people experience aura. It involves complex brain and trigeminal-vascular mechanisms. Triggers and symptoms vary, and a new sudden severe headache or headache with neurological warning signs needs urgent assessment.

Q22. Briefly explain depression and schizophrenia.

Depression can involve persistent low mood or loss of interest, changes in sleep or appetite, reduced energy, impaired concentration and feelings of worthlessness. Schizophrenia can involve psychosis, such as hallucinations or delusions, disorganised thinking and reduced motivation or emotional expression. Both are complex health conditions involving biological, psychological and social factors; neither should be explained by a single neurotransmitter imbalance alone.

Q23. What is Alzheimer's disease?

Alzheimer's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disease and a common cause of dementia. It is associated with abnormal amyloid-beta and tau biology, synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss. Early symptoms often include difficulty forming new memories, followed by broader cognitive and functional decline. Diagnosis considers the pattern of symptoms and other possible causes.

Q24. What is meningitis?

Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges, caused by infections such as bacteria or viruses and less commonly by non-infectious causes. Symptoms can include fever, headache, neck stiffness, light sensitivity and altered consciousness. Suspected meningitis—especially with rapid deterioration, rash or confusion—is a medical emergency.

UNIT II — Gastrointestinal System (12 hours)
Q1. Trace the path of food through the gastrointestinal tract.

Food passes through the mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine (duodenum, jejunum and ileum), large intestine (caecum and colon), rectum and anal canal. Accessory digestive organs include the salivary glands, liver, gallbladder and pancreas; they release secretions that aid digestion.

Q2. Describe the main functions of the stomach.

The stomach stores food temporarily, mixes it mechanically, secretes acid and enzymes, begins protein digestion, and releases chyme gradually into the duodenum. Mucus and bicarbonate help protect the lining. Intrinsic factor produced by parietal cells is needed for normal vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum.

Q3. How is hydrochloric acid produced in the stomach?

Parietal cells produce hydrochloric acid. Carbon dioxide and water form carbonic acid through carbonic anhydrase; it dissociates into hydrogen and bicarbonate. A proton pump (H⁺/K⁺-ATPase) secretes hydrogen into the stomach lumen, while chloride follows through channels to form HCl. Bicarbonate enters blood after meals, producing the post-meal alkaline tide.

Q4. How is gastric acid secretion regulated?

Acetylcholine from vagal pathways, gastrin from G cells and histamine from enterochromaffin-like cells stimulate parietal-cell acid secretion through interacting receptors. Somatostatin and intestinal signals can inhibit secretion. The balance changes with meals, acidity and neural and hormonal feedback.

Q5. What is the role of pepsin in digestion?

Chief cells release pepsinogen, an inactive precursor. Acid converts pepsinogen to pepsin, which breaks proteins into smaller peptides. Pepsin works best in the acidic stomach environment; most further protein digestion occurs through pancreatic and intestinal enzymes.

Q6. Describe the small intestine and its functions.

The small intestine has circular folds, villi and microvilli that greatly increase absorptive surface area. The duodenum receives chyme, bile and pancreatic secretions; the jejunum absorbs much of the digested nutrients; the ileum absorbs, among other substances, bile salts and vitamin B12 bound to intrinsic factor. Regions have overlapping functions.

Q7. State the functions of the large intestine.

The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes, houses a complex microbiota, supports fermentation of some undigested materials and forms and stores faeces. Its movements help propel contents toward the rectum for defecation.

Q8. What are the functions of saliva and salivary glands?

The parotid, submandibular and sublingual glands produce saliva. Saliva moistens food, aids swallowing and taste, buffers acids and helps protect oral tissues. Salivary amylase begins starch digestion, while antimicrobial factors contribute to oral defence.

Q9. Describe the digestive functions of the pancreas.

The exocrine pancreas secretes bicarbonate-rich fluid to neutralise acidic chyme and enzymes such as pancreatic amylase, lipase and protease precursors. Proteases are generally released as inactive zymogens to reduce self-digestion. The endocrine pancreas releases hormones, including insulin and glucagon, to regulate metabolism.

Q10. State the digestive and metabolic functions of the liver.

The liver produces bile, processes absorbed nutrients, stores glycogen and selected vitamins and minerals, synthesises albumin and many clotting factors, metabolises drugs and hormones, and converts ammonia to urea. Bile salts help emulsify dietary fats and support absorption of lipids and fat-soluble vitamins.

Q11. Explain peristalsis and segmentation.

Peristalsis is a coordinated wave of smooth-muscle contraction that moves gastrointestinal contents forward. Segmentation consists of local contractions that mix contents and improve contact with the intestinal lining. Both are regulated by enteric neural circuits and modified by autonomic and hormonal signals.

Q12. Summarise the digestion and absorption of carbohydrates, proteins and fats.

Carbohydrates are converted to monosaccharides and absorbed into portal blood. Proteins are digested to amino acids and small peptides, which enter intestinal cells and then portal blood. Lipids are emulsified by bile salts and digested by lipases; lipid products enter micelles, cross the intestinal lining and are reassembled into lipids packaged in chylomicrons, which largely enter lymph before blood.

Q13. What is inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)?

IBD mainly includes Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. It involves chronic, relapsing inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract linked to immune, genetic, microbial and environmental factors. Crohn's disease can affect any GI region and may involve deeper bowel layers; ulcerative colitis affects the colon, usually beginning at the rectum and extending continuously. Symptoms may include diarrhoea, abdominal pain, fatigue and bleeding.

Q14. Explain peptic ulcer disease.

Peptic ulcers are breaks in the lining of the stomach or duodenum. Common causes include Helicobacter pylori infection and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Ulcers develop when mucosal defence is impaired relative to acid and pepsin injury. Complications include bleeding, perforation and obstruction; vomiting blood or passing black stools requires urgent care.

Q15. Define jaundice and explain common causes.

Jaundice is yellowing of the skin and sclera due to increased bilirubin. Causes include excess red-cell breakdown, liver-cell disease, impaired bilirubin processing and obstruction of bile flow. Blood bilirubin fractions and liver tests help guide evaluation; jaundice is a sign, not a diagnosis by itself.

Q16. What is hepatitis?

Hepatitis is inflammation of the liver. Causes include hepatitis viruses, alcohol, medicines, autoimmune disease and metabolic conditions. Symptoms may include fatigue, nausea, abdominal discomfort, dark urine and jaundice, but some cases are asymptomatic. Prevention and treatment depend on the cause; vaccines are available for hepatitis A and B.

Q17. What causes typhoid fever?

Typhoid fever is a systemic infection caused by Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, usually transmitted through food or water contaminated by an infected person. It can cause prolonged fever, headache, abdominal symptoms and serious complications. Safe water, sanitation, food hygiene and vaccination help prevent it; diagnosis and antibiotic selection require clinical guidance because resistance patterns vary.

Q18. Distinguish alcohol-associated and non-alcohol-associated fatty liver disease.

Alcohol-associated liver disease is related to alcohol exposure and may range from fat accumulation to hepatitis, fibrosis and cirrhosis. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD; terminology evolving from NAFLD) is associated with metabolic risk factors such as obesity, diabetes and dyslipidaemia. Both can progress in some people; assessment considers exposure history, metabolic health and evidence of liver injury or fibrosis.

UNIT III — Respiratory and Urinary Systems (12 hours)

Respiratory System

Q1. Describe the anatomy of the respiratory system.

The respiratory tract includes the nose, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles and lungs. The conducting zone moves, warms, humidifies and filters air. The respiratory zone, including alveoli, is the main site of gas exchange. The right lung usually has three lobes and the left lung two, leaving space for the heart.

Q2. Explain the mechanism of inspiration and expiration.

During quiet inspiration, the diaphragm contracts and descends while external intercostal muscles help expand the thorax. Thoracic volume increases, alveolar pressure falls slightly below atmospheric pressure, and air flows in. Quiet expiration is usually passive as respiratory muscles relax and elastic recoil reduces lung volume. Forced breathing recruits additional muscles.

Q3. How is respiration regulated?

Respiratory networks in the medulla and pons generate and adjust breathing patterns. Central chemoreceptors respond mainly to changes in CO₂ through its effects on cerebrospinal-fluid pH; peripheral chemoreceptors in carotid and aortic bodies respond to low arterial oxygen, CO₂ and pH. Lung and airway receptors also modify breathing.

Q4. Define tidal volume, vital capacity and residual volume.

Tidal volume is the air moved in a normal breath. Vital capacity is the maximum volume exhaled after a maximal inspiration. Residual volume is the air remaining after maximal expiration. Other measures include inspiratory reserve, expiratory reserve, total lung capacity and functional residual capacity. Spirometry measures many—but not all—of these volumes.

Q5. How are oxygen and carbon dioxide transported in blood?

Most oxygen is carried bound to haemoglobin; a small amount is dissolved in plasma. Carbon dioxide is transported mainly as bicarbonate, with some bound to proteins including haemoglobin and some dissolved. In tissues, carbonic anhydrase in red cells supports conversion of CO₂ and water to carbonic acid and bicarbonate.

Q6. What are artificial respiration and resuscitation?

Artificial ventilation provides air or oxygen to support breathing when a person cannot breathe adequately. Resuscitation refers to emergency actions to support circulation and breathing, including cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) when indicated. Follow current recognised first-aid guidance, call local emergency services and use an automated external defibrillator (AED) if available; training is important.

Q7. Explain asthma pathophysiology.

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disorder with variable airflow limitation and bronchial hyperresponsiveness. Triggers can cause airway smooth-muscle contraction, mucosal swelling and mucus production. Symptoms include wheeze, cough, chest tightness and breathlessness. Severe or rapidly worsening breathing difficulty is an emergency.

Q8. What is chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)?

COPD is a chronic lung disease with persistent airflow limitation, commonly related to harmful particle or gas exposure, especially tobacco smoke. It includes varying contributions from small-airway disease and emphysema. Symptoms often include chronic cough, sputum and breathlessness. Prevention, inhaled treatment, rehabilitation and management depend on clinical assessment.

Q9. Describe tuberculosis (TB).

Tuberculosis is an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and most often affects the lungs. It spreads through airborne particles from a person with infectious pulmonary TB. Symptoms may include prolonged cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss and blood-stained sputum. Diagnosis, combination treatment and public-health measures are essential to reduce transmission and drug resistance.

Urinary System

Q10. Describe the anatomy of the urinary system.

The urinary system consists of two kidneys, two ureters, the urinary bladder and the urethra. Kidneys filter blood and regulate water, electrolytes, acid–base balance and blood pressure; ureters transport urine to the bladder, which stores it until urination through the urethra.

Q11. What is a nephron? Name its main parts.

The nephron is the functional unit of the kidney. It includes the renal corpuscle (glomerulus and Bowman's capsule), proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule and connection to a collecting duct. Filtration, reabsorption and secretion across these structures form and adjust urine.

Q12. Explain urine formation.

Urine formation involves three major processes. Glomerular filtration moves water and small solutes from blood into the nephron. Tubular reabsorption returns needed substances such as glucose, amino acids, water and electrolytes to blood. Tubular secretion moves selected substances, including hydrogen ions and some drugs, from blood into the tubule. Hormones fine-tune water and electrolyte handling.

Q13. State the major functions of the kidneys.

Kidneys excrete metabolic waste and many drugs, regulate body water and electrolytes, maintain acid–base balance, help control blood pressure, release erythropoietin for red-cell production and activate vitamin D to support calcium balance. Kidney disease can therefore affect several organ systems.

Q14. What is the micturition reflex?

As the bladder fills, stretch receptors signal the spinal cord. Parasympathetic pathways can contract the detrusor muscle and relax the internal outlet; voluntary control of the external urethral sphincter and higher brain centres allow urination to be delayed or initiated in a suitable setting. The reflex changes with age and neurological conditions.

Q15. Explain the role of kidneys in acid–base balance.

Kidneys reclaim filtered bicarbonate, secrete hydrogen ions and generate new bicarbonate, including through ammonium excretion. These processes complement the lungs, which regulate carbon dioxide. Renal compensation develops more slowly than respiratory responses but is important for sustained acid–base regulation.

Q16. Explain the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS).

When renal perfusion, sodium delivery or effective circulating volume falls, juxtaglomerular cells can release renin. Renin leads to angiotensin I formation; ACE converts it to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II constricts blood vessels and promotes aldosterone release. Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion in the distal nephron, helping support blood volume and pressure.

Q17. Distinguish acute kidney injury and chronic kidney disease.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a relatively rapid decline in kidney function, caused by factors such as reduced perfusion, intrinsic kidney injury or urinary obstruction. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) involves abnormalities of kidney structure or function persisting for at least three months. Both require evaluation of cause, severity, fluid/electrolyte status and medication safety.

Q18. What is a urinary tract infection (UTI)?

A UTI is an infection involving the urinary tract, commonly caused by bacteria entering through the urethra. A lower UTI may cause painful or frequent urination; kidney infection can cause fever, flank pain, nausea or systemic illness. Diagnosis and treatment depend on symptoms, urine testing, patient factors and local antimicrobial guidance.

UNIT IV — Endocrine System (10 hours)
Q1. What is an endocrine gland?

An endocrine gland releases hormones into blood or surrounding fluid to act on target cells that possess appropriate receptors. Endocrine signals help regulate growth, metabolism, reproduction, stress responses, fluid balance and homeostasis.

Q2. Classify hormones chemically.

Hormones can be classified as peptide/protein hormones (such as insulin), steroid hormones derived from cholesterol (such as cortisol and oestrogen), and amino-acid-derived hormones (such as adrenaline and thyroid hormones). Some hormones are lipid-derived, such as eicosanoids, which often act locally.

Q3. Explain the major mechanisms of hormone action.

Water-soluble hormones often bind cell-surface receptors and activate second messengers such as cAMP, calcium or kinase pathways. Lipid-soluble steroid and thyroid hormones can enter cells and bind intracellular receptors that regulate gene expression. These are broad patterns; receptor pathways differ by hormone and tissue.

Q4. Describe the pituitary gland and its hormones.

The anterior pituitary releases growth hormone, prolactin, TSH, ACTH, FSH and LH. The posterior pituitary stores and releases oxytocin and vasopressin (ADH), which are made in the hypothalamus. Pituitary hormones regulate growth, lactation, thyroid and adrenal function, reproduction, water balance and childbirth-related processes.

Q5. State the functions of thyroid hormones.

The thyroid produces T4 and T3, which increase metabolic activity and are important for normal growth and brain development. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) from the pituitary regulates thyroid hormone synthesis through negative feedback. The thyroid also produces calcitonin, whose role in everyday adult calcium regulation is more limited than that of parathyroid hormone and vitamin D.

Q6. What is the role of the parathyroid glands?

Parathyroid hormone (PTH) is released when blood calcium falls. It increases renal calcium reabsorption, promotes phosphate excretion and stimulates activation of vitamin D, which increases intestinal calcium absorption. PTH also affects bone turnover, helping restore calcium balance.

Q7. Explain the adrenal gland and its hormones.

The adrenal cortex produces mineralocorticoids such as aldosterone, glucocorticoids such as cortisol, and adrenal androgens. The adrenal medulla produces catecholamines, mainly adrenaline and noradrenaline. These hormones help regulate salt balance, metabolism, stress responses and sympathetic effects.

Q8. Explain the endocrine functions of the pancreas.

Pancreatic islet beta cells secrete insulin, which generally lowers blood glucose, while alpha cells secrete glucagon, which raises blood glucose mainly through liver actions. Delta cells release somatostatin, which modulates secretion. The balance responds to nutrient availability and other signals.

Q9. What are the functions of the pineal gland and thymus?

The pineal gland secretes melatonin, which helps coordinate circadian timing and sleep-related rhythms. The thymus supports T-lymphocyte maturation, especially during childhood; it becomes smaller and is partly replaced by fatty tissue with age, although immune functions persist.

Q10. Explain diabetes mellitus pathophysiology.

Diabetes mellitus results from inadequate insulin secretion, impaired insulin action or both, leading to hyperglycaemia. Type 1 is commonly autoimmune beta-cell destruction; type 2 combines insulin resistance and progressive beta-cell dysfunction. Persistent hyperglycaemia can cause retinopathy, kidney disease, neuropathy and cardiovascular complications.

Q11. What is hypothyroidism?

Hypothyroidism occurs when thyroid hormone supply is insufficient for the body's needs. Causes include autoimmune thyroiditis, iodine imbalance, thyroid surgery or certain medicines. Symptoms may include tiredness, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin and slowed heart rate, but symptoms alone cannot confirm the diagnosis. TSH and free T4 are commonly used in assessment.

Q12. What is hyperthyroidism?

Hyperthyroidism is excessive thyroid hormone production; thyrotoxicosis describes the effects of excess thyroid hormone from any cause. Causes include Graves' disease, toxic nodules and some forms of thyroiditis. Features can include weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, palpitations and anxiety. Diagnosis uses clinical evaluation and thyroid tests.

Q13. Define goitre.

Goitre is enlargement of the thyroid gland. It can occur with iodine deficiency, autoimmune thyroid disease, nodules or other thyroid conditions and may occur with normal, low or high thyroid hormone levels. Evaluation depends on symptoms, examination, thyroid-function tests and sometimes imaging.

Q14. What is polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)?

PCOS is a common endocrine and metabolic condition that may involve irregular ovulation, clinical or biochemical androgen excess and polycystic ovarian appearance. Not every person has all features. It can be associated with acne, excess hair growth, irregular periods, fertility difficulties and increased metabolic risk. Diagnosis requires clinical criteria and exclusion of other causes.

UNIT V — Reproductive System and Cancer (12 hours)
Q1. Describe the main organs of the male reproductive system.

The testes produce sperm and testosterone. The epididymis supports sperm maturation and storage; the vas deferens transports sperm; accessory glands, including seminal vesicles and prostate, add fluid to semen; and the urethra carries semen out of the body. The penis supports delivery of semen during sexual activity.

Q2. Describe the main organs of the female reproductive system.

The ovaries produce oocytes and hormones. The uterine tubes transport the oocyte and are the usual site of fertilisation. The uterus supports implantation and fetal development; the cervix connects the uterus to the vagina. The vagina receives semen and forms the birth canal. External genital structures protect openings and contribute to sexual function.

Q3. State the functions of testosterone, oestrogen and progesterone.

Testosterone supports male reproductive development, sperm production and secondary sexual characteristics. Oestrogens support female reproductive development, endometrial growth and bone health. Progesterone prepares and maintains the endometrium for pregnancy and supports pregnancy-related changes. All three hormones can have effects in people of different sexes, with concentrations varying by physiology.

Q4. Explain the menstrual cycle.

The menstrual cycle is coordinated by hypothalamic GnRH, pituitary FSH and LH, and ovarian oestrogen and progesterone. During the follicular phase, follicles develop and oestrogen helps rebuild the endometrium. An LH surge usually triggers ovulation. In the luteal phase, the corpus luteum produces progesterone; if pregnancy does not occur, hormone levels fall and menstruation follows.

Q5. What is fertilisation?

Fertilisation usually occurs in the ampulla of the uterine tube when a sperm fuses with an oocyte. The resulting zygote restores the diploid chromosome number and begins cleavage divisions while moving toward the uterus. Implantation occurs later when the developing embryo attaches to the uterine lining.

Q6. Explain spermatogenesis.

Spermatogenesis occurs in the seminiferous tubules of the testes. Spermatogonia divide and develop into primary spermatocytes, which undergo meiosis to produce haploid spermatids. Spermatids differentiate into spermatozoa. Sertoli cells support development, while Leydig cells produce testosterone under LH stimulation.

Q7. Explain oogenesis.

Oogenesis begins before birth, when oogonia form primary oocytes that pause in meiosis. After puberty, selected oocytes resume development during cycles. Typically, one secondary oocyte is ovulated and completes meiosis II only if fertilisation occurs. The process produces one large functional ovum and smaller polar bodies because cytoplasm is divided unequally.

Q8. Summarise pregnancy and placental functions.

After implantation, the placenta supports exchange of oxygen, nutrients and waste between maternal and fetal circulations without normally mixing the blood directly. It produces hormones including human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), progesterone and oestrogens. The placenta is not an absolute barrier; some medicines, infections and substances can cross it.

Q9. What is parturition?

Parturition is childbirth. Near term, coordinated uterine contractions, cervical changes and hormonal and local signals help deliver the baby and placenta. Oxytocin and prostaglandins contribute to contraction and labour progression. Timing and course vary, and complications require skilled obstetric care.

Q10. What is AIDS and how is it related to HIV?

HIV is a virus that infects CD4 T lymphocytes and other immune cells. Without effective treatment, immune damage can progress to acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), the advanced stage of HIV infection. HIV is transmitted through certain body fluids, including blood, semen, vaginal/rectal fluids and breast milk—not by casual contact, sharing food or hugging. Effective antiretroviral therapy suppresses the virus and helps people live long lives.

Q11. Describe syphilis.

Syphilis is a sexually transmitted infection caused by Treponema pallidum. It may progress through primary, secondary, latent and tertiary stages if untreated; symptoms vary by stage and can include a painless sore, rash and later organ or neurological complications. It is treatable with appropriate antibiotics, and testing and partner management are important.

Q12. Describe gonorrhoea.

Gonorrhoea is a sexually transmitted infection caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae. It can cause urethral, cervical, rectal or throat infection and may have few or no symptoms. Complications include pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility. Antibiotic resistance is an important concern, so diagnosis and treatment should follow current local clinical guidance.

Q13. How can sexually transmitted infections be prevented?

Risk reduction includes correct and consistent condom use, testing based on risk and symptoms, timely treatment of affected partners, vaccination where available (such as HPV and hepatitis B vaccines), and avoiding sexual contact until advised after treatment. HIV prevention may also include PrEP or PEP for eligible people under clinical guidance. Stigma can discourage testing and should be avoided.

Q14. Define cancer and explain its basic pathogenesis.

Cancer is a group of diseases in which abnormal cells grow and divide without normal control and may invade nearby tissue or spread to distant sites. Cancer development involves accumulated genetic and epigenetic changes that affect cell growth, DNA repair, cell death and interactions with the immune system and tissue environment.

Q15. What are oncogenes and tumour-suppressor genes?

Proto-oncogenes normally help regulate growth and survival; activating changes can turn them into oncogenes that promote uncontrolled growth. Tumour-suppressor genes normally restrain cell division, repair damage or promote cell death; loss of their function can contribute to cancer. Cancer usually results from multiple changes rather than a single mutation.

Q16. Name major cancer risk factors.

Risk factors include tobacco, certain infections (such as HPV and hepatitis B/C), ultraviolet and ionising radiation, some occupational or environmental exposures, alcohol, inherited predisposition, age and some metabolic or hormonal factors. A risk factor increases probability but does not mean that a person will definitely develop cancer.

Q17. Distinguish benign and malignant tumours.

Benign tumours do not invade distant tissues and usually remain local, though their location can still cause harm. Malignant tumours invade surrounding tissues and may metastasise to distant sites. Tumour behaviour depends on its type, location and biological features.

Q18. What is metastasis?

Metastasis is the spread of cancer cells from the primary tumour to distant organs through blood, lymphatic channels or body cavities, followed by growth at a new site. Metastasis is a major feature of many malignant cancers and strongly affects staging and treatment decisions.

Q19. How is cancer commonly detected and staged?

Evaluation may include screening tests, imaging, laboratory tests and tissue biopsy. Histopathology often confirms the diagnosis. Staging describes the extent of cancer, commonly considering tumour size or local invasion (T), lymph nodes (N) and distant metastasis (M) for many solid tumours. Screening is different from diagnostic testing and is recommended according to age, risk and guidelines.

Q20. Name broad approaches to cancer treatment.

Treatment may include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy or combinations. Choice depends on cancer type, stage, molecular features, patient health and goals of care. These treatments require specialist assessment and monitoring.
