UNIT I — Aliphatic Saturated Hydrocarbons: Alkanes and Cycloalkanes (12 hours)
Q1. What are alkanes? Give their general formula and examples.

Alkanes are saturated acyclic hydrocarbons containing only carbon–carbon single bonds. Their general formula is CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Examples are methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), propane (C₃H₈) and butane (C₄H₁₀). Their carbon atoms are generally sp³ hybridised with approximately tetrahedral geometry.

Q2. State the important physical properties of alkanes.

Alkanes are generally non-polar, poorly soluble in water and soluble in many non-polar organic solvents. Their boiling points usually increase with molecular size; branching generally lowers boiling point compared with a straight-chain isomer. Smaller members are gases, middle members are liquids, and higher members are solids at room temperature.

Q3. Explain the Wurtz reaction.

In the Wurtz reaction, two alkyl-halide molecules couple in the presence of sodium metal in dry ether to form a higher alkane: 2 R–X + 2 Na → R–R + 2 NaX. It is most useful for preparing symmetrical alkanes from suitable primary alkyl halides. Using two different halides often gives a mixture of products.

Q4. What is Kolbe's electrolysis reaction?

In Kolbe electrolysis, electrolysis of an aqueous solution of a carboxylate salt causes decarboxylation at the anode. The resulting radicals couple to form an alkane: 2 RCOO⁻ → R–R + 2 CO₂ + 2 e⁻ (overall products depend on electrode and solution conditions). It commonly produces a symmetrical alkane with twice the alkyl-group carbon count, excluding the carboxyl carbon.

Q5. Explain Clemmensen reduction.

Clemmensen reduction converts the carbonyl group of an aldehyde or ketone to a methylene group under strongly acidic conditions using zinc amalgam. A simplified transformation is R–CO–R′ → R–CH₂–R′. It is useful when the substrate tolerates strongly acidic conditions; it is not suitable for every molecule.

Q6. Explain Wolff–Kishner reduction.

Wolff–Kishner reduction converts an aldehyde or ketone carbonyl group to a methylene group using hydrazine and strongly basic, heated conditions. The carbonyl first forms a hydrazone, which then loses nitrogen to give the reduced product. It is often selected when strongly acidic Clemmensen conditions would damage the substrate.

Q7. Compare Clemmensen and Wolff–Kishner reductions.

Both reduce aldehydes and ketones to hydrocarbons at the carbonyl carbon. Clemmensen uses zinc amalgam in strongly acidic conditions; Wolff–Kishner uses hydrazine and strong base with heat. Selection depends on the functional groups and their tolerance of acid or base. Neither method should be assumed suitable without considering the full molecule.

Q8. Name common chemical reactions of alkanes.

Alkanes undergo combustion, free-radical halogenation under suitable conditions, and cracking or isomerisation in industrial settings. Because C–C and C–H bonds are relatively unreactive, alkanes do not usually undergo addition reactions like alkenes.

Q9. Explain free-radical halogenation of an alkane.

Halogenation replaces a hydrogen atom with a halogen through a chain mechanism. Initiation: light or heat splits X₂ into radicals. Propagation: a halogen radical abstracts H from the alkane, and the alkyl radical reacts with X₂ to form haloalkane and regenerate a halogen radical. Termination: radicals combine. The reaction may give mixtures because different hydrogen positions can react.

Q10. Why is chlorination less selective than bromination?

Radical chlorination is generally faster and less selective, so it can substitute at different kinds of hydrogen positions. Radical bromination is slower and more selective because the hydrogen-abstraction step has a larger energetic distinction between radical stabilities. Product ratios also depend on the substrate and conditions.

Q11. What are liquid paraffin, soft paraffin and hard paraffin?

These are petroleum-derived mixtures of hydrocarbons. Liquid paraffin is an oily liquid used in selected pharmaceutical and personal-care products and, in appropriate formulations, as a lubricant. Soft paraffin is a semi-solid mixture used as an ointment base and emollient. Hard paraffin is a harder wax-like material used to stiffen ointment and cream bases. Pharmaceutical grade and intended route matter; they are not interchangeable with arbitrary industrial petroleum products.

Q12. What are cycloalkanes? Give their general formula.

Cycloalkanes are saturated hydrocarbons whose carbon atoms form one or more rings. A monocyclic cycloalkane has the general formula CₙH₂ₙ. Examples include cyclopropane, cyclobutane, cyclopentane and cyclohexane.

Q13. Explain Baeyer's strain theory.

Baeyer's strain theory proposed that cycloalkane stability depends on deviation of ring bond angles from the tetrahedral angle of about 109.5°. Small rings have considerable angle strain. The theory assumed rings were planar, which is a major limitation because many rings adopt non-planar conformations that reduce strain.

Q14. What is the Coulson–Moffitt modification?

The Coulson–Moffitt model explains bonding in cyclopropane using bent or “banana” bonds. The C–C bonding orbitals bend outward from the internuclear axis, helping explain why cyclopropane has unusual bonding and relatively high reactivity compared with larger cycloalkanes.

Q15. Explain the Sachse–Mohr theory.

The Sachse–Mohr theory recognises that larger cycloalkane rings, especially cyclohexane, can adopt non-planar conformations. Chair and boat forms avoid the severe angle strain predicted by a planar-ring model; the chair form is especially stable because it minimises angle and torsional strain.

Q16. Compare chair and boat conformations of cyclohexane.

The chair conformation has near-tetrahedral bond angles and largely staggered bonds, so it has low angle and torsional strain. The boat conformation has eclipsing interactions and steric interactions between flagpole hydrogens, making it higher in energy. Cyclohexane can interconvert between chair conformations through ring-flip motion.

UNIT II — Alkenes and Conjugated Dienes (12 hours)
Q1. What are alkenes? Give their general formula.

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon–carbon double bond. For an acyclic molecule with one double bond, the general formula is CₙH₂ₙ. The double bond consists of one sigma bond and one pi bond and restricts rotation, allowing geometric isomerism in suitable structures.

Q2. Explain dehydration of alcohols as a method of preparing alkenes.

Dehydration removes water from an alcohol to form an alkene under suitable acidic or catalytic conditions. A hydrogen is removed from a neighbouring carbon while the hydroxyl group leaves, forming a C=C bond. The major product often follows the more substituted-alkene tendency, but structure and reaction conditions affect the outcome.

Q3. How are alkenes prepared by dehydrohalogenation?

Dehydrohalogenation removes a hydrogen atom and a halide from adjacent carbon atoms in an alkyl halide, forming a double bond. It is commonly an elimination reaction promoted by a base. Product distribution depends on substrate structure, base, solvent, temperature and stereochemical requirements.

Q4. What is dehalogenation of vicinal dihalides?

A vicinal dihalide has halogen atoms on adjacent carbon atoms. Under suitable reductive conditions, removal of the two halogens can form an alkene. This transformation is useful in synthesis but the reagent and conditions must be selected for the substrate.

Q5. What is the Wittig reaction?

The Wittig reaction converts an aldehyde or ketone carbonyl group into a carbon–carbon double bond using a phosphorus ylide. The carbonyl carbon and ylide carbon become the two alkene carbons, and a phosphorus–oxygen product is formed. It is useful for forming alkenes at a chosen position in a synthetic route.

Q6. Name important reactions of alkenes.

Alkenes undergo electrophilic addition of hydrogen halides, halogens and water under suitable conditions; catalytic hydrogenation; oxidation such as epoxidation or dihydroxylation; and cleavage by ozonolysis or other oxidants. The pi bond is relatively electron-rich and attracts many electrophiles.

Q7. Explain electrophilic addition to an alkene.

The pi electrons attack an electrophile, forming a new bond and often a carbocation or a related intermediate. A nucleophile then reacts with the intermediate to give the addition product. The exact mechanism depends on the reagent; rearrangements can occur when a sufficiently stable carbocation intermediate forms.

Q8. State Markovnikov's rule.

For addition of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene under ordinary ionic conditions, hydrogen generally adds to the carbon that already has more hydrogen atoms, while X adds to the more substituted carbon. This orientation is often explained by formation of the more stable carbocation intermediate.

Q9. Explain anti-Markovnikov addition.

In the classic peroxide effect, HBr adds to certain alkenes by a free-radical chain mechanism with the opposite orientation to Markovnikov addition. Bromine ends up at the less substituted alkene carbon in the usual case. The peroxide effect is characteristic of HBr and should not be generalised to HCl or HI under the same conditions.

Q10. What is ozonolysis?

Ozonolysis cleaves a carbon–carbon double bond using ozone. After an appropriate work-up, the two alkene carbons become carbonyl-containing products; depending on the alkene and work-up, products may include aldehydes, ketones or further-oxidised products. The products can help identify the original position of a double bond.

Q11. What are conjugated dienes?

Conjugated dienes contain two double bonds separated by one single bond, as in 1,3-butadiene. Their adjacent p orbitals overlap across the system, allowing pi electrons to be delocalised. This conjugation stabilises the molecule compared with a comparable isolated diene.

Q12. Why are conjugated dienes more stable than isolated dienes?

Conjugated dienes have delocalised pi electrons spread across several atoms, lowering the molecule's energy through resonance. Evidence includes intermediate bond lengths and distinctive addition patterns. Isolated double bonds are separated by more than one single bond and do not share the same continuous pi system.

Q13. Explain the Diels–Alder reaction.

The Diels–Alder reaction is a concerted [4+2] cycloaddition between a conjugated diene in the s-cis conformation and a dienophile, forming a six-membered ring. It creates two new sigma bonds and one new pi bond. Electron-withdrawing substituents on many dienophiles can increase reactivity, and stereochemical relationships are often retained.

Q14. Distinguish 1,2-addition and 1,4-addition to a conjugated diene.

In 1,2-addition, new groups attach to carbon 1 and carbon 2 of the conjugated system. In 1,4-addition, they attach to carbon 1 and carbon 4, with the double bond shifting to the middle. Relative amounts depend on conditions; lower-temperature control may favour the faster-forming kinetic product, while higher-temperature equilibration may favour the more stable thermodynamic product.

Q15. Compare electrophilic and free-radical addition.

Electrophilic addition begins when an electron-rich pi bond reacts with an electrophile and may proceed through a carbocation or bridged intermediate. Free-radical addition involves radical initiation and chain propagation steps. Mechanism determines regioselectivity and stereochemical outcome; the classic peroxide-assisted anti-Markovnikov addition of HBr is radical, not a general rule for all HX additions.

UNIT III — Alkyl Halides: Substitution and Elimination (12 hours)
Q1. What are alkyl halides?

Alkyl halides (haloalkanes) are compounds in which a halogen atom is bonded to an sp³-hybridised carbon of an alkyl group. They may be primary, secondary or tertiary depending on how many carbon groups are attached to the carbon bearing the halogen. This structure strongly affects substitution and elimination reactions.

Q2. Explain the SN2 mechanism.

SN2 means bimolecular nucleophilic substitution. The nucleophile attacks the carbon bearing the leaving group from the side opposite the leaving group in one concerted step. The rate depends on both substrate and nucleophile: rate = k[RX][Nu⁻]. Backside attack typically causes inversion of configuration at a stereogenic carbon.

Q3. Which factors favour SN2 reactions?

SN2 is favoured by less hindered substrates (often methyl and primary), a good nucleophile, a good leaving group and polar aprotic solvents in many cases. Tertiary substrates are strongly hindered and generally do not undergo ordinary SN2 substitution readily. Exact rates depend on the complete substrate and reaction conditions.

Q4. Explain the SN1 mechanism.

SN1 means unimolecular nucleophilic substitution. The leaving group first departs to form a carbocation in the rate-determining step; the nucleophile then attacks, and deprotonation may follow if needed. The ideal rate law is rate = k[RX]. Because the carbocation is planar, attack can occur from either face, often causing racemisation or partial racemisation.

Q5. Which factors favour SN1 reactions?

SN1 is favoured when the substrate can form a relatively stable carbocation, such as many tertiary, benzylic or allylic substrates; when the leaving group is good; and often in polar protic solvents that stabilise ions. Rearrangements may occur if a carbocation can form a more stable one.

Q6. Compare SN1 and SN2 reactions.

SN1 is stepwise, has a carbocation intermediate and a rate depending mainly on substrate concentration; it can rearrange and often loses stereochemical purity. SN2 is concerted, has a rate depending on both substrate and nucleophile, avoids carbocation rearrangements and gives inversion at a stereogenic centre. Substrate, nucleophile, solvent and leaving group determine which pathway dominates.

Q7. Explain the E1 elimination mechanism.

E1 is a two-step unimolecular elimination. The leaving group departs to form a carbocation, then a base removes a beta hydrogen to form a double bond. The rate-determining step depends on substrate concentration, so the ideal rate law is rate = k[RX]. E1 often competes with SN1 and may allow rearrangements.

Q8. Explain the E2 elimination mechanism.

E2 is a concerted elimination in which a base removes a beta hydrogen while the leaving group departs from the adjacent carbon, forming a double bond. Its ideal rate law is rate = k[RX][base]. The hydrogen and leaving group usually need an anti-periplanar arrangement; in cyclohexanes this often means a trans-diaxial geometry.

Q9. What factors affect E1 and E2 reactions?

E1 is favoured by carbocation-stabilising substrates and ionising solvents, often with weaker bases. E2 is favoured by a sufficiently strong base and a substrate with an accessible beta hydrogen; steric hindrance can favour elimination over substitution. Temperature, solvent, leaving group and substrate stereochemistry also affect the outcome.

Q10. State Zaitsev's rule.

Zaitsev's rule predicts that, in many beta-elimination reactions, the more substituted alkene is the major product because it is often more stable. There are exceptions: bulky bases may favour the less substituted Hofmann product, and stereoelectronic constraints or ring structure can control the product.

Q11. Compare substitution and elimination reactions.

Substitution replaces a leaving group with a nucleophile. Elimination removes a leaving group and a beta hydrogen to create a pi bond. Strong, bulky bases and higher temperatures often favour elimination; good nucleophiles and less hindered substrates can favour substitution. These are trends rather than absolute rules.

Q12. What is a leaving group? Give examples.

A leaving group is an atom or group that departs with the bonding electron pair. Halides such as iodide and bromide are often good leaving groups; chloride is generally less effective, while fluoride is usually poor in typical aliphatic substitution conditions. Leaving-group ability also depends on solvent and substrate.

Q13. How does solvent affect nucleophilic substitution?

Polar protic solvents can stabilise ions and often favour SN1 by helping ionisation; they can strongly solvate anionic nucleophiles and slow some SN2 reactions. Polar aprotic solvents often leave anionic nucleophiles more reactive and can favour SN2. Solvent effects depend on the complete reaction system.

Q14. What is stereochemistry in SN1, SN2, E1 and E2?

SN2 generally causes inversion at the reacting stereogenic carbon. SN1 often produces racemisation or partial racemisation because a carbocation is approximately planar. E2 has a geometric requirement, commonly anti-periplanar H and leaving group; E1 is less stereospecific because a carbocation intermediate forms before deprotonation.

UNIT IV — Benzene and Its Derivatives (12 hours)
Q1. Describe the structure of benzene.

Benzene (C₆H₆) is a planar six-membered carbon ring. Each carbon is approximately sp² hybridised, and the unhybridised p orbitals overlap around the ring to form a delocalised pi system. All C–C bonds are equivalent and intermediate in length between typical single and double bonds.

Q2. Explain resonance in benzene.

Benzene is represented by resonance contributors with alternating double bonds, but the actual molecule is not rapidly switching between separate structures. Its six pi electrons are delocalised over the ring, producing equivalent bonds and extra stability compared with a hypothetical localised structure.

Q3. State Hückel's rule for aromaticity.

A monocyclic, planar, fully conjugated system is aromatic when it contains (4n + 2) pi electrons, where n is a non-negative integer. Benzene has six pi electrons (n = 1), so it meets the rule. Geometry and continuous conjugation are also required; electron count alone is not enough.

Q4. Why does benzene usually undergo substitution rather than addition?

Substitution replaces a ring hydrogen while restoring the aromatic pi system after the reaction. Addition would normally break the continuous aromatic delocalisation and lose stabilisation. Therefore, under common conditions benzene favours electrophilic aromatic substitution rather than ordinary addition.

Q5. Explain IUPAC naming of mono- and disubstituted benzene derivatives.

For a monosubstituted benzene, name the substituent followed by benzene, unless a retained parent name is accepted (for example, methylbenzene/toluene). For disubstituted benzene, number the ring to give the lowest appropriate locants, such as 1,2-, 1,3- or 1,4-. The common terms ortho (o-), meta (m-) and para (p-) correspond to 1,2-, 1,3- and 1,4-substitution, respectively, but systematic numbering is preferred in formal names.

Q6. What is electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS)?

EAS is a reaction in which an electrophile replaces a hydrogen on an aromatic ring. The ring attacks the electrophile to form a resonance-stabilised sigma complex; loss of a proton then restores aromaticity. The overall process preserves the aromatic ring in the product.

Q7. Explain nitration of benzene.

Nitration introduces a nitro group (–NO₂) onto benzene. In the classic mechanism, a strongly acidic nitrating mixture generates the nitronium ion (NO₂⁺), the electrophile. Benzene forms a sigma complex with it, then loses a proton to restore aromaticity and give nitrobenzene.

Q8. Explain halogenation of benzene.

Benzene reacts with a halogen in the presence of an appropriate Lewis-acid catalyst to form a halobenzene. The catalyst helps generate a sufficiently electrophilic halogen species. Electrophilic attack forms the sigma complex, and deprotonation restores aromaticity.

Q9. What is Friedel–Crafts alkylation?

Friedel–Crafts alkylation adds an alkyl group to an aromatic ring using an alkylating agent and a Lewis-acid catalyst in the classic reaction. Limitations include carbocation rearrangements, polyalkylation because an alkyl group activates the ring, and failure with strongly deactivated rings or some catalyst-binding groups.

Q10. What is Friedel–Crafts acylation?

Friedel–Crafts acylation introduces an acyl group (–COR), commonly using an acylating agent with a Lewis-acid catalyst, to form an aromatic ketone after work-up. The acylium electrophile is resonance-stabilised, rearrangement is usually not a problem, and the acyl group deactivates the ring, so polyacylation is less common than in alkylation.

Q11. Explain sulphonation and desulphonation of benzene.

Sulphonation introduces a sulfonic acid group (–SO₃H) using sulfur-containing electrophilic conditions. It is reversible: under suitable aqueous acidic or heated conditions the sulfonic acid group can be removed by desulphonation. The sulfonic acid group can also act as a temporary blocking group in synthesis.

Q12. What is the effect of substituents on reactivity and orientation?

Electron-donating groups such as –OH, –OR and many amino groups usually activate the ring and direct new substitution mainly to ortho and para positions. Alkyl groups are weak activators and are generally ortho/para directors. Strong electron-withdrawing groups such as –NO₂, –COOH and –CHO usually deactivate the ring and direct substitution mainly to meta. Halogens are unusual: they deactivate the ring but direct ortho/para because of resonance donation.

Q13. Why are halogens deactivating but ortho/para-directing?

Halogens withdraw electron density inductively because of their electronegativity, making the ring less reactive overall. They can donate a lone pair by resonance, which stabilises the ortho and para sigma-complex contributors more than the meta pathway. The inductive deactivation outweighs resonance activation, while resonance influences orientation.

Q14. What is the difference between activating and deactivating substituents?

Activating substituents increase the rate of electrophilic aromatic substitution compared with benzene, commonly by donating electron density. Deactivating substituents reduce the rate, commonly by withdrawing electron density. Most activators are ortho/para directors; most deactivators are meta directors, with halogens as the important exception.

UNIT V — Carbonyl Compounds: Aldehydes and Ketones (12 hours)
Q1. What are aldehydes and ketones?

Aldehydes contain a terminal carbonyl group, –CHO, bonded to at least one hydrogen. Ketones contain a carbonyl carbon bonded to two carbon groups. The carbonyl bond is polar, with the carbon electrophilic and oxygen relatively electron-rich; this polarity explains many addition reactions.

Q2. How are aldehydes and ketones prepared?

Common synthetic routes include controlled oxidation of suitable alcohols (primary alcohols to aldehydes under suitable conditions; secondary alcohols to ketones), ozonolysis of alkenes, and selected transformations of acyl derivatives. The chosen method depends on the starting material, desired product and functional groups present.

Q3. State the main physical properties of carbonyl compounds.

The polar carbonyl group gives aldehydes and ketones dipole–dipole interactions. Their boiling points are generally higher than those of similar-sized non-polar hydrocarbons but lower than comparable alcohols because they cannot donate hydrogen bonds to each other. Smaller members can accept hydrogen bonds from water and may be water-soluble; solubility generally decreases as the carbon chain grows.

Q4. Explain nucleophilic addition to a carbonyl group.

A nucleophile attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon and the pi electrons shift toward oxygen, producing a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate. Protonation may then give an alcohol-containing product. Acid-catalysed reactions often activate the carbonyl oxygen first, while basic conditions can increase nucleophile strength. Aldehydes are often more reactive than ketones because they are less sterically hindered and less electron-rich.

Q5. What is aldol condensation?

Aldol reaction occurs when an aldehyde or ketone with at least one alpha hydrogen forms an enolate or enol that attacks another carbonyl group, creating a beta-hydroxy carbonyl compound. On dehydration under suitable conditions, an alpha,beta-unsaturated carbonyl compound may form. The reaction is important for forming carbon–carbon bonds.

Q6. What is crossed aldol condensation?

Crossed aldol condensation uses two different carbonyl compounds. If both can form enolates, mixtures of products may result. Selectivity can be improved when one partner has no alpha hydrogen or when one enolate is generated in a controlled way before adding the other carbonyl compound.

Q7. Explain the Cannizzaro reaction.

The Cannizzaro reaction is a base-promoted disproportionation of aldehydes that lack alpha hydrogen. One aldehyde molecule is oxidised to a carboxylate while another is reduced to an alcohol. It involves hydride transfer between aldehyde molecules under strongly basic conditions.

Q8. What is crossed Cannizzaro reaction?

Crossed Cannizzaro reaction uses two different aldehydes, often one aldehyde without alpha hydrogen and formaldehyde as a preferential hydride donor. The products can include an alcohol from one aldehyde and a carboxylate from the other. Product selectivity depends on the substrates and conditions.

Q9. Explain benzoin condensation.

Benzoin condensation couples two molecules of an aromatic aldehyde, traditionally catalysed by cyanide or a thiamine-derived catalyst, to form an alpha-hydroxy ketone such as benzoin. The catalyst enables an umpolung (polarity reversal) of the aldehyde carbon, allowing carbon–carbon bond formation. Cyanide is highly toxic and this reaction must never be attempted outside approved professional laboratory conditions.

Q10. What is the Perkin condensation?

The Perkin reaction forms an alpha,beta-unsaturated aromatic acid, classically by reacting an aromatic aldehyde with an acid anhydride in the presence of the corresponding carboxylate salt. It is a carbon–carbon bond-forming condensation used in the synthesis of cinnamic-acid-type compounds.

Q11. Describe oxidation and reduction reactions of aldehydes and ketones.

Aldehydes are readily oxidised to carboxylic acids under many conditions. Ketones resist mild oxidation, although strong conditions can cleave carbon–carbon bonds. Both aldehydes and ketones can be reduced to alcohols; aldehydes give primary alcohols and ketones give secondary alcohols. Stronger reductions can convert the carbonyl carbon to a methylene group.

Q12. What is chloral and what is its pharmaceutical relevance?

Chloral (trichloroacetaldehyde) is a chlorinated aldehyde historically used in the preparation of chloral hydrate, a sedative-hypnotic. It has significant safety limitations and is not a routine modern self-medication product. Its inclusion in this syllabus is mainly a medicinal-chemistry example; clinical use must follow current professional standards.

Q13. What is paraldehyde?

Paraldehyde is a cyclic trimer of acetaldehyde. It has been used historically as a sedative and anticonvulsant, but its use is now limited because of safety, formulation and availability concerns. It is useful in organic chemistry as an example of aldehyde polymerisation/cyclisation chemistry and in pharmaceutical history.

Q14. What is ketoprofen? Is it an aldehyde or ketone?

Ketoprofen is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) used for pain and inflammation in appropriate settings. Its structure contains a ketone carbonyl and a carboxylic acid group, so it is a pharmaceutical example containing a ketone functional group. It can cause adverse effects, including gastrointestinal and kidney problems, and should be used only according to suitable medical or pharmacy guidance.

Q15. Compare aldehydes and ketones in nucleophilic addition.

Aldehydes are generally more reactive than ketones toward nucleophilic addition because their carbonyl carbon is less sterically crowded and usually has less electron donation from carbon substituents. Formaldehyde is particularly reactive; the exact order depends on the nucleophile, solvent and other substituents.
