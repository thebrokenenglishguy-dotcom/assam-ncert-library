Unit I — Pharmacology of Drugs Acting on the Peripheral Nervous System

a) Organization and functions of the peripheral nervous system (PNS).

b) Neurohumoral transmission and co-transmission. Neurotransmitters and their receptors, including non-adrenergic and non-cholinergic (NANC) neurotransmitters.

c) Parasympathomimetic and parasympatholytic drugs.

d) Sympathomimetic and sympatholytic drugs.

e) Skeletal muscle relaxants (peripheral and central).

f) Drugs used in the treatment of myasthenia gravis and glaucoma.

Lecture hours: 10

Questions and Answers

Q1. Describe the organisation of the peripheral nervous system.

Answer: The peripheral nervous system includes sensory pathways and motor pathways. The motor division includes the somatic system, which controls skeletal muscle, and the autonomic system, which regulates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands through sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric pathways.

Q2. What is neurohumoral transmission?

Answer: Neurohumoral transmission is communication in which nerve cells release chemical messengers that act on receptors of target cells. It includes synthesis, storage, release, receptor binding, and termination of action by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion. Co-transmitters may modify the main transmitter’s effect.

Q3. Differentiate parasympathomimetic and parasympatholytic drugs.

Answer: Parasympathomimetics mimic acetylcholine or increase its action; examples include bethanechol and neostigmine. Parasympatholytics block muscarinic effects; atropine is an example. Their actions can affect heart rate, secretions, smooth muscle, pupils, and other organs.

Q4. What are neuromuscular blockers, and how are they different from central muscle relaxants?

Answer: Neuromuscular blockers act at the neuromuscular junction to prevent skeletal-muscle activation and are used in controlled anaesthesia settings. Central muscle relaxants reduce muscle tone through actions in the central nervous system. These classes have different indications and safety requirements.

Unit II — Pharmacology of Drugs Acting on the Cardiovascular System

a) Introduction to cardiovascular haemodynamics and cardiac electrophysiology.

b) Drugs used in congestive cardiac failure.

c) Anti-arrhythmic drugs.

d) Anti-anginal and newer anti-ischaemic drugs.

e) Anti-hypertensive drugs.

f) Shock, types of shocks, and drugs used in their management.

g) Stroke, types of strokes, and drugs used in their management.

Lecture hours: 10

Questions and Answers

Q1. What is cardiac output, and how is it related to haemodynamics?

Answer: Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute. It equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Haemodynamics also considers blood pressure, vascular resistance, preload, and afterload, all of which influence cardiovascular function.

Q2. Outline the major drug approaches in chronic heart failure.

Answer: Depending on the patient and heart-failure type, treatment may include ACE inhibitors/ARBs or ARNI, evidence-based beta blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, and diuretics for congestion. Other agents may be used in selected cases. Treatment requires clinical assessment and monitoring.

Q3. Classify anti-arrhythmic drugs.

Answer: The Vaughan Williams classes are Class I sodium-channel blockers, Class II beta blockers, Class III drugs that prolong repolarisation, and Class IV non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers. Some drugs have multiple mechanisms; selection depends on the arrhythmia and patient risk.

Q4. What is the pharmacological management principle for angina and hypertension?

Answer: Anti-anginal therapy aims to balance myocardial oxygen supply and demand using agents such as nitrates, beta blockers, or calcium-channel blockers. Antihypertensive treatment may use diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, calcium-channel blockers, and other agents. Drug selection depends on comorbidities and current guidelines.

Q5. What are shock and stroke?

Answer: Shock is life-threatening circulatory failure that causes inadequate tissue perfusion; types include hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, distributive, and obstructive. Stroke is acute neurological injury from interrupted blood flow or bleeding. Both require urgent emergency assessment; treatment depends on the cause and must not be delayed.

Unit III — Pharmacology of Drugs Acting on Blood and Kidney

1. Pharmacology of drugs acting on blood

a) Anti-platelet agents.

b) Coagulants and anticoagulants.

c) Fibrinolytics and plasma expanders.

d) Haematinics.

e) Anti-hyperlipidaemic drugs.

2. Pharmacology of drugs acting on kidney

a) Diuretics.

b) Anti-diuretics.

Lecture hours: 10

Questions and Answers

Q1. What is the role of antiplatelet drugs, anticoagulants, and fibrinolytics?

Answer: Antiplatelet drugs reduce platelet activation or aggregation. Anticoagulants inhibit the coagulation cascade. Fibrinolytics promote breakdown of fibrin in selected acute conditions. They have different indications and bleeding risks and must not be treated as interchangeable.

Q2. What are haematinics?

Answer: Haematinics are substances needed for red blood cell production, commonly iron, folate, and vitamin B12. They are used when a relevant deficiency or indication is established. The cause of anaemia should be evaluated rather than treating all anaemia in the same way.

Q3. How do antihyperlipidaemic drugs act?

Answer: Statins reduce cholesterol synthesis through HMG-CoA reductase inhibition; fibrates mainly lower triglycerides through PPAR-alpha activation; other classes can reduce cholesterol absorption or increase removal of LDL. Selection depends on lipid levels and cardiovascular risk.

Q4. Classify diuretics and explain their action in brief.

Answer: Loop diuretics inhibit the Na-K-2Cl transporter in the thick ascending limb; thiazides inhibit the Na-Cl transporter in the distal convoluted tubule; potassium-sparing agents reduce sodium reabsorption or block aldosterone; carbonic anhydrase inhibitors and osmotic diuretics act by other mechanisms. Antidiuretics, such as vasopressin analogues, reduce water loss in appropriate settings.

Unit IV — Pharmacology of Autacoids and Related Drugs

a) Introduction to autacoids and their classification. Therapeutic significance of important agonists and antagonists of prostaglandins, thromboxane, leukotrienes, angiotensin, bradykinin, and substance P.

b) Histamine and antihistamines.

c) 5-HT, its agonists and antagonists, and drugs used in migraine.

d) Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), antipyretics, and analgesics.

e) Anti-gout and antirheumatic drugs, including Disease Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs (DMARDs).

Lecture hours: 9

Questions and Answers

Q1. What are autacoids?

Answer: Autacoids are locally produced substances that act near their site of synthesis. Examples include histamine, prostaglandins, thromboxane, leukotrienes, serotonin, bradykinin, angiotensin, and substance P. They can influence inflammation, vascular tone, pain, allergy, and other responses.

Q2. How do H1 antihistamines differ from H2 antagonists?

Answer: H1 antihistamines reduce H1-mediated allergic symptoms such as sneezing and itching. H2 antagonists block histamine-driven gastric acid secretion. They act at different receptor types and are used for different clinical purposes.

Q3. What are NSAIDs, antipyretics, and analgesics?

Answer: NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin synthesis by inhibiting cyclooxygenase and can relieve pain, fever, and inflammation. Antipyretics reduce elevated body temperature; analgesics relieve pain. Some medicines have more than one of these effects, and safety depends on dose, duration, and patient factors.

Q4. What are DMARDs and anti-gout drugs?

Answer: Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) reduce disease activity and joint damage in inflammatory arthritis; they include conventional synthetic, biologic, and targeted synthetic agents. Anti-gout drugs may treat acute inflammation or lower urate over time. Choice and monitoring depend on the patient and current guidance.

Q5. How are serotonin-related drugs used in migraine?

Answer: Serotonin (5-HT) receptors influence vascular and neural pathways. Some migraine medicines, such as triptans, act at selected serotonin receptors to relieve attacks; preventive options may target other pathways. Appropriate use depends on diagnosis, contraindications, and clinical guidance.

Unit V — Pharmacology of Drugs Acting on the Respiratory and Immune Systems

i) Pharmacology of drugs acting on respiratory system

a) Drugs used in the treatment of bronchial asthma and COPD.

b) Definitions, classification, and therapeutic uses of nasal decongestants, mucolytics, expectorants, and antitussives.

c) Respiratory stimulants.

ii) Pharmacology of drugs acting on immune system

Mechanism of action, adverse effects, and therapeutic uses of important classes of immune stimulants and immunosuppressants.

Lecture hours: 6

Questions and Answers

Q1. Differentiate bronchodilators and anti-inflammatory drugs used in asthma or COPD.

Answer: Bronchodilators relax airway smooth muscle and improve airflow; examples include beta-2 agonists and muscarinic antagonists. Anti-inflammatory treatment, especially inhaled corticosteroids in appropriate patients, reduces airway inflammation. Asthma and COPD treatment plans differ and should follow current guidance.

Q2. Differentiate expectorants, mucolytics, antitussives, and nasal decongestants.

Answer: Expectorants help loosen or clear mucus; mucolytics reduce mucus viscosity; antitussives suppress cough when appropriate; nasal decongestants reduce nasal mucosal swelling. Their use depends on the cause of symptoms, patient age, contraindications, and duration.

Q3. What are respiratory stimulants?

Answer: Respiratory stimulants increase respiratory drive through central or peripheral mechanisms. Their uses are limited and context-specific because they may cause adverse effects and do not replace airway support or treatment of the underlying cause.

Q4. Differentiate immunostimulants and immunosuppressants.

Answer: Immunostimulants enhance selected immune functions and may be used in specific conditions. Immunosuppressants reduce immune activity, for example to prevent transplant rejection or treat autoimmune disease. Both require careful consideration of infection risk, monitoring, and indication.
