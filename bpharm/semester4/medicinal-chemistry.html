Questions and Answers

Q1. What is medicinal chemistry, and what is its scope?

Answer: Medicinal chemistry applies chemistry, biochemistry, and pharmacology to discover, design, modify, and study medicines. It examines relationships between chemical structure and biological activity, drug properties, metabolism, and interactions with biological targets.

Q2. How do ionisation and solubility affect drug action?

Answer: Ionisation depends on a drug’s acidic or basic groups and the pH of its environment. The ionised form is often more water-soluble, while the unionised form may cross lipid membranes more readily. The balance affects dissolution, absorption, distribution, and excretion.

Q3. What is the partition coefficient?

Answer: The partition coefficient describes how a compound distributes between two immiscible phases, commonly an organic phase and water. It gives an indication of lipophilicity, which can influence membrane passage, distribution, and formulation. For ionisable drugs, pH-dependent distribution is often described by logD.

Q4. Differentiate Phase I and Phase II drug metabolism.

Answer: Phase I reactions include oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis, often introducing or exposing a functional group. Phase II reactions conjugate a drug or metabolite with a group such as glucuronic acid or sulfate, often increasing water solubility and facilitating elimination. Either phase can alter activity or toxicity.

Questions and Answers

Q1. Differentiate sympathomimetic and sympatholytic drugs.

Answer: Sympathomimetic drugs increase or imitate adrenergic activity, for example salbutamol at beta-2 receptors or phenylephrine at alpha-1 receptors. Sympatholytic drugs reduce adrenergic activity, for example propranolol at beta receptors or prazosin at alpha-1 receptors.

Q2. What is structure–activity relationship (SAR)? Explain its importance for beta blockers.

Answer: SAR links chemical structure with biological activity. For beta blockers, changes in the aromatic region, oxypropanolamine side chain, and substituents can affect receptor affinity, selectivity, potency, and pharmacokinetic properties. SAR helps explain why related compounds have different profiles.

Q3. Differentiate cholinergic agonists and anticholinergic drugs.

Answer: Cholinergic agonists activate acetylcholine receptors or increase acetylcholine activity; examples include bethanechol and pilocarpine. Anticholinergic drugs block muscarinic receptors; examples include atropine, ipratropium, and tropicamide. Their effects depend on receptor subtype, dose, and site of action.

Q4. What is the basic pharmacophore of a local anaesthetic?

Answer: Most local anaesthetics contain a lipophilic aromatic region, an intermediate linkage (commonly ester or amide), and a hydrophilic amine. They reversibly block voltage-gated sodium channels, reducing nerve impulse conduction. Ester and amide agents differ in metabolism and allergy considerations.

Questions and Answers

Q1. Classify anti-anginal drugs and state their main actions.

Answer: Organic nitrates such as nitroglycerin reduce cardiac workload mainly by venodilation. Beta blockers reduce heart rate and contractility. Calcium-channel blockers reduce vascular tone and, for some agents, cardiac workload. Drug choice depends on patient factors and clinical guidance.

Q2. How do ACE inhibitors lower blood pressure?

Answer: ACE inhibitors such as captopril and enalapril reduce conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II. This lowers vasoconstriction and aldosterone release, reducing blood pressure. They can also increase bradykinin, which contributes to some adverse effects such as cough.

Q3. What is congestive heart failure, and which drug classes are used?

Answer: Heart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot meet the body’s needs without abnormal filling pressures. Depending on the type and patient, treatment may include renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, evidence-based beta blockers, diuretics, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, and selected other agents. Digoxin may be used in selected cases.

Q4. Classify anti-arrhythmic drugs using the Vaughan Williams system.

Answer: Class I drugs block fast sodium channels; Class II drugs are beta blockers; Class III drugs mainly prolong repolarisation through potassium-channel effects; Class IV drugs are non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers. Some clinically important drugs have actions outside a single class.

Questions and Answers

Q1. How do statins and fibrates reduce blood lipids?

Answer: Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase and reduce hepatic cholesterol synthesis, lowering LDL cholesterol. Fibrates activate PPAR-alpha and mainly reduce triglycerides while often increasing HDL. Choice depends on the lipid pattern, risk, interactions, and current guidelines.

Q2. Differentiate anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs.

Answer: Anticoagulants reduce the activity of the coagulation cascade, as warfarin does through vitamin K recycling and other agents through specific clotting targets. Antiplatelet drugs reduce platelet activation or aggregation, for example clopidogrel blocks the P2Y12 receptor. They are used for different indications and are not interchangeable.

Q3. Classify diuretics and give examples.

Answer: Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors include acetazolamide; thiazides include hydrochlorothiazide; loop diuretics include furosemide; potassium-sparing agents include spironolactone, amiloride, and triamterene; osmotic diuretics include mannitol. They act at different nephron sites and have different electrolyte effects.

Q4. Explain the structure–activity relationship of thiazide diuretics in brief.

Answer: Thiazide activity depends on key substitutions on the benzothiadiazine-related scaffold; electron-withdrawing groups and suitable substitution patterns can influence potency. Structural changes also affect duration and pharmacokinetics. The specific structures should be studied alongside the prescribed medicinal-chemistry text.

Questions and Answers

Q1. Differentiate H1 and H2 antihistamines.

Answer: H1 antagonists or inverse agonists reduce histamine-mediated effects such as itching, sneezing, and allergic symptoms; examples include diphenhydramine and cetirizine. H2 blockers reduce histamine-stimulated gastric acid secretion; examples include famotidine and cimetidine.

Q2. What is the mechanism of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)?

Answer: PPIs such as omeprazole and pantoprazole suppress gastric acid secretion by inhibiting the gastric parietal-cell H+/K+-ATPase. They are used in acid-related disorders. Their activation and duration of action depend on drug properties and the acidic secretory environment.

Q3. What is the general mechanism of NSAIDs?

Answer: Most NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes and reduce prostaglandin synthesis, producing anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and antipyretic effects. Risks may include gastrointestinal injury, renal effects, fluid retention, and cardiovascular events; the risk differs by drug and patient.

Q4. What is SAR, and how can it be applied to NSAIDs?

Answer: SAR examines how structural changes affect activity and properties. In NSAIDs, acidic groups and hydrophobic regions commonly contribute to cyclooxygenase binding, while substituents influence potency, selectivity, metabolism, and adverse effects. COX-2-selective agents have distinct structural features.
