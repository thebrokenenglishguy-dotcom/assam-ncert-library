Unit I — Introduction to Social Pharmacy and Public Health

a) Social Pharmacy: Definition, scope, historical development, and importance. Social pharmacy as a multidisciplinary field.

b) Public Health: Definition, concepts, history, core functions, and ethical considerations.

c) Interplay between Social Pharmacy and Public Health: The evolving role of the pharmacist in the public health arena.

d) Concept of Health and Disease: WHO definition of health; dimensions of health — physical, mental, social, spiritual, and environmental.

e) Determinants of Health: Social, economic, environmental, lifestyle, and healthcare-service determinants and their impact on population health.

f) Health Indicators: Indicators used to measure health status and health outcomes in a population.

Lecture hours: 6

Questions and Answers

Q1. Define social pharmacy and explain its scope.

Answer: Social pharmacy studies how social, behavioural, economic, cultural, and health-system factors affect the use of medicines and pharmacy services. It includes medicine access, adherence, health education, public-health promotion, medicine safety, and the pharmacist’s role in communities.

Q2. Define public health and list its core functions.

Answer: Public health is the organised effort of society to protect and improve population health. Core functions commonly include assessment of population health, policy development, and assurance that needed services are available. Ethical practice balances benefit, fairness, autonomy, privacy, and responsible use of resources.

Q3. How does the WHO concept of health differ from simply being free of disease?

Answer: The WHO definition describes health as a state of physical, mental, and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. In practice, health is multidimensional and is also influenced by environmental, spiritual, social, and economic circumstances.

Q4. What are determinants of health and health indicators?

Answer: Determinants include income, education, housing, environment, lifestyle, social support, and access to healthcare. Health indicators are measurable values—such as life expectancy, infant mortality, disease incidence, or vaccination coverage—used to assess population health and changes over time.

Unit II — Health Systems, Policy, and Pharmacoepidemiology

a) Healthcare Delivery Systems: Overview of global healthcare systems.

b) Indian Healthcare System: Structure, public and private sectors, and primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of care.

c) Health Policy: Introduction to health-policy formulation and analysis; objectives of India’s National Health Policy.

d) National Health Mission (NHM): NRHM and NUHM — goals, strategies, and impact on public-health indicators.

e) Pharmacoepidemiology: Definition, aims, scope, and applications.

f) Measures of Disease Frequency and Distribution: Incidence, prevalence, endemic, epidemic, pandemic; morbidity and mortality rates, odds ratio, and relative risk.

g) Introduction to Biostatistics: Role in public health; types of data; basic data-presentation methods.

Lecture hours: 6

Questions and Answers

Q1. Describe the levels of healthcare in India.

Answer: Primary care is the first point of contact and covers basic, preventive, and continuing services. Secondary care provides specialist services, often through district hospitals. Tertiary care provides advanced specialist and super-specialist services. Public and private providers both contribute.

Q2. What are the aims of India’s National Health Policy and the National Health Mission?

Answer: National health policy sets broad priorities for improving health, access, quality, and financial protection. The National Health Mission, including rural and urban components, supports accessible and affordable healthcare, strengthening services and improving public-health outcomes. Use current official documents for exact targets.

Q3. Define pharmacoepidemiology, incidence, and prevalence.

Answer: Pharmacoepidemiology studies the use and effects of medicines in large populations. Incidence counts new cases arising in a population at risk during a stated period. Prevalence counts all existing cases in a population at a particular time or over a stated period.

Q4. Differentiate relative risk and odds ratio.

Answer: Relative risk compares the probability of an outcome in an exposed group with that in an unexposed group. An odds ratio compares the odds of an outcome between groups. Both describe association and must be interpreted with study design, confidence intervals, and possible bias in mind.

Unit III — Preventive Healthcare, Health Promotion, and Communicable Diseases

a) Levels of Prevention: Primordial, primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention with examples.

b) Role of Pharmacists in Disease Prevention and Health Promotion: Immunization services, screening programs, and lifestyle counselling.

c) Health Education: Definition, principles, methods, and importance; development of effective health-education materials.

d) Mother and Child Health (MCH): Antenatal care, postnatal care, breastfeeding, and immunization schedules.

e) Communicable Diseases: Modes of transmission, prevention, and control of tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS, malaria, dengue, typhoid, and influenza; pharmacist’s role.

Lecture hours: 6

Questions and Answers

Q1. Explain the four levels of disease prevention.

Answer: Primordial prevention prevents the emergence of risk factors; primary prevention prevents disease before it occurs, for example through vaccination; secondary prevention detects disease early through screening; tertiary prevention reduces complications and disability after disease is established.

Q2. What roles can pharmacists play in health promotion?

Answer: Pharmacists can provide medicine counselling, support vaccination where authorised, encourage screening, promote healthy lifestyles, check medicine safety, and refer people with warning signs. Activities must follow local laws, training, and professional standards.

Q3. What are the key elements of maternal and child health services?

Answer: They include antenatal care, skilled care at birth, postnatal follow-up, breastfeeding support, nutrition, growth monitoring, immunisation, and early identification of complications. Exact schedules should follow current national guidance.

Q4. How are communicable diseases transmitted and controlled?

Answer: Transmission may occur through air or droplets, contaminated food or water, vectors, blood, or sexual contact. Control measures depend on the disease and may include vaccination, sanitation, vector control, early diagnosis, appropriate treatment, infection prevention, and health education. Examples in the syllabus include TB, HIV/AIDS, malaria, dengue, typhoid, and influenza.

Unit IV — Non-Communicable Diseases, Nutrition, Mental Health, and National Programs

a) Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs): Diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, and cancer; prevention and management.

b) Nutrition and Health: Balanced diet, macro- and micronutrients, malnutrition, nutritional-deficiency disorders, food safety, and adulteration.

c) Mental Health: Common mental disorders, stigma, promotion of mental well-being; pharmacist’s role.

d) National Health Programs in India: Programs related to MCH, NCDs, communicable diseases, tobacco control, and deafness prevention.

Lecture hours: 6

Questions and Answers

Q1. Differentiate communicable and non-communicable diseases.

Answer: Communicable diseases are caused by infectious agents that can spread directly or indirectly. Non-communicable diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory disease, and cancer are generally long-term and arise from interacting genetic, behavioural, and environmental factors.

Q2. What is a balanced diet, and what are macronutrients and micronutrients?

Answer: A balanced diet provides energy and nutrients in appropriate amounts for health and activity. Macronutrients—carbohydrates, proteins, and fats—are needed in larger amounts. Micronutrients—vitamins and minerals—are needed in smaller amounts but are essential for normal body functions.

Q3. How can pharmacists support mental health?

Answer: Pharmacists can provide respectful counselling, support safe and appropriate medicine use, recognise potential adverse effects, encourage adherence, reduce stigma, and refer people for professional assessment when needed. They should protect confidentiality and avoid diagnosing beyond their scope.

Q4. Why are national health programmes important?

Answer: They organise resources and services around priority health needs, such as maternal and child health, communicable diseases, NCDs, tobacco control, and prevention of hearing impairment. They can improve coverage and equity through coordinated prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up.

Unit V — Pharmacoeconomics, Rational Use of Medicines, Professional Roles, and Future Trends

a) Pharmacoeconomics: Introduction, significance; Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA), Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA), and Cost-Utility Analysis (CUA).

b) Rational Use of Medicines (RUM): Definition, importance, problems of irrational drug use; pharmacist’s role.

c) Medication Adherence: Factors affecting adherence, consequences of non-adherence, and improvement strategies.

d) Drug Misuse and Abuse: Alcohol, tobacco, opioids, prescription-drug abuse; pharmacist’s role.

e) Professionalism and Ethics in Social Pharmacy: Ethical dilemmas in public-health pharmacy.

f) Disaster Management: Role of pharmacists.

g) Emerging Trends: Telepharmacy, digital health, personalized medicine, and expanding public-health responsibilities of pharmacists.

Lecture hours: 6

Questions and Answers

Q1. Differentiate CBA, CEA, and CUA in pharmacoeconomics.

Answer: Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) expresses both costs and benefits in monetary terms. Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) compares costs with outcomes in natural units, such as cases prevented. Cost-utility analysis (CUA) commonly uses outcomes such as quality-adjusted life years. The method should match the decision question.

Q2. What is rational use of medicines?

Answer: Rational use means patients receive medicines appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet individual requirements, for an adequate period, and at the lowest appropriate cost to them and the community. Irrational use includes unnecessary antibiotics, incorrect dosing, and unsafe self-medication.

Q3. What factors affect medication adherence, and how can it be improved?

Answer: Adherence may be affected by cost, adverse effects, complex regimens, beliefs, low health literacy, forgetfulness, and poor access. Strategies include clear counselling, simplified regimens where appropriate, reminders, follow-up, shared decisions, and addressing affordability or adverse effects.

Q4. What are telepharmacy, disaster management, and professional ethics in pharmacy?

Answer: Telepharmacy uses digital communication to support pharmacy services at a distance within applicable rules. In disasters, pharmacists help maintain essential medicine supplies, safe storage, dispensing, and public information. Professional ethics require competence, confidentiality, honesty, respect, and prioritising patient welfare.
