Lecture hours: 9

Questions and Answers

Q1. What are therapeutic proteins?

Answer: Therapeutic proteins are proteins used to prevent, diagnose, or treat disease. Examples include insulin, some hormones, clotting factors, and monoclonal antibodies. Their quality depends on identity, purity, folding, biological activity, stability, and control of contaminants.

Q2. How are proteins characterised for pharmaceutical use?

Answer: Protein characterisation may examine molecular mass, sequence, purity, charge variants, higher-order structure, biological potency, aggregation, and impurities. Methods can include electrophoresis, chromatography, mass spectrometry, spectroscopy, and bioassays, selected according to the protein and quality requirement.

Q3. What are monoclonal antibodies and antigens?

Answer: An antigen is a substance recognised by components of the immune system. A monoclonal antibody is a population of highly similar antibodies that bind a particular epitope. Therapeutic antibodies may neutralise targets, block receptors, or recruit immune functions.

Q4. What is enzyme immobilisation, and why is it used?

Answer: Enzyme immobilisation attaches or confines an enzyme to a support or matrix while retaining useful activity. It can improve reuse, separation, and process control, and may increase stability. Methods include adsorption, covalent binding, entrapment, and cross-linking.

  • Production of interferons, hepatitis-B vaccine, and insulin
  • Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and its applications

Lecture hours: 9

Questions and Answers

Q1. What is recombinant DNA technology?

Answer: Recombinant DNA technology joins selected DNA sequences and introduces them into a suitable host or system to express or study a desired gene product. Core steps include gene selection, DNA preparation, vector construction, transfer into a host, selection, expression, and product purification.

Q2. Explain the principle and applications of PCR.

Answer: Polymerase chain reaction amplifies a selected DNA region through repeated cycles of denaturation, primer annealing, and extension by a DNA polymerase. It is used in diagnosis, research, identity testing, mutation analysis, and confirmation of genetic material.

Q3. How is recombinant human insulin produced in principle?

Answer: The insulin gene or a suitable coding sequence is placed in an expression system, commonly a genetically engineered microorganism. The expressed product is recovered, processed if needed, purified, and tested for identity, potency, purity, and safety under controlled manufacturing conditions.

Q4. What are mutations and mutants?

Answer: A mutation is a change in genetic material. Mutations may involve a single base, larger DNA segments, or chromosome structure or number. A mutant is an organism or cell carrying a mutation. Mutations may be spontaneous or induced and can be harmful, neutral, or beneficial.

Lecture hours: 9

Questions and Answers

Q1. What is microbial biotransformation?

Answer: Microbial biotransformation uses microorganisms or their enzymes to convert a substrate into a different chemical product. It can offer selectivity under relatively mild conditions and is useful in producing or modifying pharmaceutical compounds.

Q2. Describe the main stages of industrial fermentation.

Answer: Typical stages include strain selection and improvement, preparation of inoculum, medium formulation and sterilisation, controlled fermentation, monitoring of pH, temperature, aeration and agitation, product recovery, purification, and quality testing.

Q3. How are penicillin and vitamin B12 produced industrially in broad terms?

Answer: Selected microorganisms are cultivated in optimised fermentation media under controlled conditions. The product is then separated and purified using suitable downstream processes. Exact strains, media, operating conditions, and yields depend on the manufacturing process.

Q4. How are blood products collected and stored safely?

Answer: Blood collection uses screened donors, sterile equipment, correct identification, and validated collection procedures. Components are separated when appropriate, tested, labelled, and stored under product-specific temperature and time limits. Traceability and prevention of contamination are essential.

Lecture hours: 9

Questions and Answers

Q1. What is gene therapy? Name its main types.

Answer: Gene therapy aims to treat disease by introducing, removing, or modifying genetic material in cells. Broad approaches include adding a functional gene, silencing a harmful gene, or editing genetic material. Delivery may be in vivo directly to the patient or ex vivo after cells are modified outside the body.

Q2. What are viral and non-viral gene-delivery systems?

Answer: Viral vectors use modified viruses to deliver genetic material and can be efficient, but immune responses and vector-related risks must be managed. Non-viral systems include lipid particles, polymers, and physical methods; they may offer flexibility but can have delivery-efficiency limitations.

Q3. What was the Human Genome Project, and why is genomics important?

Answer: The Human Genome Project produced a reference sequence and maps of human genetic information. Genomics studies the genome and its variation. It supports research into disease mechanisms, risk, drug response, and potential personalised treatment.

Q4. How can bioinformatics and AI support personalised medicine?

Answer: Bioinformatics analyses biological data such as sequences and gene-expression profiles. AI can help identify patterns, predict risks, or support treatment selection when trained and validated on suitable data. Both require reliable data, privacy protection, bias assessment, and clinical validation.

Questions and Answers

Q1. Differentiate innate and adaptive immunity.

Answer: Innate immunity provides rapid, broadly targeted defence through barriers, phagocytes, complement, and other mechanisms. Adaptive immunity is antigen-specific and involves B cells, antibodies, and T cells; it can develop immunological memory.

Q2. Differentiate live-attenuated, inactivated, subunit, and newer vaccines.

Answer: Live-attenuated vaccines contain weakened organisms; inactivated vaccines contain killed organisms; subunit vaccines contain selected antigens. Other platforms include toxoid, viral-vector, and nucleic-acid vaccines. Selection depends on disease, safety, immune response, age, and programme guidance.

Q3. What are the main stages in vaccine preparation and evaluation?

Answer: Stages include antigen or platform selection, development, controlled production, purification or formulation, quality testing, preclinical assessment where applicable, clinical trials, regulatory review, and ongoing safety and effectiveness monitoring.

Q4. What is sera therapy, and how is it different from vaccination?

Answer: Sera therapy provides ready-made antibodies or antitoxins for more immediate, usually temporary protection or treatment. Vaccination stimulates the recipient’s own immune response and may create memory. Sera can cause reactions and must be used according to approved indications and guidance.
