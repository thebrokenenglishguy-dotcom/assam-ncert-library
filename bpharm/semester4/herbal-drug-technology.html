Questions and Answers

Q1. What is plant tissue culture, and why is it useful in herbal drug production?

Answer: Plant tissue culture is the growth of plant cells, tissues, or organs under sterile, controlled conditions on a suitable nutrient medium. It can provide a controlled source of medicinal plant material, support year-round production, conserve rare plants, and help produce valuable secondary metabolites.

Q2. Differentiate callus culture and suspension culture.

Answer: Callus culture grows an unorganised mass of plant cells on a usually solid medium. Suspension culture grows dispersed cells or small cell aggregates in a liquid medium, usually with agitation. Suspension cultures can be scaled up in bioreactors for metabolite production.

Q3. Explain elicitation, precursor feeding, and biotransformation.

Answer: Elicitation uses a biological or chemical signal to stimulate metabolite production. Precursor feeding supplies a starting compound in a biosynthetic pathway. Biotransformation uses plant cells, enzymes, or microorganisms to convert a compound into a desired product.

Q4. What are Shikonin and Paclitaxel in plant biomanufacturing?

Answer: Shikonin is a plant-derived naphthoquinone pigment with biological activity. Paclitaxel is an anticancer drug originally isolated from yew trees. They are examples of valuable plant-derived compounds that can be produced or investigated using plant cell culture and controlled biomanufacturing.

Teaching hours: 10

Questions and Answers

Q1. What are conventional herbal formulations? Give examples.

Answer: Conventional herbal formulations use familiar dosage forms to deliver herbal ingredients. Examples include syrups, mixtures, powders, capsules, tablets, creams, and ointments. Their quality depends on suitable ingredients, dose uniformity, stability, and microbial quality.

Q2. What are phytosomes, liposomes, and nanoformulations?

Answer: Phytosomes are complexes of plant constituents, commonly with phospholipids, to improve interaction with biological membranes. Liposomes are vesicles made from lipid bilayers that can carry suitable drugs or extracts. Nanoformulations use nanoscale carriers or particles to alter delivery, stability, or absorption.

Q3. What are natural-origin pharmaceutical excipients?

Answer: They are supporting ingredients obtained from natural sources. Examples include natural gums as binders or viscosity builders, starch as a disintegrant, plant-derived colours and flavours, and waxes as protective agents. They must still meet identity, purity, safety, and quality requirements.

Q4. Describe the uses of herbal cosmetics.

Answer: Herbal cosmetics use plant-derived ingredients in skin care, hair care, and oral hygiene. Examples include sunscreen lotions or gels, hair oils, shampoos, herbal dyes, mouthwashes, gargles, face serums, and face packs. Formulations should be assessed for safety, stability, and contamination.

Unit III — Quality Control and Standardisation of Herbal Medicines
  • WHO, AYUSH, and EU guidelines on quality control, stability, and shelf-life studies of herbal medicines
  • Approaches for standardisation and quality control of botanicals and formulations
  • Testing methods and regulatory considerations
  • Role of DNA fingerprinting and molecular markers such as rbcL, matK, and SCAR in quality control
  • Forensic pharmacognosy: role in identification of illicit herbal drugs (e.g., Cannabis and Opium)

Teaching hours: 7

Questions and Answers

Q1. Why is standardisation important for herbal medicines?

Answer: Herbal medicines can vary because of species, growing conditions, harvesting, storage, and extraction. Standardisation uses defined identity tests, analytical methods, marker compounds, and quality limits to improve batch-to-batch consistency, safety, and efficacy.

Q2. What roles do WHO, AYUSH, and EU guidelines play in herbal quality control?

Answer: They provide frameworks or requirements for quality, safety, stability, manufacturing, and documentation. The exact rules differ by jurisdiction and product category, so manufacturers must follow the applicable current official requirements.

Q3. What are DNA fingerprinting and molecular markers in botanical identification?

Answer: DNA fingerprinting compares genetic patterns to help identify or distinguish plant material. Markers such as rbcL, matK, and SCAR can support authentication, especially when physical appearance is insufficient. DNA results complement rather than replace chemical and other quality tests.

Q4. What is forensic pharmacognosy?

Answer: Forensic pharmacognosy applies pharmacognostic and analytical methods to identify plant-derived materials in legal or investigative contexts. It may help examine suspected illicit herbal drugs such as Cannabis or opium, while conclusions should rely on validated methods and appropriate chain of custody.

Unit IV — Herb–Drug / Food / Herb Interactions
  • General introduction to interactions and role of ADME, Cytochrome P450, and P-gp
  • Herb–drug interactions:
    • St. John’s Wort with warfarin
    • Ginkgo biloba with aspirin
  • Herb–food interactions:
    • Licorice with salty foods
    • Turmeric with fats
    • Green tea with iron-rich foods
  • Herb–herb interactions:
    • Ephedra with Ginseng
    • Chamomile with Valerian
  • Adverse reactions related to plants and foods, such as allergy, intolerance, and toxicity

Teaching hours: 10

Questions and Answers

Q1. What factors affect herb–drug interactions?

Answer: Interactions may change absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion (ADME). Important mechanisms include effects on cytochrome P450 enzymes and P-glycoprotein (P-gp), as well as additive pharmacological effects that increase toxicity or reduce treatment effectiveness.

Q2. Explain the clinical concern with St John’s wort and warfarin.

Answer: St John’s wort can induce drug-metabolising enzymes and transporters, potentially lowering exposure to some medicines and reducing their effects. With warfarin, changes can destabilise anticoagulation. Patients should not start or stop the herb without professional review and appropriate monitoring.

Q3. Give examples of herb–food and herb–herb interactions from the syllabus.

Answer: Licorice with salty foods may worsen sodium retention and blood pressure. Turmeric constituents may be absorbed differently with dietary fats. Green tea can reduce absorption of non-haem iron. Ephedra with ginseng may increase stimulant-related effects, while chamomile with valerian may add to sedation.

Q4. What are adverse reactions to plants and foods?

Answer: They include allergy, intolerance, and toxicity. Allergy involves an immune response; intolerance is a non-immune adverse response; toxicity results from harmful substances or excessive exposure. Assessment considers the product, dose, timing, other medicines, and patient factors.

Teaching hours: 8

Questions and Answers

Q1. What is the regulatory framework for herbal and ASU medicines in India?

Answer: Herbal and Ayurveda, Siddha, and Unani (ASU) medicines are regulated under applicable Indian drug laws and rules. Requirements depend on product category and may cover licensing, quality, labelling, manufacturing, sale, registration, and export. Current official rules should be checked before regulatory decisions.

Q2. What are ASU DTAB, ASU DCC, Schedule T, and Schedule E1?

Answer: ASU DTAB advises on technical matters concerning ASU drugs; ASU DCC supports consultation and coordination on regulatory matters. Schedule T specifies good manufacturing practice requirements for ASU medicines. Schedule E1 lists specified poisonous substances used in ASU systems and requires relevant precautions under applicable rules.

Q3. What are phytopharmaceuticals?

Answer: Phytopharmaceuticals are plant-derived products developed and characterised using modern scientific and regulatory approaches. Their development requires defined composition, quality control, safety evaluation, and evidence appropriate to the intended use.

Q4. What is Ayush Aahara?

Answer: Ayush Aahara refers to food products associated with traditional AYUSH knowledge and governed by applicable food regulations. It is distinct from a medicine claim; product classification, permitted claims, ingredients, and labelling must follow current rules.
