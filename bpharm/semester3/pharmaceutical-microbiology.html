Unit I — Introduction and Role of Microorganisms in Pharmaceutical Industry

Q1. What is microbiology?

Answer: The study of microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, protozoa and microscopic algae.

Q2. Why is microbiology important in pharmacy?

Answer: It supports antibiotic and biologic production, contamination control, infection prevention and quality testing of medicines.

Q3. Differentiate bacteria, fungi and viruses.

Answer: Bacteria are prokaryotic cells; fungi are eukaryotes; viruses are acellular agents that replicate only inside suitable host cells.

Q4. What is microbial cultivation?

Answer: Growing microorganisms in or on a nutrient medium under controlled conditions.

Q5. What is a culture medium?

Answer: A preparation supplying nutrients and conditions for microbial growth; it may be liquid, semisolid or solid.

Q6. Differentiate selective and differential media.

Answer: Selective media inhibit some organisms while supporting others. Differential media distinguish organisms by visible reactions.

Q7. What is aseptic technique?

Answer: Practices that prevent unwanted microorganisms from entering a culture, product or work area.

Q8. What is microbial isolation?

Answer: Separating an organism from a mixed population to obtain a distinct colony or culture, often by streak plating.

Q9. What is microbial enumeration?

Answer: Estimating microorganism numbers using methods such as viable plate count, membrane filtration or validated counting techniques.

Q10. What is a colony-forming unit (CFU)?

Answer: A unit estimating viable microorganisms able to form colonies under test conditions; a colony may arise from a single cell or a cluster.

Q11. Differentiate compound and electron microscopes.

Answer: A compound microscope uses visible light and glass lenses. An electron microscope uses an electron beam and can reveal finer detail.

Q12. What is Gram staining?

Answer: A differential stain: Gram-positive bacteria usually appear purple and Gram-negative bacteria pink or red after counterstaining.

Q13. What is acid-fast staining?

Answer: A method for organisms with waxy, mycolic-acid-rich cell walls, such as many mycobacteria, which resist acid-alcohol decolourisation.

Q14. What is IMViC testing?

Answer: Indole, Methyl Red, Voges–Proskauer and Citrate tests; the combined pattern helps differentiate certain enteric bacteria.

Q15. How are antibiotics produced by microorganisms?

Answer: Selected strains are grown under controlled fermentation conditions, followed by recovery, purification and quality testing of the antibiotic.

Q16. What is streptomycin?

Answer: An aminoglycoside antibiotic originally obtained from Streptomyces griseus; it acts on the bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit.

Q17. What are cephalosporins?

Answer: Beta-lactam antibiotics that inhibit bacterial cell-wall synthesis; manufacturing commonly uses fermentation-derived starting materials and further processing.

Q18. Why is pure culture important?

Answer: It allows reliable identification and controlled study without interference from other organisms.

Q19. Name methods used to identify bacteria.

Answer: Colony morphology, microscopy, staining, biochemical tests, antigen tests, mass spectrometry and molecular methods such as PCR.

Q20. Differentiate bacterial morphology and biochemical identification.

Answer: Morphology examines cell shape and arrangement; biochemical tests assess metabolic reactions and enzyme activities.

Unit II — Evaluation of Microbiological Contamination

Q21. What is microbial contamination?

Answer: Unwanted microorganisms present in raw materials, equipment, products, water, air or manufacturing environments.

Q22. Name common sources of contamination.

Answer: Personnel, air, water, raw materials, equipment, premises, packaging, cleaning tools and poor process practices.

Q23. Differentiate intrinsic and extrinsic sources.

Answer: Intrinsic sources originate within the material or product; extrinsic sources enter from outside during processing, handling or storage.

Q24. How can air introduce contamination?

Answer: Air carries microorganisms in droplets and particles from people, movement, ventilation or disturbed dust.

Q25. Why is pharmaceutical water monitored?

Answer: Water can support microbial growth and transfer organisms or endotoxins into products, so quality must suit its intended use.

Q26. How can personnel reduce contamination?

Answer: Follow hygiene, gowning, hand hygiene, controlled movement, training and aseptic practices appropriate to the area.

Q27. How is contamination controlled during nonsterile manufacture?

Answer: Use suitable facility design, cleaning, disinfection, qualified utilities, controlled raw materials, monitoring and good manufacturing practices.

Q28. What is an aseptic area?

Answer: A controlled environment designed to minimise microbial and particulate contamination when sterile products or components are exposed.

Q29. Why are cleaning and maintenance important?

Answer: Residues and damaged surfaces can harbour microorganisms; effective cleaning and maintenance reduce contamination and cross-contamination.

Q30. What is microbial spoilage?

Answer: Microbial growth or activity that changes a product's quality, safety, appearance, odour, potency or performance.

Q31. Name factors affecting spoilage.

Answer: Water activity, pH, temperature, nutrients, oxygen, preservative effectiveness, packaging and initial microbial load.

Q32. Name possible signs of microbial spoilage.

Answer: Turbidity, gas, colour or odour changes, pH changes, visible growth, separation or loss of performance; testing is needed to confirm the cause.

Q33. What is fermentation?

Answer: A controlled process using microorganisms or cells to produce substances such as antibiotics, enzymes, organic acids or biomass.

Q34. Differentiate batch and continuous fermentation.

Answer: Batch fermentation runs for a defined period after charging ingredients. Continuous fermentation has fresh medium entering and culture leaving during operation.

Q35. What is a fermenter?

Answer: A vessel supporting controlled microbial or cell growth with mixing, aeration, temperature control and monitoring.

Q36. Name common parts of a stirred-tank fermenter.

Answer: Vessel, agitator, baffles, sparger, sensors, sterile lines, sampling ports and temperature-control jacket or coil.

Q37. What is a sparger?

Answer: A device that introduces air or another gas into a fermenter, often to support oxygen transfer.

Q38. What is bioburden?

Answer: The population of viable microorganisms on or in a material or product before a specified microbial reduction or sterilisation step.

Q39. What is environmental monitoring?

Answer: Sampling air, surfaces, personnel or other environmental points to assess microbial or particulate conditions.

Q40. Why investigate contamination root causes?

Answer: Root-cause investigation identifies how contamination occurred and supports corrective and preventive action.

Unit III — Microbial Control and Evaluation

Q41. What is microbial control?

Answer: Reducing, removing or preventing microorganisms to a level appropriate for the product, process and risk.

Q42. What is laminar airflow equipment?

Answer: Equipment supplying filtered, controlled, generally unidirectional air to protect a defined work zone; it must be correctly qualified and used.

Q43. What is HEPA filtration?

Answer: High-efficiency particulate air filtration removes a high proportion of particles of a specified test size and helps control airborne particles carrying microbes.

Q44. What is clean-area classification?

Answer: Grading an environment by measured airborne particle concentrations under specified conditions; microbial monitoring is also important.

Q45. What is a biological safety level (BSL)?

Answer: A set of containment practices, safety equipment and facility requirements matched to the risk of biological work.

Q46. Name the four commonly described biosafety levels.

Answer: BSL-1, BSL-2, BSL-3 and BSL-4, from basic to highest containment; selection depends on agent risk and guidance.

Q47. Differentiate antiseptics and disinfectants.

Answer: Antiseptics are used on living tissue; disinfectants are used on inanimate surfaces or objects.

Q48. What is a pharmaceutical preservative?

Answer: An ingredient intended to inhibit microbial growth during storage or use; it does not replace good manufacturing practice or guarantee sterility.

Q49. What is a sporicide?

Answer: An agent capable of inactivating bacterial spores under defined conditions.

Q50. Name factors affecting disinfectant activity.

Answer: Concentration, contact time, temperature, pH, organic matter, microbial type, surface and preparation method.

Q51. What is the phenol coefficient?

Answer: A historical comparison of disinfectant activity with phenol under specified conditions; modern methods often use other standardised tests.

Q52. What is a use-dilution test?

Answer: A test assessing a disinfectant at its use concentration against microorganisms on carriers under defined conditions.

Q53. What is a suspension test?

Answer: Microorganisms are exposed to a disinfectant for a defined time, the action is neutralised, and survivors are assessed.

Q54. Why validate disinfectant neutralisation?

Answer: Residual disinfectant could continue killing microbes during recovery and falsely exaggerate efficacy if not neutralised.

Q55. Differentiate cleaning and disinfection.

Answer: Cleaning removes soil and residues; disinfection inactivates many microorganisms on surfaces. Cleaning is often needed first.

Q56. What is an antimicrobial effectiveness test?

Answer: A test evaluating whether an antimicrobial agent or preservative performs as intended against specified microorganisms.

Q57. Why select disinfectants based on evidence?

Answer: Selection should consider demonstrated efficacy, compatibility, contact time, safety and facility procedures.

Q58. What is contamination prevention?

Answer: A combination of facility design, personnel practices, cleaning, disinfection, suitable utilities, monitoring and validated processes.

Q59. Why document clean-area procedures?

Answer: Documentation supports consistent execution, training, traceability, investigations and evidence that controls remain effective.

Q60. Why is disinfectant contact time important?

Answer: The surface must remain exposed for the specified time for the validated antimicrobial effect to be achieved.

Unit IV — Sterilization Procedures, Assurance and Evaluation

Q61. What is sterilisation?

Answer: A validated process intended to render a product free from viable microorganisms, including resistant forms such as bacterial spores.

Q62. Differentiate sterilisation and disinfection.

Answer: Sterilisation targets all viable microbial forms within the validated process scope; disinfection reduces specified microbes but may not destroy spores.

Q63. What is moist-heat sterilisation?

Answer: A method, commonly using saturated steam under pressure, that inactivates microbes mainly through protein denaturation and related damage.

Q64. What is an autoclave?

Answer: Equipment using saturated steam at controlled temperature, pressure and time to sterilise compatible loads.

Q65. Why is air removal important in steam sterilisation?

Answer: Trapped air can prevent effective steam contact and create cold spots, compromising the process.

Q66. What is dry-heat sterilisation?

Answer: Hot-air treatment that inactivates microorganisms mainly through oxidative and thermal damage; it suits compatible materials such as some glassware and oils.

Q67. What is sterilising filtration?

Answer: A suitable filter removes microorganisms from a heat-sensitive liquid or gas. The filter and process require validation; filtration may not remove all viruses or endotoxins.

Q68. What is radiation sterilisation?

Answer: Ionising radiation, such as gamma rays or electron beams, can sterilise compatible products by damaging microbial nucleic acids and other structures.

Q69. What is gas sterilisation?

Answer: A validated gas or vapour process, such as ethylene oxide for compatible products, sterilises items that cannot tolerate some heat or moisture conditions.

Q70. What is a sterilisation indicator?

Answer: An indicator provides evidence about exposure to a sterilisation process; chemical indicators monitor process variables and biological indicators challenge microbial lethality.

Q71. Differentiate chemical and biological indicators.

Answer: Chemical indicators respond to specified process variables but do not prove sterility. Biological indicators use defined viable challenge organisms.

Q72. What is sterilisation validation?

Answer: Documented evidence that a sterilisation process consistently meets predetermined requirements for the specified load, equipment and operating conditions.

Q73. What is sterility assurance level (SAL)?

Answer: A probability-based expression of viable microorganism survival after a sterilisation process; a commonly cited target for some terminally sterilised products is 10⁻⁶ when applicable.

Q74. What is bioburden determination?

Answer: Estimating viable microorganisms on or in a product before sterilisation using a suitable recovery method.

Q75. What is a D-value?

Answer: Decimal reduction time: the time at a specified condition needed to reduce a microbial population by 90%, or one log₁₀, under the model used.

Q76. What is a z-value?

Answer: The temperature change needed to change a thermal D-value by a factor of ten under a specified model.

Q77. What is microbial death kinetics?

Answer: The description of how viable microbial numbers decline over time during a control or sterilisation process.

Q78. Differentiate bacteriostatic and bactericidal activity.

Answer: Bacteriostatic activity inhibits bacterial growth; bactericidal activity kills bacteria under specified test conditions.

Q79. What is sterility testing?

Answer: Testing a product sample for evidence of viable microorganisms using a pharmacopoeial method; it cannot alone prove every batch unit is sterile.

Q80. Why are load configuration and cycle records important?

Answer: They show that the validated cycle was applied to the intended load and support investigation of deviations or equipment problems.

Unit V — Microbiological Quality Control

Q81. What is microbiological quality control?

Answer: Planned sampling and testing to assess microbial quality and verify that products and environments meet applicable requirements.

Q82. What is a microbial limit test?

Answer: A test estimating specified groups or total numbers of microorganisms in a nonsterile product and, where required, checking specified organisms.

Q83. Differentiate total aerobic microbial count and total yeast and mould count.

Answer: The first estimates qualifying aerobic microorganisms under its method; the second estimates fungi under specified culture conditions.

Q84. Why test for specified microorganisms?

Answer: Certain organisms can pose particular risks or indicate unsuitable hygiene, so applicable standards require limits or absence in selected products.

Q85. What is a microbial assay of an antibiotic?

Answer: A potency test measuring the effect of an antibiotic on a susceptible microorganism using a validated method.

Q86. What is agar diffusion assay?

Answer: A sample diffuses through agar seeded with a test organism; an inhibition zone relates to activity under controlled conditions.

Q87. What is a turbidimetric assay?

Answer: A biological assay estimating activity by measuring microbial growth or turbidity in liquid culture.

Q88. How can vitamins be assayed microbiologically?

Answer: A microorganism with a defined vitamin requirement is grown in media containing graded vitamin amounts, and growth is compared with standards.

Q89. What is sterility testing by membrane filtration?

Answer: A suitable sample is filtered through a membrane that retains microbes; the membrane is transferred to prescribed culture media and incubated.

Q90. What is direct inoculation sterility testing?

Answer: A specified amount of product is added directly to suitable culture media and incubated under the applicable method.

Q91. Why are method suitability and growth-promotion tests important?

Answer: They confirm the product does not prevent microbial recovery and that media can support growth of required test organisms.

Q92. What is water microbiological monitoring?

Answer: Testing water samples using methods appropriate to the water system and intended use, alongside other system controls.

Q93. Name methods for microbiological air monitoring.

Answer: Active air sampling, settle plates and surface sampling near relevant operations; each has limitations and requires a defined programme.

Q94. What is active air sampling?

Answer: A known volume of air is drawn through or onto a collection medium to estimate viable airborne microorganisms.

Q95. What is a settle plate?

Answer: An agar plate is exposed for a defined time to collect viable particles settling from air; it does not measure a known air volume.

Q96. What is cell culture?

Answer: Growth of cells outside an organism under controlled conditions using suitable nutrients and environmental parameters.

Q97. Differentiate primary and continuous cell cultures.

Answer: Primary cultures come directly from tissue and usually have limited lifespan; continuous cell lines can proliferate for extended periods.

Q98. What are pharmaceutical applications of cell culture?

Answer: Vaccine and biologic production, potency testing, toxicity studies, disease research and investigation of drug effects.

Q99. What precautions are needed in cell culture?

Answer: Aseptic technique, suitable containment, qualified media and reagents, contamination checks, cell-line identification and documentation.

Q100. Why follow the applicable pharmacopoeia?

Answer: The current applicable text defines methods, controls and acceptance requirements; limits differ by product and intended use.
