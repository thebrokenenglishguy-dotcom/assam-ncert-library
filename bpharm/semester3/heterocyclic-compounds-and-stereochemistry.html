Unit I — Carboxylic Acids, Amines, Alcohols, Phenols and Polynuclear Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Q1. How are aliphatic and aromatic carboxylic acids classified?

Answer: Aliphatic carboxylic acids have the –COOH group attached to an aliphatic carbon framework. Aromatic carboxylic acids have the –COOH group attached directly to an aromatic ring, as in benzoic acid.

Q2. How can primary alcohols be oxidised to carboxylic acids?

Answer: Strong oxidising agents such as acidified potassium dichromate or permanganate can oxidise primary alcohols first to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids.

Q3. What is the carbonation of a Grignard reagent?

Answer: A Grignard reagent, RMgX, reacts with carbon dioxide and, after acidic work-up, forms a carboxylic acid with one additional carbon atom: RMgX + CO₂, then H₃O⁺ → RCOOH.

Q4. What is the Kolbe–Schmitt reaction?

Answer: Sodium phenoxide reacts with carbon dioxide under pressure and heat, followed by acidification, to give mainly salicylic acid under suitable conditions.

Q5. Why are carboxylic acids acidic?

Answer: They donate a proton to form a carboxylate ion. The negative charge in the carboxylate is stabilised by resonance over two oxygen atoms.

Q6. How do electron-withdrawing substituents affect carboxylic acid acidity?

Answer: Electron-withdrawing groups generally increase acidity by stabilising the carboxylate ion. Electron-donating groups generally reduce acidity; the effect depends on position and strength.

Q7. What is nucleophilic acyl substitution?

Answer: A nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon of an acyl compound, forming a tetrahedral intermediate, after which a leaving group is displaced to form a substituted acyl product.

Q8. What is decarboxylation?

Answer: Decarboxylation is the removal of carbon dioxide from a carboxylic acid or its derivative. Some β-keto acids decarboxylate readily on heating.

Q9. What is the Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) reaction?

Answer: The HVZ reaction halogenates the alpha carbon of a carboxylic acid containing an alpha hydrogen, commonly using bromine and phosphorus, followed by hydrolysis.

Q10. Give pharmaceutical applications of benzoic acid, salicylic acid and acetylsalicylic acid.

Answer: Benzoic acid and its salts can be used as preservatives; salicylic acid is used in topical keratolytic preparations; acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) is an analgesic, antipyretic, anti-inflammatory and, at low doses, an antiplatelet drug.

Q11. How can amines be prepared by reduction of nitro compounds?

Answer: Nitro compounds can be reduced to amines using suitable reducing systems such as catalytic hydrogenation or metal-acid reagents.

Q12. How can nitriles be converted into primary amines?

Answer: Reduction of a nitrile, R–C≡N, using hydrogenation or a suitable hydride reagent gives a primary amine, R–CH₂NH₂.

Q13. What is Hofmann bromamide degradation?

Answer: An amide reacts with bromine and aqueous alkali to produce a primary amine with one fewer carbon atom than the original amide.

Q14. Why are amines basic?

Answer: The nitrogen atom has a lone pair that can accept a proton. Basicity depends on the availability of this lone pair and the stability of the resulting ammonium ion.

Q15. How do substituents affect amine basicity?

Answer: Electron-donating groups often increase electron density on nitrogen, while electron-withdrawing groups reduce it. Solvation and steric effects also influence the observed basicity, especially in water.

Q16. What are diazonium salts and how are they prepared?

Answer: Aromatic primary amines react with nitrous acid, usually generated in situ from sodium nitrite and mineral acid at low temperature, to form aromatic diazonium salts.

Q17. What is the Sandmeyer reaction?

Answer: The Sandmeyer reaction replaces the diazonium group in an aromatic diazonium salt with groups such as chlorine, bromine or cyanide using appropriate copper(I) salts.

Q18. What is azo coupling?

Answer: A diazonium ion couples with an activated aromatic ring, such as phenol or an aromatic amine, to form an azo compound containing the –N=N– linkage, often used as a dye.

Q19. How are alcohols classified?

Answer: Alcohols are classified as primary, secondary or tertiary according to the number of carbon groups attached to the carbon bearing the –OH group; they may also be aliphatic or aromatic-substituted.

Q20. What is oxymercuration–demercuration?

Answer: It is a method for hydrating an alkene to give a Markovnikov alcohol, generally without the carbocation rearrangements associated with acid-catalysed hydration.

Q21. How are carbonyl compounds reduced to alcohols?

Answer: Aldehydes are reduced to primary alcohols, while ketones are reduced to secondary alcohols. Common reagents include sodium borohydride and lithium aluminium hydride.

Q22. Why are phenols generally more acidic than ordinary alcohols?

Answer: The phenoxide ion formed after proton loss is stabilised by resonance over the aromatic ring and oxygen; ordinary alkoxide ions lack comparable resonance stabilisation.

Q23. What is the cumene process for phenol manufacture?

Answer: Cumene is oxidised to cumene hydroperoxide, which undergoes acid-catalysed cleavage to form phenol and acetone.

Q24. What is the Reimer–Tiemann reaction?

Answer: Phenol reacts with chloroform and aqueous alkali, followed by work-up, to introduce a formyl group mainly at the ortho position, producing salicylaldehyde.

Q25. How do phenols undergo halogenation and nitration?

Answer: The –OH group activates the aromatic ring and directs electrophilic substitution mainly to ortho and para positions. Product distribution depends on conditions and reagent strength.

Q26. State pharmaceutical uses of glycerine, thymol and paracetamol.

Answer: Glycerine is used as a humectant and in some laxative formulations; thymol has antiseptic uses; paracetamol is an analgesic and antipyretic.

Q27. What are polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons?

Answer: They contain two or more fused or linked aromatic rings. Examples include naphthalene, anthracene and phenanthrene.

Q28. What is the Haworth synthesis of fused aromatic systems?

Answer: Haworth-type synthesis builds fused ring systems through steps such as acylation, reduction, cyclisation and dehydrogenation; the exact sequence depends on the target compound.

Q29. Where does electrophilic substitution commonly occur in naphthalene?

Answer: Electrophilic substitution commonly occurs at the alpha (1-) position because the corresponding intermediate is often more resonance-stabilised, though conditions and reaction type matter.

Q30. Name medicinal compounds associated with naphthalene or phenanthrene frameworks.

Answer: Examples include propranolol and naphazoline, which contain naphthalene groups, and morphine and codeine, which contain a phenanthrene-related fused-ring framework.

Unit II — Optical Isomerism

Q31. What is stereoisomerism?

Answer: Stereoisomers have the same molecular formula and connectivity but differ in the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms.

Q32. Name the main types of stereoisomerism.

Answer: The two broad types are configurational stereoisomerism, including enantiomerism and diastereomerism, and conformational isomerism arising from rotation or ring conformations.

Q33. What is optical activity?

Answer: Optical activity is the ability of a substance to rotate the plane of plane-polarised light under specified conditions.

Q34. What is chirality?

Answer: A molecule is chiral if its mirror image cannot be superimposed on it. A common cause is a tetrahedral carbon attached to four different groups.

Q35. What are elements of symmetry?

Answer: Common symmetry elements include a rotation axis, a mirror plane, and a centre of inversion. Certain improper rotation axes also indicate achirality.

Q36. Differentiate chiral and achiral molecules.

Answer: A chiral molecule is not superimposable on its mirror image; an achiral molecule is superimposable on its mirror image.

Q37. What are enantiomers?

Answer: Enantiomers are stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other. In an achiral environment they generally have the same ordinary physical properties, except for optical rotation and interactions with chiral systems.

Q38. What are diastereomers?

Answer: Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images. They often differ in physical properties and can frequently be separated by ordinary methods.

Q39. What is a meso compound?

Answer: A meso compound contains stereogenic centres but is achiral because of internal symmetry, so its optical activity cancels within the same molecule.

Q40. What is the D/L system?

Answer: D and L designate configuration relative to a reference molecule, commonly glyceraldehyde, using a defined structural convention. D/L does not directly indicate the direction of optical rotation.

Q41. What are the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog (CIP) rules?

Answer: CIP rules rank substituents by priority, first by atomic number and then by the first point of difference; multiple bonds are treated using duplicate-atom conventions.

Q42. How is an R or S configuration assigned?

Answer: Rank the four substituents 1 to 4 using CIP rules. View the lowest-priority group pointing away; if 1→2→3 runs clockwise the configuration is R, and if anticlockwise it is S.

Q43. Does R always mean dextrorotatory and S always mean levorotatory?

Answer: No. R/S describes absolute configuration, while (+)/(−) describes the experimentally observed direction of optical rotation. There is no universal correspondence.

Q44. What is a racemic mixture?

Answer: A racemic mixture contains equal amounts of two enantiomers and is optically inactive overall because their rotations cancel under the same conditions.

Q45. What is resolution of a racemic mixture?

Answer: Resolution is the separation of enantiomers, for example by converting them into diastereomeric salts with a chiral resolving agent, using chiral chromatography, or employing suitable enzymes.

Q46. Why is optical isomerism important in pharmacy?

Answer: Enantiomers can differ in receptor binding, metabolism, potency, duration and toxicity. Therefore, stereochemistry may influence the efficacy and safety of a medicine.

Q47. What is enantiomeric excess?

Answer: Enantiomeric excess measures the excess of one enantiomer over the other: ee (%) = |R−S|/(R+S) × 100, where R and S represent their amounts.

Q48. What is specific rotation?

Answer: Specific rotation is a standardised measure of optical rotation for a substance at a specified temperature, wavelength, path length and concentration convention.

Q49. Can a molecule with more than one stereogenic centre be achiral?

Answer: Yes. A meso compound can have multiple stereogenic centres yet be achiral because of internal symmetry.

Q50. What is the pharmaceutical importance of racemate resolution?

Answer: It may allow a desired enantiomer to be isolated for studying or developing a medicine, but whether separation improves treatment depends on the specific drug's evidence and clinical properties.

Unit III — Geometrical and Conformational Isomerism

Q51. What is geometrical isomerism?

Answer: Geometrical isomerism is stereoisomerism caused by restricted rotation, commonly around a carbon-carbon double bond or within a ring, producing different spatial arrangements.

Q52. Differentiate cis-trans and E-Z nomenclature.

Answer: Cis/trans describes relative positions in suitable simple structures. E/Z uses CIP priorities on each double-bond carbon: Z means the higher-priority groups are on the same side; E means they are on opposite sides.

Q53. What are syn and anti isomers?

Answer: Syn and anti are context-dependent terms describing relative positions of groups or faces in a structure or reaction. Their exact meaning should be stated for the particular system.

Q54. What conditions are required for alkene geometrical isomerism?

Answer: Each carbon of the double bond must carry two different substituents, and rotation around the double bond must be restricted.

Q55. What is conformational isomerism?

Answer: Conformational isomerism arises from different spatial arrangements produced by rotation around single bonds or changes in ring shape, without breaking covalent bonds.

Q56. Describe conformations of ethane.

Answer: Ethane has staggered and eclipsed conformations. The staggered conformation is lower in energy because it has less torsional strain.

Q57. What are the important conformations of butane?

Answer: Important conformations include anti, gauche, eclipsed, and fully eclipsed. The anti conformation is generally most stable because the methyl groups are farthest apart.

Q58. Why is the chair conformation of cyclohexane stable?

Answer: The chair conformation has near-tetrahedral bond angles and largely staggered bonds, minimising angle and torsional strain.

Q59. Differentiate axial and equatorial bonds in cyclohexane.

Answer: Axial bonds point roughly up or down relative to the ring axis; equatorial bonds extend outward around the ring's perimeter. Substituents often prefer equatorial positions to reduce 1,3-diaxial interactions.

Q60. What is a ring flip in cyclohexane?

Answer: A ring flip interconverts chair conformations, exchanging axial and equatorial positions while retaining whether substituents are up or down.

Q61. What is atropisomerism?

Answer: Atropisomerism is stereoisomerism caused by restricted rotation around a bond, often in substituted biphenyls, so distinct conformers can be sufficiently stable to be isolated.

Q62. What structural conditions can make a biphenyl optically active?

Answer: Bulky ortho substituents can restrict rotation, and the arrangement must lack symmetry that makes the molecule achiral. Stable axial chirality is required for isolable atropisomers.

Q63. Why can E/Z isomers have different physical properties?

Answer: Their different spatial arrangements change molecular shape and dipole moments and can affect boiling point, melting point, stability and biological interactions.

Q64. How can E/Z isomers be distinguished?

Answer: They can be distinguished using suitable spectroscopic methods, chromatography, physical-property measurements, or structural analysis; the best method depends on the compound.

Q65. What is torsional strain?

Answer: Torsional strain arises from repulsion between bonding electron pairs in eclipsed or near-eclipsed arrangements.

Q66. What is steric strain?

Answer: Steric strain occurs when atoms or groups are forced too close together, increasing repulsion and raising molecular energy.

Q67. Why is stereochemistry important for drug molecules?

Answer: Three-dimensional shape affects interactions with chiral biological targets, so stereoisomers may show different activity, selectivity, pharmacokinetics or toxicity.

Q68. What is the difference between conformers and configurational isomers?

Answer: Conformers interconvert by rotation or ring motion without breaking bonds; configurational isomers generally require bond breaking or a more substantial process to interconvert.

Q69. What is a stereogenic axis?

Answer: A stereogenic axis is an axis around which the arrangement of substituents creates stereoisomerism, as in many chiral biaryl compounds.

Q70. Why is a single drawing not enough to identify all stereochemical features?

Answer: A two-dimensional drawing may hide three-dimensional relationships; wedges, dashes, stereochemical descriptors and the relevant naming rules are needed.

Unit IV — Chemistry of Five-Membered Heterocycles

Q71. What is a heterocyclic compound?

Answer: A heterocyclic compound contains a ring with at least one atom other than carbon, commonly nitrogen, oxygen or sulfur.

Q72. How are heterocyclic compounds classified?

Answer: They can be classified by ring size, type and number of heteroatoms, saturation, aromaticity, and whether rings are fused.

Q73. What is the Hantzsch–Widman system?

Answer: It is an IUPAC naming system for many heteromonocyclic ring systems, using components that indicate heteroatoms and ring size or saturation according to its rules.

Q74. What is pyrrole?

Answer: Pyrrole is an aromatic five-membered ring containing one nitrogen atom. Its nitrogen lone pair contributes to the aromatic pi-electron system.

Q75. What is furan?

Answer: Furan is an aromatic five-membered ring containing one oxygen atom; one oxygen lone pair contributes to the aromatic pi system.

Q76. What is thiophene?

Answer: Thiophene is an aromatic five-membered ring containing one sulfur atom, with one sulfur lone pair contributing to the aromatic pi system.

Q77. Compare the relative aromaticity of pyrrole, furan and thiophene.

Answer: All three are aromatic, but their relative aromatic character depends on the criterion and environment. In many introductory comparisons, thiophene is considered more aromatic than pyrrole, which is more aromatic than furan.

Q78. Why is pyrrole weakly basic?

Answer: The nitrogen lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet, so it is less available to accept a proton than the lone pair of a typical amine.

Q79. Why do pyrrole, furan and thiophene undergo electrophilic substitution mainly at the 2-position?

Answer: Attack at the alpha (2-) position generally gives a more resonance-stabilised sigma complex than attack at the beta position.

Q80. What is the Paal–Knorr synthesis of pyrroles?

Answer: A 1,4-dicarbonyl compound reacts with ammonia or a primary amine under suitable conditions to form a pyrrole ring.

Q81. What is the Feist–Benary synthesis?

Answer: It is a method for synthesising substituted furans, commonly through the reaction of an alpha-haloketone with a beta-dicarbonyl compound under basic conditions.

Q82. What is the Hinsberg synthesis of thiophenes?

Answer: Hinsberg-type methods form substituted thiophenes through reactions of suitable 1,4-dicarbonyl-related precursors with sulfurising reagents; specific variants use different starting materials.

Q83. What is electrophilic aromatic substitution?

Answer: An electrophile attacks an aromatic ring to form a sigma complex; loss of a proton then restores aromaticity.

Q84. Name drugs containing a pyrrole ring or pyrrole-related structure from the syllabus.

Answer: Ethosuximide and procyclidine are listed in the syllabus as medicinal examples associated with pyrrole-containing structures; check the prescribed text for the exact structural relationship.

Q85. Name medicines listed with furan rings.

Answer: Furosemide and nitrofurazone are listed as furan-containing medicinal examples.

Q86. Name medicines listed with thiophene rings.

Answer: Cephaloridine and clopidogrel are examples listed in the syllabus as containing thiophene-related structural features.

Q87. Why is the heteroatom important in a heterocycle?

Answer: It changes electron distribution, basicity, polarity, hydrogen bonding, reactivity and the way the molecule interacts with biological targets.

Q88. What does IUPAC nomenclature achieve?

Answer: It provides a systematic name that communicates a compound's structure and helps avoid ambiguity between common or trade names.

Q89. How can the reactivity of pyrrole differ from benzene?

Answer: Pyrrole is generally more reactive toward electrophilic substitution because its ring is electron-rich, although reaction conditions must be chosen to avoid polymerisation or side reactions.

Q90. Why are heterocyclic rings common in medicines?

Answer: Heteroatoms can tune solubility, pKa, hydrogen bonding, metabolic stability and target binding, making heterocycles useful in medicinal chemistry.

Unit V — Chemistry of Other Heterocycles

Q91. What are fused heterocyclic compounds?

Answer: Fused heterocycles contain two or more rings that share a common bond, with at least one ring containing a heteroatom.

Q92. What is the Knorr synthesis of pyrazoles?

Answer: Knorr-type pyrazole synthesis commonly involves the reaction of a 1,3-dicarbonyl compound with a hydrazine derivative to form a pyrazole ring.

Q93. What is the Debus–Radziszewski synthesis of imidazoles?

Answer: A classical route forms imidazoles by condensing a 1,2-dicarbonyl compound, an aldehyde and ammonia or an ammonium source, with substituents depending on the reactants.

Q94. What is the Hantzsch synthesis of pyridines?

Answer: The Hantzsch pyridine synthesis condenses suitable beta-keto esters, an aldehyde and ammonia to produce a dihydropyridine, which may be oxidised to a pyridine derivative.

Q95. What is the Skraup synthesis of quinoline?

Answer: The Skraup synthesis traditionally forms quinoline by heating aniline with glycerol and an oxidising agent in the presence of acid; conditions are vigorous and require specialised safety controls.

Q96. How do pyrazole and imidazole undergo electrophilic substitution?

Answer: Substitution generally occurs at positions favoured by ring electron distribution and reaction conditions. The nitrogen atoms influence both reactivity and protonation; the exact position depends on the substrate and reagent.

Q97. Name common heterocyclic ring systems listed in the syllabus.

Answer: Examples include indole, pyrimidine, benzimidazole, purine, azepine, pyrazole, oxazole, phenothiazine, benzotriazole and quinoxaline.

Q98. What is indole?

Answer: Indole is a fused bicyclic aromatic system consisting of a benzene ring fused to a pyrrole ring.

Q99. What is pyrimidine?

Answer: Pyrimidine is a six-membered aromatic heterocycle containing nitrogen atoms at positions 1 and 3; it is part of the structures of several biological bases and drugs.

Q100. What is benzimidazole?

Answer: Benzimidazole is a fused aromatic ring system containing a benzene ring fused to an imidazole ring.

Q101. What is purine?

Answer: Purine is a fused bicyclic heteroaromatic system that forms the core structure of adenine and guanine and several medicines.

Q102. What is azepine?

Answer: Azepine is a seven-membered unsaturated heterocyclic ring containing one nitrogen atom; related ring systems occur in medicines.

Q103. What is oxazole?

Answer: Oxazole is a five-membered aromatic heterocycle containing oxygen and nitrogen in the ring.

Q104. What is phenothiazine?

Answer: Phenothiazine is a tricyclic ring system containing sulfur and nitrogen; substituted derivatives include medicines such as some antipsychotics and antihistamines.

Q105. What is benzotriazole?

Answer: Benzotriazole is a fused ring system containing a benzene ring and a triazole ring with three nitrogen atoms.

Q106. What is quinoxaline?

Answer: Quinoxaline is a fused aromatic system consisting of a benzene ring fused to a pyrazine ring.

Q107. Compare the basicity of pyridine, imidazole and quinoline.

Answer: Pyridine and quinoline are weak bases because the nitrogen lone pair is outside the aromatic pi system. Imidazole is generally more basic in water because protonation gives a resonance-stabilised conjugate acid; exact values depend on conditions.

Q108. Name two medicines containing pyrazole rings from the syllabus.

Answer: Sildenafil and celecoxib are listed in the syllabus under pyrazole-containing medicines; consult the prescribed structures to identify the specific ring and substituents.

Q109. Name medicines listed under imidazole-containing compounds.

Answer: Metronidazole and pilocarpine are listed in the syllabus as examples associated with imidazole chemistry; pilocarpine's precise heterocyclic structure should be checked in the prescribed reference.

Q110. Name medicines listed under pyridine-containing compounds.

Answer: Isoniazid and chlorpheniramine are listed as pyridine-related medicinal examples in the syllabus.

Q111. Name quinoline-related medicines from the syllabus.

Answer: Chloroquine and ciprofloxacin are listed in the syllabus in the quinoline section; ciprofloxacin is more precisely classified as a fluoroquinolone.

Q112. Name indole-related medicines from the syllabus.

Answer: Indomethacin and reserpine are listed as indole-related medicinal examples.

Q113. Name benzimidazole-containing medicines.

Answer: Albendazole and mebendazole are benzimidazole derivatives used as anthelmintic medicines.

Q114. Name medicines listed under pyrimidine-containing compounds.

Answer: Fluorouracil and sulphadiazine are listed in the syllabus under pyrimidine-related compounds.

Q115. Name purine analogues listed in the syllabus.

Answer: Mercaptopurine and thioguanine are purine analogues used in selected cancer and immune-related treatment settings.

Q116. Name azepine-related medicines listed in the syllabus.

Answer: Diazepam and loxapine are listed under azepine heterocycles in the syllabus; their full structures contain additional fused or related ring features.

Q117. Why does heterocycle basicity matter in pharmacy?

Answer: Basicity influences ionisation, aqueous solubility, membrane permeability, salt formation, receptor interactions and formulation behaviour.

Q118. What is a conjugate acid?

Answer: A conjugate acid forms when a base accepts a proton. Its stability helps determine the base's strength.

Q119. Why must the structures of medicines be studied rather than only memorised by name?

Answer: Structural understanding helps identify functional groups, heterocyclic rings, likely chemical properties, naming patterns and relationships between structure and biological activity.

Q120. What precautions are needed when studying organic synthesis reactions?

Answer: Learn the reactants, conditions, product, mechanism and selectivity, but do not perform reactions without appropriate laboratory supervision, risk assessment and protective equipment.
