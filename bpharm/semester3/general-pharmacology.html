Unit I — Introduction to Pharmacology

Q1. Define pharmacology.

Answer: Pharmacology is the study of drugs and their interactions with living systems, including their actions, uses, movement through the body, and harmful effects.

Q2. What are the main branches of pharmacology?

Answer: Major branches include pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics, pharmacotherapeutics, toxicology, clinical pharmacology, pharmacogenomics, and chemotherapy.

Q3. Differentiate pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics.

Answer: Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug—absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME). Pharmacodynamics describes what the drug does to the body.

Q4. What is a drug?

Answer: A drug is a substance used to diagnose, prevent, treat, or relieve disease, or to modify physiological functions.

Q5. What are the common sources of drugs?

Answer: Drugs may come from plants, animals, microorganisms, minerals, chemical synthesis, biotechnology, or other modern production methods.

Q6. What is the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)?

Answer: ICD is the World Health Organization's system for classifying and coding diseases, health conditions, and causes of death. It classifies diseases, not drug names.

Q7. What is an International Non-proprietary Name (INN)?

Answer: An INN is a globally recognised generic name assigned to a pharmaceutical substance by the World Health Organization.

Q8. Differentiate generic and brand names of a medicine.

Answer: The generic name identifies the active drug substance, while the brand name is the name chosen by a manufacturer for a marketed product.

Q9. What are essential medicines?

Answer: Essential medicines meet the priority healthcare needs of a population and are selected with attention to public-health relevance, evidence of efficacy and safety, and comparative cost-effectiveness.

Q10. What is rational drug use?

Answer: Rational drug use means patients receive medicines appropriate to their clinical needs, in suitable doses and for an adequate duration, at the lowest reasonable cost to them and the community.

Q11. Why is rational use of medicines important?

Answer: It improves treatment outcomes, reduces avoidable adverse effects and antimicrobial resistance, and helps prevent waste and unnecessary costs.

Q12. Name Indian initiatives that promote generic and affordable medicines.

Answer: Examples include the Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana (PMBJP) and the National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM), alongside public-sector medicine procurement and awareness initiatives.

Q13. What is the oral route of drug administration?

Answer: The oral route means swallowing a medicine for absorption through the gastrointestinal tract. It is convenient and usually economical, but absorption may be variable and some drugs undergo first-pass metabolism.

Q14. What are the advantages and disadvantages of the intravenous route?

Answer: Intravenous administration gives rapid systemic delivery and precise control, but requires trained personnel and aseptic technique and carries risks such as infection, embolism, and rapid adverse reactions.

Q15. Name common routes of drug administration.

Answer: Common routes include oral, sublingual, buccal, rectal, intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, inhalational, topical, transdermal, intranasal, and ophthalmic routes.

Q16. How is a route of administration selected?

Answer: Selection depends on the patient's condition, required speed and duration of action, drug properties, desired local or systemic effect, safety, and patient ability to use the route.

Unit II — Pharmacokinetics

Q17. What is drug absorption?

Answer: Drug absorption is the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the systemic circulation, except when it is administered directly into the bloodstream.

Q18. Describe common mechanisms of drug absorption.

Answer: Mechanisms include passive diffusion, facilitated diffusion, active transport, and endocytosis or vesicular transport for selected substances.

Q19. What is passive diffusion?

Answer: Passive diffusion is movement down a concentration gradient without direct energy use or a carrier. It is important for many drugs crossing biological membranes.

Q20. What is active transport?

Answer: Active transport uses a carrier and energy, directly or indirectly, to move a substance against its concentration or electrochemical gradient.

Q21. What is facilitated diffusion?

Answer: Facilitated diffusion uses a membrane carrier or channel to move a substance down its gradient without direct energy consumption.

Q22. What factors affect drug absorption?

Answer: Factors include drug solubility, formulation and dissolution, lipid solubility, ionisation, surface area, blood flow, gastric emptying, intestinal motility, food, interactions, and disease.

Q23. What is bioavailability?

Answer: Bioavailability is the fraction of an administered dose that reaches systemic circulation unchanged. For intravenous administration, it is considered 100%.

Q24. What is bioequivalence?

Answer: Two pharmaceutical products are bioequivalent when their rate and extent of absorption show no significant difference under appropriately designed study conditions.

Q25. What is first-pass metabolism?

Answer: First-pass metabolism is presystemic metabolism in the intestinal wall and/or liver that reduces the amount of unchanged drug reaching systemic circulation after certain routes, especially oral administration.

Q26. What is drug distribution?

Answer: Distribution is the reversible movement of a drug between blood and body tissues after it enters systemic circulation.

Q27. What is volume of distribution (Vd)?

Answer: Vd is an apparent volume relating the amount of drug in the body to its plasma concentration: Vd = amount of drug in body / plasma drug concentration.

Q28. What does a high volume of distribution suggest?

Answer: It suggests that much of the drug is outside the plasma, often distributed into tissues. Vd is an apparent value, not necessarily a real anatomical volume.

Q29. What is plasma protein binding?

Answer: It is the reversible association of a drug with proteins in plasma, such as albumin or alpha-1-acid glycoprotein.

Q30. Why is plasma protein binding clinically important?

Answer: It can affect the free drug fraction available for distribution, metabolism, excretion, and pharmacological action. The unbound fraction is generally available to cross membranes and interact with targets.

Q31. What is drug biotransformation?

Answer: Biotransformation or metabolism is the enzymatic conversion of a drug into metabolites, often making it easier to eliminate. Metabolites may be inactive, active, or toxic.

Q32. Differentiate Phase I and Phase II metabolism.

Answer: Phase I reactions include oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis. Phase II reactions conjugate a drug or metabolite with a group such as glucuronide, sulfate, or glutathione.

Q33. What is the cytochrome P450 system?

Answer: Cytochrome P450 enzymes are a family of enzymes, especially in the liver, that metabolise many drugs and other chemicals; enzyme inhibition or induction can alter drug exposure.

Q34. What is enterohepatic cycling?

Answer: Enterohepatic cycling occurs when a drug or metabolite is excreted into bile, enters the intestine, and is reabsorbed, potentially prolonging its presence in the body.

Q35. What is a prodrug?

Answer: A prodrug is a substance administered in a form that is converted in the body into an active drug or active species.

Q36. What is drug excretion?

Answer: Excretion is the removal of a drug or its metabolites from the body, mainly through the kidneys, but also through bile, faeces, lungs, sweat, or breast milk for certain substances.

Q37. What is renal clearance?

Answer: Renal clearance is the volume of plasma from which a drug is completely removed by the kidneys per unit time.

Q38. What is half-life?

Answer: Elimination half-life is the time required for the plasma concentration or amount of drug in the body to decrease by 50% during the relevant elimination phase.

Q39. What is steady state during repeated dosing?

Answer: Steady state is reached when the average rate of drug administration equals the average rate of elimination, so the average drug concentration remains stable over time.

Q40. What is a loading dose?

Answer: A loading dose is a larger initial dose used when appropriate to reach a target drug concentration more quickly. It depends on the target concentration, Vd, and bioavailability.

Q41. How can kidney or liver disease affect pharmacokinetics?

Answer: Kidney disease can reduce elimination of renally cleared drugs; liver disease can alter metabolism, protein binding, or bile excretion. Doses may need individual adjustment and monitoring.

Unit III — Pharmacodynamics

Q42. What is pharmacodynamics?

Answer: Pharmacodynamics studies the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs, their mechanisms of action, and the relationship between drug concentration and response.

Q43. What are the main types of drug action?

Answer: Common descriptions include stimulation, depression, replacement, local action, cytotoxic action, and modification of physiological processes. The categories can overlap.

Q44. What is a mechanism of drug action?

Answer: It is the molecular or physiological process through which a drug produces its effect, such as receptor activation, enzyme inhibition, or ion-channel modulation.

Q45. What is a dose-response relationship?

Answer: It describes how the magnitude or probability of a drug effect changes with dose or concentration.

Q46. Differentiate graded and quantal dose-response relationships.

Answer: A graded response measures the size of an effect in an individual system; a quantal response measures the proportion of a population that shows a defined effect.

Q47. What is potency?

Answer: Potency describes the amount or concentration of a drug needed to produce a specified effect. A more potent drug needs a lower dose for that effect, but is not necessarily more effective or safer.

Q48. What is efficacy?

Answer: Efficacy is the maximum effect a drug can produce in a given system, often represented by Emax.

Q49. What is a receptor?

Answer: A receptor is a cellular macromolecule that recognises a ligand and initiates or modifies a biological response.

Q50. Name major receptor families.

Answer: Major families include ligand-gated ion channels, G-protein-coupled receptors, enzyme-linked receptors, and intracellular or nuclear receptors.

Q51. What is receptor regulation?

Answer: Receptor regulation is a change in receptor number, location, or responsiveness. Continued stimulation may cause desensitisation or down-regulation, while reduced stimulation can sometimes cause up-regulation.

Q52. What is a spare receptor?

Answer: Spare receptors exist when a maximal response can be achieved without occupying all available receptors, often because of signal amplification.

Q53. Differentiate agonist, partial agonist, and antagonist.

Answer: An agonist activates a receptor and produces a response. A partial agonist produces a lower maximum response than a full agonist in the same system. An antagonist binds without activating the receptor and blocks agonist action.

Q54. What is an inverse agonist?

Answer: An inverse agonist reduces constitutive receptor activity and produces the opposite effect to an agonist at receptors that are active even without a ligand.

Q55. What is signal transduction?

Answer: Signal transduction is the process by which receptor activation is converted into intracellular signals and a cellular response, often through second messengers or protein phosphorylation.

Q56. What is tachyphylaxis?

Answer: Tachyphylaxis is a rapid decrease in response to a drug after repeated administration over a short period.

Q57. What is idiosyncrasy?

Answer: Idiosyncrasy is an unusual, often unexpected drug response that is not explained by the usual pharmacological action at ordinary doses.

Q58. What is drug tolerance?

Answer: Tolerance is a reduction in response after repeated use, so a larger dose may be required to produce the same effect. It can be pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, or behavioural.

Q59. Differentiate physical dependence and addiction.

Answer: Physical dependence is adaptation that may cause withdrawal if a drug is stopped or reduced. Addiction involves impaired control and continued use despite harm; dependence alone does not establish addiction.

Q60. What is an additive drug effect?

Answer: An additive effect occurs when the combined effect of two drugs is approximately equal to the expected sum of their individual effects, under the chosen measure.

Q61. What is synergism?

Answer: Synergism occurs when the combined effect of two drugs is greater than expected from their individual effects. Clinical importance depends on the drugs and outcome.

Q62. What is an adverse drug reaction (ADR)?

Answer: An ADR is a harmful and unintended response to a medicine occurring at doses normally used for prevention, diagnosis, or treatment, or for modifying physiological function.

Q63. What are common types of ADRs?

Answer: A commonly used classification includes Type A (dose-related and predictable), Type B (unpredictable), Type C (related to chronic use), Type D (delayed), Type E (withdrawal), and Type F (therapeutic failure).

Q64. What is a drug-drug interaction?

Answer: It occurs when one drug changes the effect, concentration, safety, or response to another drug.

Q65. Differentiate pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions.

Answer: Pharmacokinetic interactions alter absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion of another drug. Pharmacodynamic interactions alter effects at targets or physiological systems without necessarily changing concentration.

Q66. How can drug interactions be reduced?

Answer: Review all medicines and supplements, check known interactions, consider patient-specific risks, monitor where needed, and consult an authorised prescriber or pharmacist before changing treatment.

Unit IV — Drug Discovery, Evaluation, and Recent Trends

Q67. What are the main stages of drug discovery and development?

Answer: The process commonly includes target identification and validation, hit discovery, lead optimisation, preclinical testing, clinical trials, regulatory review, and post-marketing monitoring.

Q68. What is preclinical evaluation?

Answer: Preclinical evaluation assesses a candidate's pharmacological activity, pharmacokinetics, and potential toxicity before it is tested in human clinical trials.

Q69. What is the purpose of pharmacological screening?

Answer: Screening identifies and characterises compounds with potentially useful biological effects and helps select candidates for further testing.

Q70. What is an in vitro study?

Answer: An in vitro study is conducted outside a living organism, for example in cells, tissues, biochemical systems, or reconstructed human tissue models.

Q71. What is an in vivo study?

Answer: An in vivo study is conducted in a living organism. It can provide information about whole-body effects but has ethical and translational limitations.

Q72. What is in silico evaluation?

Answer: In silico evaluation uses computer models, simulations, or computational analyses to predict or examine drug properties, activity, or risks.

Q73. State advantages and limitations of in vitro testing.

Answer: It can be controlled, relatively rapid, and useful for human-cell-based testing, but may not fully reproduce whole-body absorption, metabolism, immune responses, or organ interactions.

Q74. State advantages and limitations of in silico screening.

Answer: It can rapidly prioritise candidates and reduce experimental burden, but predictions depend on model quality, training data, and validation and cannot replace all experimental evidence.

Q75. What is reconstructed human epidermis testing used for?

Answer: It uses laboratory-grown human skin models to assess selected skin irritation or related endpoints according to a validated method.

Q76. What is a reconstructed corneal epithelium test?

Answer: It uses a laboratory-grown corneal epithelial model to assess certain eye-hazard endpoints under a validated testing guideline.

Q77. What is skin corrosion testing by a direct peptide reactivity assay (DPRA)?

Answer: DPRA measures a chemical's reactivity with model peptides and is used as one source of evidence in defined approaches for skin sensitisation assessment; it is not, by itself, a complete general skin-corrosion test.

Q78. What is an organ-on-chip model?

Answer: An organ-on-chip is a microengineered system that recreates selected structural or functional features of an organ, often using living cells and controlled fluid flow.

Q79. What is chronopharmacology?

Answer: Chronopharmacology studies how biological timing and rhythms influence drug effects, including drug action and the body's handling of medicines.

Q80. What is a circadian rhythm?

Answer: A circadian rhythm is a biological cycle of about 24 hours that influences processes such as sleep, hormone release, body temperature, and drug responses.

Q81. What is chronotherapy?

Answer: Chronotherapy means timing treatment to align with biological rhythms or daily patterns of disease, when evidence shows timing may improve effectiveness or reduce harm.

Q82. What is pharmacogenomics?

Answer: Pharmacogenomics studies how genetic variation influences drug response and may help guide medicine selection or dosing for particular patients.

Q83. What is gene therapy?

Answer: Gene therapy uses genetic material or approaches that modify gene expression to treat or prevent disease; approved uses and risks depend on the specific product and condition.

Q84. What are biosimilars?

Answer: Biosimilars are biological medicines highly similar to an already approved reference biological product, with no clinically meaningful differences in safety, purity, and potency under the applicable regulatory framework.

Q85. What is precision medicine?

Answer: Precision medicine tailors prevention or treatment using relevant information such as genetics, biomarkers, clinical characteristics, and environment.

Q86. Why are ethics important in drug discovery?

Answer: Ethical practice protects research participants and animals, supports valid consent and welfare, promotes scientific integrity, and requires transparent reporting and responsible risk assessment.

Q87. What is the 3Rs principle in animal research?

Answer: Replacement uses alternatives where possible; Reduction minimises the number of animals needed; Refinement reduces pain, distress, and harm.

Q88. Why is post-marketing surveillance necessary?

Answer: Clinical trials may not reveal rare or long-term effects. Surveillance helps identify safety signals and assess medicines in broader real-world use.

Unit V — Toxicology

Q89. Define toxicology.

Answer: Toxicology is the study of harmful effects of chemicals, drugs, and other agents on living organisms, including how toxicity occurs, is assessed, and can be managed.

Q90. Name major branches of toxicology.

Answer: Branches include clinical, forensic, environmental, occupational, regulatory, and mechanistic toxicology.

Q91. What is a poison?

Answer: A poison is a substance that can cause harmful effects when sufficient amounts enter the body by a particular route.

Q92. What is a lethal dose?

Answer: A lethal dose is an amount capable of causing death under specified conditions. Values depend on species, route, exposure conditions, and the substance; a single value does not predict an individual's outcome.

Q93. What is the difference between acute and chronic toxicity?

Answer: Acute toxicity follows a single exposure or exposures over a short period; chronic toxicity develops after repeated or long-term exposure.

Q94. What is sub-acute toxicity testing?

Answer: Sub-acute toxicity testing assesses harmful effects of repeated exposure over a relatively short defined period, according to the relevant study guideline.

Q95. What is carcinogenicity testing?

Answer: It evaluates whether an agent can cause or increase the incidence of cancer under specified conditions.

Q96. What is genotoxicity?

Answer: Genotoxicity is the ability of an agent to damage genetic material, including DNA or chromosomes.

Q97. What is teratogenicity?

Answer: Teratogenicity is the capacity of an exposure to cause abnormal development in an embryo or fetus.

Q98. What is safety pharmacology?

Answer: Safety pharmacology studies potential undesirable effects of a drug on vital physiological functions, especially cardiovascular, respiratory, and central nervous systems.

Q99. What are ICH and OECD guidelines?

Answer: ICH develops harmonised technical guidelines for pharmaceutical development and registration. OECD develops internationally used test guidelines and principles, including for chemical safety testing.

Q100. What are the general principles of managing acute poisoning?

Answer: Priorities are to protect rescuers, assess and stabilise airway, breathing and circulation, identify the substance and timing where possible, contact emergency services or a poison information centre, and provide appropriate supportive care.

Q101. What should be done first if poisoning is suspected?

Answer: Seek urgent medical or poison-centre advice. Do not induce vomiting or give home remedies unless instructed by qualified professionals; preserve the container or details of the substance if safe.

Q102. What are antidotes?

Answer: Antidotes are substances that prevent, neutralise, or counteract a poison's effects through specific or supportive mechanisms. Not all poisons have a specific antidote.

Q103. Give examples of antidotes and their uses.

Answer: Examples include naloxone for opioid toxicity, N-acetylcysteine for paracetamol (acetaminophen) poisoning, and atropine with pralidoxime in selected organophosphate poisoning cases. Treatment must be directed by trained clinicians.

Q104. How is heavy metal poisoning managed in principle?

Answer: Management includes stopping exposure, assessing the metal and severity, supportive care, and chelation only when indicated by a qualified clinician. The chelator and regimen depend on the metal and clinical circumstances.

Q105. What is decontamination in poisoning?

Answer: Decontamination aims to reduce continued absorption of a toxic substance, using methods selected for the exposure. Some methods, such as activated charcoal, are appropriate only in selected cases and under clinical guidance.

Q106. Why is induced vomiting generally not recommended in poisoning?

Answer: It can cause aspiration and further injury, particularly with corrosive substances or hydrocarbons, and does not reliably remove the poison.

Q107. What is an exposure-response relationship in toxicology?

Answer: It describes how the likelihood or severity of harmful effects changes with the amount, concentration, or duration of exposure.

Q108. Why are Good Laboratory Practice and proper records important in toxicity studies?

Answer: They support traceability, reliable data, quality assurance, and regulatory review of non-clinical safety studies.
