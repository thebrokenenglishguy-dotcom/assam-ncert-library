Unit I — Fundamentals of Dosage Form Development

Q1. What is a dosage form?

Answer: A dosage form is the physical form in which a medicine is supplied, such as a tablet, capsule, solution, suspension, cream, or injection.

Q2. What is pre-formulation?

Answer: Pre-formulation is the study of the physical and chemical properties of a drug substance before designing a dosage form.

Q3. Why are pre-formulation studies important?

Answer: They help select suitable excipients, manufacturing methods, packaging, storage conditions, and a dosage form that remains safe, effective, and stable.

Q4. What is solubility, and why is it important in dosage form design?

Answer: Solubility is the amount of a substance that dissolves in a specified solvent under defined conditions. It can affect dissolution, absorption, bioavailability, and formulation choices.

Q5. What is pKa?

Answer: pKa is the negative logarithm of an acid dissociation constant. It helps predict the ionisation of weak acids and bases at a given pH.

Q6. How does pH affect drug solubility?

Answer: For ionisable drugs, changing pH can change the balance between ionised and unionised forms and therefore change apparent solubility. The direction and size of the effect depend on the drug.

Q7. Differentiate crystalline and amorphous forms.

Answer: Crystalline solids have an ordered molecular arrangement; amorphous solids lack long-range order. Amorphous forms may dissolve faster but are often less physically stable.

Q8. What is polymorphism?

Answer: Polymorphism is the ability of a solid substance to exist in more than one crystal structure. Different forms may differ in solubility, melting point, stability, and processing behaviour.

Q9. What is particle size, and how can it affect a drug product?

Answer: Particle size describes the dimensions of powder particles. It can influence dissolution rate, blend uniformity, flow, content uniformity, and suspension stability.

Q10. Why is particle shape important?

Answer: Particle shape affects packing, friction, flow, surface area, mixing, and compaction of powders.

Q11. Name common powder-flow parameters.

Answer: Common measures include angle of repose, flow through an orifice, bulk density, tapped density, Carr's compressibility index, and Hausner ratio.

Q12. What is Carr's compressibility index?

Answer: It estimates powder compressibility from bulk and tapped density: CI (%) = [(tapped density − bulk density) / tapped density] × 100. Interpretation depends on the material and method.

Q13. What is the Hausner ratio?

Answer: Hausner ratio = tapped density / bulk density. A higher ratio often indicates greater interparticle interaction and poorer flow, though it is not a standalone acceptance test.

Q14. What is drug–excipient compatibility?

Answer: It is the assessment of whether a drug and an excipient interact in ways that could affect product quality, stability, safety, or performance.

Q15. How is drug–excipient compatibility evaluated?

Answer: Screening may use visual observation, thermal analysis such as DSC, spectroscopy, chromatography, and stability studies. No single test proves compatibility in every formulation.

Q16. What factors are considered when selecting packaging?

Answer: Consider moisture, oxygen, light, product compatibility, protection from contamination, mechanical strength, dose delivery, child resistance where required, and regulatory requirements.

Q17. What is container–closure interaction?

Answer: It is an interaction between the medicine and its packaging system, such as adsorption, absorption, leaching, permeation, or loss of volatile components.

Q18. What is drug-product stability?

Answer: Stability is the ability of a drug substance or product to remain within specified quality limits, including identity, strength, purity, and performance, throughout its shelf life.

Q19. What is ICH Q1 guidance concerned with?

Answer: The ICH Q1 series covers stability testing of new drug substances and products, including study design, storage conditions, and evaluation of stability data.

Q20. Differentiate long-term and accelerated stability studies.

Answer: Long-term studies use recommended storage conditions over the intended shelf life; accelerated studies use more stressful conditions to help assess stability and potential degradation pathways.

Unit II — Compressed Solids: Tablets and Tablet Coating

Q21. What is a tablet?

Answer: A tablet is a solid unit dosage form containing one or more drug substances, usually prepared by compression, with excipients as needed.

Q22. Name common tablet excipients and their roles.

Answer: Diluents add bulk; binders provide cohesion; disintegrants help tablets break apart; lubricants reduce friction; glidants improve flow; and coatings protect or modify product performance.

Q23. Give examples of common tablet excipients.

Answer: Examples include lactose or microcrystalline cellulose as diluents, povidone as a binder, croscarmellose sodium as a disintegrant, magnesium stearate as a lubricant, and colloidal silicon dioxide as a glidant.

Q24. What is tablet compression tooling?

Answer: Tooling consists mainly of punches and dies that shape powder or granules into tablets during compression.

Q25. What factors matter when selecting tablet punches and dies?

Answer: Consider tablet shape and size, punch-tip design, material and coating, production speed, compatibility with the press, and resistance to wear and corrosion.

Q26. What are the main tablet manufacturing methods?

Answer: The main methods are direct compression, wet granulation followed by compression, and dry granulation followed by compression.

Q27. What is direct compression?

Answer: Direct compression blends suitable powders and compresses them without a granulation step. It requires materials with appropriate flow and compactibility.

Q28. What is wet granulation?

Answer: Wet granulation uses a granulating liquid, often containing a binder, to form granules that are then dried, sized, blended, and compressed.

Q29. What is dry granulation?

Answer: Dry granulation densifies powder into slugs or ribbons without a granulating liquid; the material is then milled and compressed.

Q30. What is in-process quality control (IPQC) for tablets?

Answer: IPQC checks manufacturing parameters during production, such as blend uniformity, tablet weight, thickness, hardness, appearance, and other controls defined in the product procedure.

Q31. What tests are commonly performed on finished tablets?

Answer: Tests may include appearance, identification and assay, uniformity of dosage units, dissolution, disintegration where applicable, friability, hardness as an in-process or product-specific measure, and microbial limits when relevant.

Q32. What is tablet weight variation?

Answer: It is the difference between individual tablet weights and the batch average, assessed using the applicable pharmacopoeial or approved specification.

Q33. What is tablet hardness?

Answer: Hardness is a measure of the force needed to break a tablet under a defined test. It can help monitor mechanical strength but does not replace dissolution or other performance tests.

Q34. What is friability?

Answer: Friability measures a tablet's tendency to chip or abrade during a standardised tumbling test.

Q35. What is disintegration?

Answer: Disintegration is the breaking of a tablet into smaller particles under specified test conditions; it is distinct from dissolution, which measures drug entering solution.

Q36. What is dissolution testing?

Answer: Dissolution testing measures the rate and extent to which a drug substance goes into solution from a dosage form under defined conditions.

Q37. What is capping in tablets?

Answer: Capping is separation of the top or bottom portion of a tablet from the main body. Causes can include trapped air, poor granule properties, excessive fines, or unsuitable compression settings.

Q38. What is lamination?

Answer: Lamination is splitting of a tablet into two or more horizontal layers, often associated with air entrapment or formulation and compression problems.

Q39. What causes sticking and picking?

Answer: Sticking is adhesion of material to punch surfaces; picking is local removal of material from the tablet surface, often at embossed areas. Moisture, formulation, tooling, and process settings can contribute.

Q40. What is mottling?

Answer: Mottling is uneven colour distribution on a tablet, potentially caused by dye migration, poor mixing, ingredient colour differences, or drying conditions.

Q41. Why is tablet packaging important?

Answer: Packaging protects tablets from moisture, light, oxygen, contamination, mechanical damage, and mix-ups while providing identification and storage information.

Q42. What is tablet coating?

Answer: Tablet coating applies a layer around a tablet to improve appearance, protect the drug, mask taste, ease swallowing, or modify release.

Q43. Differentiate sugar coating and film coating.

Answer: Sugar coating builds up layers of sugar-based materials and can substantially increase tablet size; film coating applies a thinner polymer-based film and is generally faster.

Q44. What is enteric coating?

Answer: Enteric coating resists release in the acidic stomach and permits release at a higher intestinal pH, when designed and tested for that purpose.

Q45. What is compression coating?

Answer: Compression coating places a dry powder or granule layer around a preformed core by compression, allowing separation of ingredients or specialised release designs.

Q46. What are common coating polymers?

Answer: Examples include hydroxypropyl methylcellulose for film coating and pH-dependent methacrylic acid copolymers for certain enteric coatings. Polymer choice depends on the target performance.

Q47. Name common tablet-coating defects.

Answer: Defects include picking, sticking, peeling, cracking, bridging, mottling, twinning, roughness, and colour variation.

Q48. What quality checks are used for coated tablets?

Answer: Checks may include appearance, coat uniformity, weight gain, defects, assay, dosage-unit uniformity, dissolution, and other product-specific tests.

Unit III — Powders, Granules and Capsules

Q49. What are medicated powders?

Answer: Medicated powders are dry solid preparations containing one or more drugs, with or without excipients, for internal or external use as appropriate.

Q50. What are granules?

Answer: Granules are aggregates of powder particles. They may improve flow, reduce dust, support uniform dosing, and serve as an intermediate for tablets or capsules.

Q51. What formulation factors matter for powders and granules?

Answer: Consider dose, particle size, flow, moisture sensitivity, taste, compatibility, uniformity, solubility, and the intended route and packaging.

Q52. What is powder mixing?

Answer: Powder mixing distributes ingredients through a blend to achieve the required uniformity. Particle size, density, shape, moisture, and mixing time can affect the result.

Q53. What is granulation used for?

Answer: Granulation can improve flow, reduce segregation and dust, and enhance compressibility or handling, depending on the formulation.

Q54. How are granules evaluated?

Answer: Evaluation may include particle-size distribution, flow, moisture content, bulk and tapped density, assay or content uniformity, and performance tests appropriate to the product.

Q55. What is a capsule dosage form?

Answer: A capsule is a unit dosage form in which a drug formulation is enclosed in a shell, commonly made from gelatin or another suitable material.

Q56. Differentiate hard and soft capsules.

Answer: Hard capsules usually consist of two fitted shell pieces and often contain powders, granules or pellets. Soft capsules have a one-piece flexible shell, usually containing a liquid or semi-solid fill.

Q57. What are hard gelatin capsule shells made from?

Answer: They commonly contain gelatin and water, with permitted colourants or opacifiers when needed. Exact composition depends on the product and manufacturer.

Q58. How are empty hard capsule shells manufactured?

Answer: In a common process, pins are dipped into a gelatin solution, rotated for even distribution, dried, stripped, trimmed, and joined into cap-and-body pieces.

Q59. What are non-gelatin capsule shells?

Answer: They use alternatives such as hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) or other suitable polymers. They may be selected for particular formulation, dietary, or stability requirements.

Q60. What are common hard-capsule filling methods?

Answer: Filling may be manual, semi-automatic, or automatic, using methods such as tamping, dosator systems, or powder dosing depending on the equipment.

Q61. What in-process checks are used during hard-capsule filling?

Answer: Checks can include fill weight, capsule locking, shell integrity, appearance, machine settings, and periodic content-uniformity checks as specified.

Q62. What finished-product tests may apply to hard capsules?

Answer: Tests may include appearance, identification and assay, uniformity of dosage units, disintegration, dissolution, moisture, and microbial quality where applicable.

Q63. What is a soft gelatin capsule?

Answer: A soft gelatin capsule is a one-piece, sealed shell containing a liquid, solution, suspension, or semi-solid fill compatible with the shell.

Q64. What are plasticisers in soft gelatin shells?

Answer: Plasticisers, such as glycerol or sorbitol in suitable formulations, increase shell flexibility and reduce brittleness.

Q65. What is the rotary-die process for soft capsules?

Answer: Two ribbons of shell material meet between rotating dies while a metered fill is injected; the dies form, fill, seal, and cut the capsules in a continuous process.

Q66. What are common soft-capsule defects?

Answer: Possible defects include leakage, seam problems, misshapen capsules, air bubbles, shell brittleness or tackiness, and fill or weight variation.

Q67. How is soft-capsule stability evaluated?

Answer: Stability studies monitor shell and fill appearance, leakage, moisture, assay, degradation products, dissolution or release performance, and packaging compatibility as appropriate.

Q68. What packaging is used for capsules?

Answer: Packaging is selected to protect against moisture, oxygen, light and physical damage; options may include suitable blister packs or tightly closed containers with appropriate barriers.

Unit IV — Modified-Release Solids

Q69. What is a modified-release dosage form?

Answer: It is a dosage form designed to change the rate, time, or place of drug release compared with an immediate-release product.

Q70. Differentiate sustained-release and controlled-release tablets.

Answer: Sustained-release products aim to extend drug release or effect. Controlled-release products aim for a more deliberately controlled release pattern; actual performance depends on the design and evidence.

Q71. What are the advantages of modified-release formulations?

Answer: They may reduce dosing frequency, support adherence, smooth concentration fluctuations, and improve convenience for suitable drugs and patients.

Q72. What are the disadvantages of modified-release formulations?

Answer: They may be more complex and costly, less flexible for dose adjustment, and vulnerable to dose dumping or altered performance if crushed, split, or used incorrectly.

Q73. What is dose dumping?

Answer: Dose dumping is unintended rapid release of a large fraction of a modified-release drug dose, potentially causing toxicity or loss of controlled delivery.

Q74. What factors guide formulation design for modified release?

Answer: Important factors include drug half-life, dose, solubility, permeability, stability, therapeutic window, absorption site, polymer properties, and the intended release profile.

Q75. How do matrix tablets control drug release?

Answer: Drug is dispersed in a polymer or other matrix. Release may occur through diffusion, matrix swelling, erosion, dissolution, or a combination.

Q76. How do reservoir systems control drug release?

Answer: A drug core is surrounded by a rate-controlling membrane through which the drug diffuses or from which it is released by a designed mechanism.

Q77. How can osmotic systems release a drug?

Answer: Water enters through a semi-permeable membrane, creating osmotic pressure that drives drug solution or suspension through a delivery orifice.

Q78. Name polymers used in sustained or controlled release.

Answer: Examples include hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, ethylcellulose, certain methacrylate copolymers, and other validated release-controlling materials.

Q79. What is the role of dissolution testing in modified-release products?

Answer: It characterises release over time under defined conditions and helps assess batch consistency, formulation performance, and relevant quality requirements.

Q80. What is an in vitro release profile?

Answer: It is a plot or dataset showing the amount or percentage of drug released over time in a specified test system.

Q81. Why can food affect some modified-release medicines?

Answer: Food can alter gastrointestinal transit, fluid conditions, bile secretion, or dosage-form behaviour. The significance is product-specific and should be assessed in appropriate studies.

Q82. Why should modified-release tablets not be crushed unless authorised?

Answer: Crushing can destroy the release-controlling structure, leading to rapid release, reduced effectiveness, or toxicity. Follow product-specific instructions from an authorised source.

Q83. What are key quality tests for modified-release tablets?

Answer: They may include identity, assay, uniformity of dosage units, dissolution at multiple time points, impurities, stability, and mechanical tests specified for the product.

Q84. What is the difference between delayed release and extended release?

Answer: Delayed release begins release after a lag or at a specified location; extended release prolongs release over time. A product may be designed to combine features.

Unit V — Microencapsulation

Q85. What is microencapsulation?

Answer: Microencapsulation encloses small particles or droplets of a core material within a coating or matrix to form microscopic capsules or particles.

Q86. Why is microencapsulation used in pharmacy?

Answer: It can protect unstable ingredients, mask taste or odour, separate incompatible materials, reduce volatility, improve handling, or modify drug release.

Q87. What are the disadvantages of microencapsulation?

Answer: Potential disadvantages include added cost and process complexity, variable loading or particle size, residual processing materials, and possible changes in release or stability.

Q88. What are the core and wall materials?

Answer: The core is the drug or material being enclosed; the wall or coating is the polymer or other material that surrounds or disperses the core.

Q89. What is spray drying?

Answer: Spray drying atomises a liquid feed into a hot gas stream, rapidly removing solvent to form dry particles. It is used when the material can tolerate the process conditions.

Q90. What is spray congealing?

Answer: Spray congealing atomises a molten or softened material into a cooler environment, where droplets solidify to form particles without relying on solvent evaporation.

Q91. What is extrusion and spheronisation?

Answer: It is a pelletisation process in which a wet mass is extruded into rods, then rounded by spheronisation to form near-spherical pellets, which are dried and processed further.

Q92. What is fluidised-bed coating?

Answer: In fluidised-bed coating, particles or pellets are suspended in an upward air stream while a coating liquid is sprayed onto them and dried.

Q93. What is phase-separation coacervation?

Answer: Coacervation forms a polymer-rich phase that deposits around dispersed core material; the coating is then hardened or stabilised using an appropriate process.

Q94. What is the difference between a microcapsule and a microsphere?

Answer: A microcapsule often has a distinct core surrounded by a wall, while a microsphere generally has the drug dispersed throughout a matrix. Actual structures depend on the manufacturing method.

Q95. What properties are evaluated in microcapsules?

Answer: Evaluation can include particle size and distribution, shape and surface, drug loading, encapsulation efficiency, moisture, residual solvent where relevant, release profile, and stability.

Q96. What is encapsulation efficiency?

Answer: Encapsulation efficiency is the percentage of the initial drug amount successfully retained in the microcapsules: (encapsulated drug / initial drug used) × 100.

Q97. What is drug loading?

Answer: Drug loading is the amount of drug in the final microcapsule product, often expressed as a percentage of total particle mass.

Q98. How is drug release from microcapsules evaluated?

Answer: Release is measured over time using a suitable in vitro method with defined media, temperature, agitation and sampling, followed by drug quantification.

Q99. What factors affect microcapsule size?

Answer: Factors include feed viscosity, surface tension, atomisation conditions, polymer concentration, core-to-wall ratio, stirring, and the specific process.

Q100. How can microencapsulation improve stability?

Answer: A suitable coating can reduce exposure to oxygen, moisture, light, or incompatible materials, but protection must be demonstrated for the particular formulation.

Q101. What is the role of polymers in microencapsulation?

Answer: Polymers form the wall or matrix and influence particle strength, compatibility, permeability, biodegradation, and drug-release rate.

Q102. What packaging considerations apply to microencapsulated products?

Answer: Packaging should preserve moisture and chemical stability, prevent damage or agglomeration, and remain compatible with the final dosage form and intended storage conditions.
