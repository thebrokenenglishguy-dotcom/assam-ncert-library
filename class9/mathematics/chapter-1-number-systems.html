1. Real Numbers

Rational numbers

A number is rational if it can be written in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.

Examples: 2/3, −5/4, 7 = 7/1, 0.25 = 1/4.

Irrational numbers

A number is irrational if it cannot be written as p/q for integers p and q, with q not equal to zero. Its decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-repeating.

Examples: √2, √3, π.

Real numbers

All rational and irrational numbers together form the set of real numbers. Every real number corresponds to a unique point on the number line, and every point on the number line corresponds to a real number.

Real numbers = Rational numbers ∪ Irrational numbers

Important: The sum or product of two irrational numbers may be rational or irrational. For example, (4 + √5) + (4 − √5) = 8 is rational, and (4 + √5)(4 − √5) = 11 is also rational.

2. Roots and Radical Notation

If a > 0 and n is a positive integer, then ⁿ√a = b means bⁿ = a and b > 0. The symbol √ is called the radical sign.

Examples: √8 is the positive number whose square is 8; ∛8 = 2 because 2³ = 8; ⁵√243 = 3 because 3⁵ = 243.

3. Identities Involving Square Roots

For positive real numbers a and b:

√(ab) = √a × √b
√(a/b) = √a / √b
(√a + √b)(√a − √b) = a − b
(a + √b)(a − √b) = a² − b
(√a + √b)(√c + √d) = √ac + √ad + √bc + √bd
(√a + √b)² = a + 2√ab + b
Example 1: Simplify (5 + √5)(5 − √5).

Use (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b².

= 5² − (√5)² = 25 − 5

Answer: 20

Example 2: Simplify (√3 + √7)².

= (√3)² + 2(√3)(√7) + (√7)²

= 3 + 2√21 + 7

Answer: 10 + 2√21

Example 3: Simplify (√11 − √7)(√11 + √7).

= 11 − 7

Answer: 4

4. Rationalisation of Denominators

Rationalising the denominator means converting a fraction into an equivalent fraction whose denominator is rational (does not contain a surd).

Case 1: Denominator contains one square root

Example 4: Rationalise 1/√2.

Multiply numerator and denominator by √2.

1/√2 × √2/√2 = √2/2

Answer: √2/2

Case 2: Denominator contains a sum or difference of two terms

Multiply by the conjugate. The conjugate of a + √b is a − √b, and vice versa.

Example 5: Rationalise 1/(2 + √3).

Multiply by the conjugate, (2 − √3).

1/(2 + √3) × (2 − √3)/(2 − √3)

= (2 − √3)/(4 − 3)

Answer: 2 − √3

Example 6: Rationalise 5/(√3 − √5).

5/(√3 − √5) × (√3 + √5)/(√3 + √5)

= 5(√3 + √5)/(3 − 5)

Answer: −5(√3 + √5)/2

Example 7: Rationalise 1/(7 + 3√2).

1/(7 + 3√2) × (7 − 3√2)/(7 − 3√2)

= (7 − 3√2)/(49 − 18)

Answer: (7 − 3√2)/31

Remember: The product of a binomial surd expression and its conjugate removes the square-root term.

5. Laws of Exponents

For a non-zero base a and suitable integer exponents m and n:

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ
aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ
(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
a⁰ = 1, when a ≠ 0
a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ
aᵐbᵐ = (ab)ᵐ
Example 8: Simplify 17² × 17⁵.

= 17²⁺⁵

Answer: 17⁷

Example 9: Simplify 23¹⁰ ÷ 23⁷.

= 23¹⁰⁻⁷

Answer: 23³

Example 10: Simplify (5²)⁷.

= 5²ˣ⁷

Answer: 5¹⁴

6. Fractional Exponents

For a positive real number a and a positive integer n:

ⁿ√a = a^(1/n)
a^(m/n) = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ = ⁿ√(aᵐ)

For positive a and rational exponents p and q, the extended laws are:

aᵖ × aᑫ = aᵖ⁺ᑫ
(aᵖ)ᑫ = aᵖᑫ
aᵖ ÷ aᑫ = aᵖ⁻ᑫ
aᵖbᵖ = (ab)ᵖ
Example 11: Find 64^(1/2).

64^(1/2) = √64

Answer: 8

Example 12: Simplify 2^(2/3) × 2^(1/3).

= 2^(2/3 + 1/3) = 2¹

Answer: 2

Example 13: Simplify (3^(1/5))⁴.

= 3^(4/5)

Answer: 3^(4/5)

7. Quick Revision and Common Mistakes

  • A rational number can be written as p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.
  • An irrational number has a non-terminating, non-repeating decimal expansion.
  • Do not assume that the sum of two irrational numbers is always irrational.
  • When rationalising a denominator with a binomial surd, multiply by its conjugate.
  • When multiplying powers with the same base, add the exponents.
  • When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the exponents.
  • When raising a power to another power, multiply the exponents.
  • A negative exponent means reciprocal; it does not make the value negative.
  • a⁰ = 1 only when a is non-zero.
  • a^(1/n) represents the nth root of a (for positive a in this chapter's definition).
