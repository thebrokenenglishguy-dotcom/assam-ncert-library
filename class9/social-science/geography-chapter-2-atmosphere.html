Exercise — Very Short Answers

1. What is atmosphere? Write in brief with data about its structure.

  1. The atmosphere is the blanket of gases surrounding the Earth.
  2. It is held around the Earth by gravity.
  3. It contains about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and about 1% other gases, including argon and carbon dioxide.
  4. It also contains water vapour and dust particles.
  5. Most of its mass is found close to the Earth's surface.

2. What are the main layers of the atmosphere based on chemical composition of the gases? Write briefly about their characteristics.

  1. Homosphere: Extends from the surface to about 80–90 km. Gases are well mixed and their proportions remain nearly constant.
  2. Heterosphere: Lies above the homosphere. Gases become separated according to their molecular weight.
  3. Heavier gases are found mainly at lower levels of the heterosphere.
  4. Lighter gases, such as hydrogen and helium, are found at higher levels.

3. Write with diagram about the layers of the atmosphere based on the variations in altitude and temperature and write the characteristics of each layer in brief.

  1. Troposphere: Lowest layer. Weather occurs here and temperature generally decreases with height.
  2. Stratosphere: Contains the ozone layer. Temperature increases with height.
  3. Mesosphere: Temperature decreases with height. Many meteors burn here.
  4. Thermosphere: Temperature rises greatly. It includes much of the ionosphere, which helps radio communication.
  5. Exosphere: Outermost, very thin layer that gradually merges into space.

4. Discuss with examples the factors responsible for variation in atmospheric pressure.

  1. Temperature: Warm air expands and becomes lighter, so pressure is usually low. Cold air is denser, so pressure is usually high.
  2. Altitude: Air pressure decreases with height because less air lies above us.
  3. Water vapour: Moist air is lighter than dry air at the same temperature and pressure, so it can have lower pressure.
  4. Earth's rotation: It affects air movement and the distribution of pressure belts.

5. With the help of diagram discuss the characteristics of the major pressure belts of the world.

  1. Equatorial low: Near 0°. Strong heating makes air rise, creating low pressure.
  2. Subtropical highs: Around 30° north and south. Air descends and creates high pressure.
  3. Subpolar lows: Around 60° north and south. Warm and cold air meet, and air rises.
  4. Polar highs: Near 90° north and south. Very cold, dense air creates high pressure.
  5. These belts shift north and south with the apparent movement of the Sun.

6. Write the importance of atmosphere towards creation of a favourable physical environment on the earth.

  1. It supplies oxygen for breathing and carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.
  2. It protects life from harmful solar radiation through the ozone layer.
  3. It keeps the Earth's temperature suitable for life.
  4. It provides water vapour for clouds and rainfall.
  5. It makes weather and the water cycle possible.

7. What is wind? Discuss the factors of origin of wind.

  1. Wind is air moving mainly from high-pressure areas towards low-pressure areas.
  2. Unequal heating of the Earth's surface creates pressure differences.
  3. Pressure Gradient Force starts air moving from high to low pressure.
  4. Earth's rotation changes the direction of moving air through the Coriolis force.
  5. Friction with the Earth's surface slows wind and changes its direction near the ground.

Exercise — Descriptive Answers

8. What is the most important reason for the origin of wind? Briefly discuss the factors determining velocity and direction of wind.

  1. The main reason for wind is the difference in air pressure caused mainly by unequal heating.
  2. Pressure Gradient Force: A greater pressure difference over a short distance produces faster wind.
  3. Coriolis force: Earth's rotation deflects wind to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere.
  4. Friction: It slows wind near the surface and affects its direction.

9. What do you mean by ‘Coriolis Force’? With the help of diagram briefly describe its contribution in determining the direction of wind.

  1. Coriolis force is the apparent deflection of moving air due to the Earth's rotation.
  2. In the Northern Hemisphere, wind is deflected to the right of its direction of movement.
  3. In the Southern Hemisphere, wind is deflected to the left.
  4. It is zero at the Equator and strongest near the poles.

10. What is meant by ‘Pressure Gradient Force’? What is its contribution in air movement?

  1. Pressure Gradient Force is the force caused by a difference in air pressure over a distance.
  2. It pushes air from high pressure towards low pressure.
  3. It is the main force that starts horizontal air movement.
  4. A steep pressure difference produces stronger winds.

11. Briefly discuss the contribution of the earth’s gravitational force in air movement.

  1. Gravity pulls air towards the Earth's surface.
  2. It gives weight to air and helps create atmospheric pressure.
  3. It keeps the atmosphere from escaping into space.
  4. It causes pressure to decrease with increasing altitude.

12. What do you mean by naming of wind? How the wind blowing over a place is named?

  1. Winds are named according to the direction from which they blow.
  2. A north wind blows from the north towards the south.
  3. A south-west wind blows from the south-west towards the north-east.
  4. Wind direction is usually reported with a wind vane or other weather instruments.

13. How the velocity of wind is determined? What are the units of wind velocity?

  1. Wind speed is measured with an instrument called an anemometer.
  2. A cup anemometer rotates faster when wind speed increases.
  3. Wind velocity is commonly measured in metres per second (m/s) or kilometres per hour (km/h).
  4. Knots are also used, especially in weather reports for ships and aircraft.

14. How the classification of wind is done? Briefly discuss with examples.

  1. Primary or planetary winds: Blow regularly over large areas, such as trade winds and westerlies.
  2. Secondary or seasonal winds: Change with seasons, such as monsoon winds.
  3. Local winds: Blow over small areas for a short time, such as land breeze and sea breeze.

15. What do you mean by primary circulation of wind? Show distribution of primary circulations in a diagram. Mention its important characteristics.

  1. Primary circulation means the permanent global wind system of the Earth.
  2. Trade winds blow from subtropical high-pressure belts towards the equatorial low.
  3. Westerlies blow from subtropical highs towards subpolar lows.
  4. Polar easterlies blow from polar highs towards subpolar lows.
  5. These winds help transfer heat from low latitudes towards high latitudes.

16. What is secondary circulation of wind? Discuss its contribution in determining the climate of a place with examples.

  1. Secondary circulation includes winds that change with seasons or are linked with pressure systems.
  2. Monsoon winds bring seasonal rainfall to India and many parts of Asia.
  3. Cyclones can bring strong winds and heavy rain to coastal areas.
  4. These winds affect rainfall, temperature and the seasonal climate of a place.

17. What is local wind? Briefly discuss with examples how local wind determines the weather condition of a place.

  1. Local winds blow over a small area for a short period.
  2. Sea breeze: Blows from sea to land during the day and cools coastal areas.
  3. Land breeze: Blows from land to sea at night.
  4. Mountain breeze: Cold air moves down mountain slopes at night.
  5. Valley breeze: Air moves up mountain slopes during the day.

18. What is a cyclone? What are its types? Mention briefly how it influences the climate of a place.

  1. A cyclone is a large system of winds that moves around a low-pressure centre.
  2. Tropical cyclones form over warm tropical oceans.
  3. Extra-tropical cyclones form mainly in middle and high latitudes, along fronts.
  4. Cyclones may bring strong winds, heavy rain, storms and floods.
  5. They can change local temperature and rainfall for a short period.

19. What is an air mass? How it is classified? What is its contribution in determining the climate of a place?

  1. An air mass is a large body of air with similar temperature and moisture throughout.
  2. It is classified by its source region: continental (dry) or maritime (moist).
  3. It may also be tropical (warm) or polar (cold).
  4. Air masses bring their temperature and moisture conditions to the places they move over.
  5. Therefore, they influence the weather and climate of a region.

20. What do you mean by ‘Front’? How it is formed? Briefly discuss the relationship between climate and front.

  1. A front is the boundary between two air masses with different temperatures and moisture.
  2. It forms when two different air masses meet.
  3. A cold front forms when cold air advances and pushes warm air upward.
  4. A warm front forms when warm air advances over colder air.
  5. Fronts often cause clouds, rain, storms and changes in temperature.

21. What do you mean by ‘Monsoon Wind’? How it is formed? In which areas of the earth its impact is quite distinct?

  1. Monsoon winds are seasonal winds that change direction with the seasons.
  2. They form mainly because land and sea heat and cool at different rates.
  3. In summer, moist winds often blow from the sea towards the land and bring rain.
  4. In winter, winds often blow from land towards the sea and are usually drier.
  5. Monsoons are important in South Asia, Southeast Asia and parts of Africa and northern Australia.

22. Write with reasons:

(a) Why do all weather phenomena occur mainly in troposphere?

  1. The troposphere contains most of the atmosphere's water vapour and dust.
  2. Clouds, rain, storms and most other weather events need water vapour and air movement.

(b) What would be the consequence of the increase in the amount of carbon dioxide?

  1. Carbon dioxide traps heat in the atmosphere.
  2. More carbon dioxide strengthens the greenhouse effect and causes global warming.
  3. It can lead to climate change, melting ice and rising sea levels.

(c) What is the reason behind the prevalence of high pressure of dry air than moist air?

  1. Water vapour is lighter than the main gases of dry air, especially nitrogen and oxygen.
  2. At the same temperature and pressure, moist air is therefore less dense than dry air.

(d) How does the ozone layer help the living-beings?

  1. The ozone layer absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation.
  2. It protects humans, animals and plants from damage caused by these rays.

(e) Why is the air pressure highest at the sea level?

  1. The weight of the air above is greatest near sea level.
  2. Air pressure decreases as altitude increases.

(f) What is the reason behind variation in vertical extent of the troposphere in the polar region and equatorial region?

  1. Strong heating makes air expand and rise in the equatorial region.
  2. So the troposphere is thicker near the Equator, about 16–18 km.
  3. Cold, dense air makes it thinner near the poles, about 8 km.

(g) Why horizontal movement of wind parallel to the earth’s surface not felt in the equatorial low pressure belt?

  1. The Coriolis force is zero at the Equator.
  2. Air mainly rises in the equatorial low-pressure belt, so horizontal winds are often weak and variable there.

Short Answer Questions

23. Give short answer to the following questions:

(a) What is the vertical extent of the atmosphere?

  1. The atmosphere has no sharp upper boundary.
  2. It becomes extremely thin with height and gradually merges into space.

(b) Up to what altitude from the earth’s surface does chemical composition of gases remain almost same?

  1. It remains nearly uniform in the homosphere, up to about 80–90 km.

(c) What is the name of the boundary line between homosphere and heterosphere?

  1. It is called the homopause or turbopause.

(d) What is Ferrell’s Law?

  1. Ferrel's Law states that moving bodies, including winds, are deflected to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere because of Earth's rotation.

(e) What is Beaufort Scale? Write briefly about the utility of this scale with example.

  1. The Beaufort Scale estimates wind force from its observed effects.
  2. It ranges from 0 (calm) to 12 (hurricane-force).
  3. For example, force 0 means smoke rises almost straight, while force 6 indicates a strong breeze with large branches moving.
  4. It is useful when an exact wind-speed instrument is not available.

(f) Write in brief about Lapse Rate.

  1. Lapse rate is the rate at which air temperature changes with height.
  2. In the troposphere, temperature usually falls by about 6.5°C per kilometre on average. This is the normal environmental lapse rate.

Short Notes

24. Write short note:

(a) Homosphere

  1. The homosphere is the lower part of the atmosphere, extending to about 80–90 km.
  2. Gases are well mixed and their proportions remain nearly constant.

(b) Heterosphere

  1. The heterosphere lies above the homosphere.
  2. Gases are arranged in layers according to their molecular weight.
  3. Lighter gases are found at greater heights.

(c) Air temperature, earth’s surface height and air pressure relationship

  1. Air pressure generally decreases as height above sea level increases.
  2. Warm air expands and rises, often creating low pressure.
  3. Cold air is denser and often creates high pressure.

(d) Polar High Pressure Belt

  1. It lies around the North and South Poles.
  2. Very cold air becomes dense and sinks.
  3. This creates high pressure and helps produce polar easterly winds.

(e) Wind system

  1. A wind system is the regular pattern of air movement over the Earth.
  2. It includes trade winds, westerlies, polar easterlies, monsoon winds and local winds.
  3. It transfers heat and moisture and affects climate.

(f) Pressure Gradient

  1. Pressure gradient means the change in air pressure over a distance.
  2. A steep pressure gradient creates a strong Pressure Gradient Force.
  3. It usually produces faster winds.

(g) Trade wind

  1. Trade winds blow from subtropical high-pressure belts towards the equatorial low-pressure belt.
  2. They blow as north-east trades in the Northern Hemisphere and south-east trades in the Southern Hemisphere.
  3. They are fairly steady winds.

(h) Horse latitude

  1. Horse latitudes are the subtropical high-pressure belts around 30° north and south.
  2. Air generally descends here, so the weather is often calm and dry.

(i) Roaring Forties

  1. Roaring Forties are strong westerly winds found around 40°–50° south latitude.
  2. They blow strongly over the open Southern Ocean.
  3. They are known for rough seas and strong winds.

(j) Anticyclone

  1. An anticyclone is a high-pressure system with air sinking and spreading outward near the surface.
  2. Winds move clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and anticlockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.
  3. It usually brings clear and calm weather.

(k) Jet Stream

  1. Jet streams are narrow bands of very fast winds high in the atmosphere.
  2. They generally flow from west to east.
  3. They influence weather systems and the movement of cyclones.

(l) Isobar

  1. An isobar is a line on a weather map joining places with equal air pressure.
  2. Closely spaced isobars usually show a strong pressure gradient and stronger winds.

(m) Tropical Cyclone

  1. A tropical cyclone is a powerful rotating storm that forms over warm tropical oceans.
  2. It has a low-pressure centre and very strong winds.
  3. It can cause heavy rain, storm surges and serious damage to coastal areas.

Differences

25. Write the differences:

(a) Horizontal wind and Vertical wind

  • Horizontal wind: Air moves parallel to the Earth's surface, mainly from high to low pressure.
  • Vertical wind: Air moves upward or downward, often due to heating or cooling.

(b) Wind and Air mass

  • Wind: Moving air, usually described by its direction and speed.
  • Air mass: A large body of air with similar temperature and moisture.

(c) Tropical cyclone and Extra-tropical cyclone

  • Tropical cyclone: Forms over warm tropical oceans; usually has no fronts.
  • Extra-tropical cyclone: Forms mainly in middle latitudes where warm and cold air masses meet; has fronts.

(d) Sea breeze and Land breeze

  • Sea breeze: Blows from sea to land during the day because land heats faster.
  • Land breeze: Blows from land to sea at night because land cools faster.

(e) Mountain wind and Valley wind

  • Mountain wind: Cold air flows down mountain slopes at night.
  • Valley wind: Warm air moves up mountain slopes during the day.

(f) Cyclone and Anticyclone

  • Cyclone: A low-pressure system; air moves inward and rises.
  • Anticyclone: A high-pressure system; air sinks and spreads outward.

(g) Cold Front and Warm Front

  • Cold front: Cold air advances and pushes warm air up quickly; showers or storms may occur.
  • Warm front: Warm air advances and rises slowly over cold air; widespread gentle rain may occur.

(h) Cold wave and Hot Wave

  • Cold wave: A period of unusually low temperature for a region.
  • Hot wave: A period of unusually high temperature for a region.

(i) Troposphere and Stratosphere

  • Troposphere: Lowest layer; most weather occurs here and temperature usually decreases with height.
  • Stratosphere: Lies above the troposphere; contains the ozone layer and temperature generally increases with height.
