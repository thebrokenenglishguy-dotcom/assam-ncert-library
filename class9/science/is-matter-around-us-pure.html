Class 9 Science — Chapter 2

Is Matter Around Us Pure? | Questions and Answers

Answers are written in simple English from the questions visible in the textbook screenshots supplied for this chapter. Questions not visible in the screenshots are not added.

In-text Questions — What Is a Mixture?

1. What is meant by a substance?

Answer: A substance is a pure form of matter made up of only one kind of particles. It has a fixed composition and characteristic properties.

2. List the points of difference between homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures.

Homogeneous mixtureHeterogeneous mixture
Has a uniform composition throughout.Does not have a uniform composition throughout.
Its components cannot usually be seen separately.Different components or parts may be seen separately.
Example: salt solution, air.Example: soil, oil and water.

In-text Questions — Solutions, Suspensions and Colloids

1. Differentiate between homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures with examples.

Answer: A homogeneous mixture has the same composition throughout, such as salt dissolved in water. A heterogeneous mixture has a non-uniform composition, such as sand mixed with water.

3. To make a saturated solution, 36 g of sodium chloride is dissolved in 100 g of water at 293 K. Find its concentration at this temperature.

Solution:
Mass of solute = 36 g
Mass of solvent = 100 g
Mass of solution = 36 + 100 = 136 g
Mass percentage = (Mass of solute ÷ Mass of solution) × 100
= (36 ÷ 136) × 100 = 26.47% (approximately).

In-text Questions — Separation of Mixtures

1. How will you separate a mixture containing kerosene and petrol, which are miscible and have a difference in boiling points greater than 25°C?

Answer: Use simple distillation. Petrol has the lower boiling point, so it vaporises first and can be condensed and collected separately.

2. Name the technique to separate (i) butter from curd, (ii) salt from sea-water, and (iii) camphor from salt.

Answer:
  1. Butter from curd: centrifugation (churning).
  2. Salt from sea-water: evaporation.
  3. Camphor from salt: sublimation.

3. What types of mixtures are separated by crystallisation?

Answer: Crystallisation is used to obtain a pure solid from its solution. It is useful for purifying solids that are soluble in a suitable solvent and may contain dissolved impurities.

In-text Questions — Physical and Chemical Changes

1. Classify the following as chemical or physical changes: cutting of trees; melting of butter; rusting of an almirah; boiling water to form steam; passing electric current through water so it breaks into hydrogen and oxygen; dissolving common salt in water; making fruit salad; burning paper and wood.

Answer:
  1. Cutting of trees — Physical change.
  2. Melting of butter — Physical change.
  3. Rusting of an almirah — Chemical change.
  4. Boiling water to form steam — Physical change.
  5. Breaking water into hydrogen and oxygen by electric current — Chemical change.
  6. Dissolving common salt in water — Physical change.
  7. Making fruit salad — Physical change.
  8. Burning paper and wood — Chemical change.

2. Try segregating the things around you as pure substances or mixtures.

Answer:
  • Pure substances: distilled water, pure iron, pure copper, oxygen and sugar (pure sucrose).
  • Mixtures: air, milk, soil, tap water, tea and lemonade.
The classification depends on the actual composition of each sample.

Textbook Exercises

1. Which separation technique will you apply?

Answer:
  1. Sodium chloride from its solution in water — evaporation or crystallisation.
  2. Ammonium chloride from sodium chloride and ammonium chloride — sublimation.
  3. Small pieces of metal in engine oil — filtration.
  4. Different pigments from flower-petal extract — chromatography.
  5. Butter from curd — centrifugation (churning).
  6. Oil from water — separating funnel.
  7. Tea leaves from tea — filtration using a strainer.
  8. Iron pins from sand — magnetic separation.
  9. Wheat grains from husk — winnowing.
  10. Fine mud particles suspended in water — centrifugation (or allowing them to settle, followed by decantation).

2. Write the steps you would use for making tea. Use the words solution, solvent, solute, dissolve, soluble, insoluble, filtrate and residue.

Answer:
  1. Take water as the solvent and heat it.
  2. Add sugar, a solute, which is soluble in water and dissolves to form a sugar solution.
  3. Add tea leaves and boil. Some substances from the leaves dissolve in the water.
  4. Add milk if desired and boil again.
  5. Pour the tea through a strainer. The liquid collected is the filtrate, while the tea leaves left in the strainer are the residue. Tea is a mixture, not a pure substance.

3. Pragya tested the solubility of salts at different temperatures.

(a) What mass of potassium nitrate is needed to make a saturated solution in 50 g of water at 313 K?
At 313 K, solubility = 62 g per 100 g water.
For 50 g water: (62 ÷ 100) × 50 = 31 g.

(b) A saturated potassium chloride solution made at 353 K is cooled to room temperature. What is observed?
Some potassium chloride crystals separate out because its solubility decreases as the solution cools.

(c) Find the solubility of each salt at 293 K. Which has the highest solubility?
Potassium nitrate = 32 g; sodium chloride = 36 g; potassium chloride = 35 g; ammonium chloride = 37 g per 100 g water. Ammonium chloride has the highest solubility at 293 K.

(d) What is the effect of temperature on the solubility of a salt?
It depends on the salt. In the given table, potassium nitrate and ammonium chloride become much more soluble as temperature rises; sodium chloride changes very little.

4. Explain with examples: (a) saturated solution, (b) pure substance, (c) colloid, (d) suspension.

Answer:
  1. Saturated solution: A solution that cannot dissolve any more solute at a given temperature. Example: water containing the maximum amount of dissolved salt at that temperature.
  2. Pure substance: Matter made of only one kind of particles and having a fixed composition. Examples: pure iron and oxygen.
  3. Colloid: A heterogeneous mixture with very small particles that do not settle on standing and scatter light. Examples: milk and fog.
  4. Suspension: A heterogeneous mixture with particles large enough to be seen and that settle on standing. Example: muddy water.

5. Classify these as homogeneous or heterogeneous mixtures: soda water, wood, air, soil, vinegar and filtered tea.

Answer:
  • Soda water — Homogeneous (when the gas is dissolved).
  • Wood — Heterogeneous.
  • Air — Homogeneous.
  • Soil — Heterogeneous.
  • Vinegar — Homogeneous.
  • Filtered tea — Homogeneous, if properly filtered and uniformly mixed.

6. How would you confirm that a colourless liquid given to you is pure water?

Answer: Measure its boiling point at normal atmospheric pressure. Pure water boils at 100°C (373 K). A pure sample also freezes at 0°C (273 K) at normal pressure.

7. Which of these materials are pure substances: ice, milk, iron, hydrochloric acid, calcium oxide, mercury, brick, wood and air?

Answer:
  • Pure substances: ice (pure solid water), iron, calcium oxide and mercury.
  • Mixtures: milk, brick, wood and air.
  • Hydrochloric acid: in everyday aqueous form, it is hydrogen chloride dissolved in water, so it is a solution (mixture). Pure hydrogen chloride is a compound.

8. Identify the solutions among these mixtures: soil, sea water, air, coal and soda water.

Answer: Sea water, air and soda water are solutions (homogeneous mixtures, when the gas remains dissolved). Soil and coal are not solutions.

9. Which of the following show the Tyndall effect: salt solution, milk, copper sulphate solution and starch solution?

Answer: Milk and starch solution show the Tyndall effect because they are colloids. Salt solution and copper sulphate solution do not show it.

10. Classify these into elements, compounds and mixtures: sodium, soil, sugar solution, silver, calcium carbonate, tin, silicon, coal, air, soap, methane, carbon dioxide and blood.

Answer:
  • Elements: sodium, silver, tin, silicon.
  • Compounds: calcium carbonate, methane, carbon dioxide.
  • Mixtures: soil, sugar solution, coal, air, soap and blood.

11. Which of the following are chemical changes: growth of a plant, rusting of iron, mixing iron filings and sand, cooking food, digestion of food, freezing water and burning a candle?

Answer:
  • Growth of a plant — Chemical change.
  • Rusting of iron — Chemical change.
  • Mixing iron filings and sand — Physical change.
  • Cooking food — Chemical change.
  • Digestion of food — Chemical change.
  • Freezing water — Physical change.
  • Burning a candle — Both physical and chemical changes. Wax melts (physical) and wax vapour burns (chemical).
