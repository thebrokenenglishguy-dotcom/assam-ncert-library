Class 9 Science — Chapter 4

Structure of the Atom | Questions and Answers

Answers are written in simple English and include the in-text questions and all 19 exercise questions visible in the supplied screenshots.

In-text Questions

1. What are canal rays?

Answer: Canal rays are streams of positively charged particles produced in a gas discharge tube. They were discovered by E. Goldstein.

2. If an atom contains one electron and one proton, will it carry any charge or not?

Answer: It will be electrically neutral. The proton has a +1 charge and the electron has a −1 charge, so the charges cancel each other.

3. On the basis of Thomson’s model of an atom, explain how the atom is neutral as a whole.

Answer: Thomson suggested that an atom is a sphere of positive charge with negatively charged electrons embedded in it. The total positive charge equals the total negative charge, so the atom is neutral.

4. On the basis of Rutherford’s model of an atom, which sub-atomic particle is present in the nucleus of an atom?

Answer: The positively charged proton is present in the nucleus.

5. Draw a sketch of Bohr’s model of an atom with three shells.

Answer: Draw a small central nucleus and three concentric circles around it. Label the circles K, L and M (or first, second and third shells). Place electrons on the shells as appropriate for the atom being represented. For example, a sodium atom has electron distribution 2, 8, 1.

6. What do you think would be the observation if the alpha-particle scattering experiment is carried out using a foil of a metal other than gold?

Answer: The general observations would be similar: most alpha particles would pass straight through, some would be deflected, and very few would bounce back. The exact amount of scattering would depend on the metal and the thickness of the foil.

7. Name the three sub-atomic particles of an atom.

Answer: Electrons, protons and neutrons.

8. A helium atom has an atomic mass of 4 u and two protons in its nucleus. How many neutrons does it have?

Solution: Number of neutrons = mass number − number of protons = 4 − 2 = 2 neutrons.

9. Write the distribution of electrons in carbon and sodium atoms.

Answer: Carbon (atomic number 6): 2, 4. Sodium (atomic number 11): 2, 8, 1.

10. If K and L shells of an atom are full, what would be the total number of electrons in the atom?

Answer: K shell holds 2 electrons and L shell holds 8 electrons. Total = 2 + 8 = 10 electrons.

11. How will you find the valency of chlorine, sulphur and magnesium?

Answer:
  • Chlorine has electronic configuration 2, 8, 7. It needs 1 electron to complete its outer shell, so valency = 1.
  • Sulphur has configuration 2, 8, 6. It needs 2 electrons, so valency = 2.
  • Magnesium has configuration 2, 8, 2. It can lose 2 electrons, so valency = 2.

12. If the number of electrons in an atom is 8 and the number of protons is also 8, then (i) what is the atomic number and (ii) what is the charge on the atom?

Answer: (i) Atomic number = number of protons = 8. (ii) The atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so its net charge is zero.

13. With the help of Table 4.1, find the mass number of oxygen and sulphur atom.

Answer: The common atoms shown in the table have mass numbers: oxygen = 16 and sulphur = 32.

15. Write the electronic configuration of any one pair of isotopes and isobars.

Answer:
  • Isotopes: Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have atomic number 6, so each has electronic configuration 2, 4.
  • Isobars: Argon-40 (atomic number 18) has configuration 2, 8, 8; calcium-40 (atomic number 20) has configuration 2, 8, 8, 2.

2. What are the limitations of J.J. Thomson’s model of the atom?

Answer: Thomson’s model could not explain the results of Rutherford’s alpha-particle scattering experiment. In particular, it could not explain why some alpha particles were deflected through large angles or bounced back, showing that positive charge is concentrated in a tiny nucleus.

3. What are the limitations of Rutherford’s model of the atom?

Answer: According to classical physics, an electron revolving around the nucleus should continuously lose energy and spiral into the nucleus. If so, atoms would be unstable. Rutherford’s model could not explain why atoms remain stable.

4. Describe Bohr’s model of the atom.

Answer:
  1. An atom has a small, positively charged nucleus at its centre.
  2. Electrons revolve around the nucleus in certain fixed orbits or shells.
  3. Each shell has a fixed energy.
  4. Electrons do not lose energy while remaining in a permitted shell.
  5. Electrons absorb or release energy when they move from one shell to another.

5. Compare all the proposed models of an atom given in this chapter.

Answer:
  • Thomson’s model: The atom is a positively charged sphere with electrons embedded in it. Positive and negative charges balance.
  • Rutherford’s model: The atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus. Electrons are outside the nucleus, and most of the atom is empty space.
  • Bohr’s model: Electrons move around the nucleus in fixed shells with definite energies.

6. Summarise the rules for writing the distribution of electrons in various shells for the first eighteen elements.

Answer:
  1. The maximum number of electrons in a shell is given by 2n², where n is the shell number.
  2. The K shell can hold a maximum of 2 electrons and the L shell a maximum of 8.
  3. The outermost shell can contain at most 8 electrons.
  4. Electrons fill inner shells before occupying outer shells.

7. Define valency by taking examples of silicon and oxygen.

Answer: Valency is the combining capacity of an atom. Silicon has electronic configuration 2, 8, 4 and valency 4. Oxygen has configuration 2, 6 and needs 2 electrons to complete its outer shell, so its valency is 2.

8. Explain with examples: (i) atomic number, (ii) mass number, (iii) isotopes and (iv) isobars. Give any two uses of isotopes.

Answer:
  1. Atomic number (Z): Number of protons in the nucleus. Example: carbon has Z = 6.
  2. Mass number (A): Total number of protons and neutrons. Example: carbon-12 has A = 12.
  3. Isotopes: Atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Example: ¹H, ²H and ³H.
  4. Isobars: Atoms of different elements with different atomic numbers but the same mass number. Example: argon-40 and calcium-40.
Uses of isotopes: Cobalt-60 is used in cancer treatment; iodine-131 is used in diagnosing or treating certain thyroid conditions. Uranium-235 is used as fuel in nuclear reactors.

9. Na⁺ has completely filled K and L shells. Explain.

Answer: Sodium has atomic number 11, so a neutral sodium atom has 11 electrons with configuration 2, 8, 1. It loses one electron to form Na⁺, leaving 10 electrons with configuration 2, 8. Thus, both K and L shells are full.

10. If bromine is available in the form of two isotopes, ⁷⁹₃₅Br (49.7%) and ⁸¹₃₅Br (50.3%), calculate the average atomic mass of bromine.

Solution:
Average atomic mass = (79 × 49.7/100) + (81 × 50.3/100)
= 39.263 + 40.743 = 80.006 u ≈ 80.0 u.

11. The average atomic mass of a sample of element X is 16.2 u. What are the percentages of isotopes ¹⁶₈X and ¹⁸₈X in the sample?

Solution: Let the fraction of X-18 be x. Then X-16 fraction is 1 − x.
16(1 − x) + 18x = 16.2
16 + 2x = 16.2, so x = 0.1.
Therefore, X-18 = 10% and X-16 = 90%.

12. If Z = 3, what would be the valency of the element? Also, name the element.

Answer: Atomic number 3 is lithium (Li). Its electronic configuration is 2, 1, so its valency is 1.

13. The nuclei of two atomic species X and Y contain: X: 6 protons, 6 neutrons; Y: 6 protons, 8 neutrons. Give their mass numbers. What is the relation between the two species?

Solution: Mass number = protons + neutrons.
X = 6 + 6 = 12; Y = 6 + 8 = 14.
They are isotopes of carbon because they have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

14. For the following statements, write T for True and F for False.

Answer:
  1. J.J. Thomson proposed that the nucleus of an atom contains only nucleons. — F
  2. A neutron is formed by an electron and a proton combining together. Therefore, it is neutral. — F
  3. The mass of an electron is about 1/2000 times that of a proton. — T
  4. An isotope of iodine is used for making tincture iodine, which is used as a medicine. — F

15. Rutherford’s alpha-particle scattering experiment was responsible for the discovery of: (a) atomic nucleus, (b) electron, (c) proton, (d) neutron.

Answer: (a) Atomic nucleus.

16. Isotopes of an element have: (a) the same physical properties, (b) different chemical properties, (c) different number of neutrons, (d) different atomic numbers.

Answer: (c) Different number of neutrons.

17. Number of valence electrons in Cl⁻ ion are: (a) 16, (b) 8, (c) 17, (d) 18.

Answer: (b) 8. A chlorine atom gains one electron to form Cl⁻, giving it the configuration 2, 8, 8.

18. Which one of the following is a correct electronic configuration of sodium? (a) 2,8 (b) 8,2,1 (c) 2,1,8 (d) 2,8,1.

Answer: (d) 2, 8, 1.
