Chapter 12 — Sound

Important concepts, textbook questions and answers in simple English.

12.1 Production of Sound

1. What is sound?

Sound is a form of energy that produces the sensation of hearing in our ears.

2. How is sound produced?

  • Sound is produced by vibrating objects.
  • When an object vibrates, it makes the particles around it vibrate.
  • These vibrations travel through a medium and reach our ears.

Examples: A tuning fork, a guitar string and our vocal cords produce sound by vibration.

3. What is vibration?

Vibration is the rapid to-and-fro motion of an object around its mean position.

12.2 Propagation of Sound

4. What is a medium?

A medium is the material or substance through which sound travels. Air, water and solids can act as media.

5. How does sound travel through a medium?

  • A vibrating object makes nearby particles vibrate.
  • These particles pass the disturbance to neighbouring particles.
  • The disturbance moves forward through the medium.
  • The particles do not travel all the way from the source to the listener. Energy travels through the medium.

6. What are compression and rarefaction?

  • Compression (C): A region where particles are close together. Pressure and density are high.
  • Rarefaction (R): A region where particles are far apart. Pressure and density are low.

7. Why is sound called a mechanical wave?

Sound needs a material medium to travel. It travels through the vibration of particles of that medium. Therefore, sound is a mechanical wave.

8. Why can sound not travel through a vacuum?

A vacuum has no particles to carry vibrations. Therefore, sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

9. Describe the bell-jar experiment.

  1. An electric bell is placed inside an airtight glass jar.
  2. At first, we can hear the bell.
  3. Air is slowly removed from the jar using a vacuum pump.
  4. The sound becomes fainter, although the bell is still vibrating.
  5. When most of the air is removed, we cannot hear the bell.

Conclusion: Sound needs a material medium to travel.

12.2.2 Sound Waves Are Longitudinal Waves

10. What is a longitudinal wave?

A longitudinal wave is a wave in which particles of the medium vibrate to and fro in a direction parallel to the direction in which the wave travels.

11. Why are sound waves longitudinal in air?

In air, particles vibrate back and forth parallel to the direction of sound travel. This produces compressions and rarefactions. Therefore, sound waves in air are longitudinal waves.

12. What is a transverse wave?

A transverse wave is a wave in which particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction of wave travel. For example, waves on the surface of water are commonly shown as transverse waves.

12.2.3 Characteristics of a Sound Wave

13. What is wavelength?

Wavelength is the distance between two consecutive compressions or two consecutive rarefactions. It is represented by the Greek letter λ (lambda). Its SI unit is metre (m).

14. What is frequency?

Frequency is the number of complete vibrations or oscillations in one second. It is represented by ν (nu) or f. Its SI unit is hertz (Hz).

15. What is time period?

Time period is the time taken to complete one vibration or oscillation. It is represented by T and its SI unit is second (s).

Frequency: ν = 1/T   |   Time period: T = 1/ν

16. What is amplitude?

Amplitude is the maximum displacement of a vibrating particle from its mean position. Greater amplitude generally produces a louder sound.

17. What is pitch?

Pitch tells us whether a sound is high or low. It depends on frequency. Higher frequency gives a higher pitch; lower frequency gives a lower pitch.

18. What is loudness?

Loudness is the way our ears respond to sound. It mainly depends on the amplitude of the sound wave. A larger amplitude usually means a louder sound.

19. What is quality or timbre of sound?

Quality or timbre is the property that helps us distinguish between two sounds having the same pitch and loudness, such as the sound of a violin and a flute.

20. Differentiate between loudness and intensity.

  • Loudness: A sensation produced in our ears. It is a subjective response.
  • Intensity: The sound energy passing each second through a unit area. It is a measurable physical quantity.

21. Write the relation between speed, frequency and wavelength of sound.

Speed = wavelength × frequency
v = λν

Here, v is speed, λ is wavelength and ν is frequency. Also, v = λ/T.

12.2.4 Speed of Sound in Different Media

22. In which medium does sound travel fastest?

Sound generally travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases. Its speed also depends on the nature and temperature of the medium.

23. How does temperature affect the speed of sound in air?

The speed of sound in air increases when the temperature increases.

24. What is a sonic boom?

When an object travels faster than the speed of sound, it is said to be supersonic. The shock waves produced by it create a very loud sound called a sonic boom.

12.3 Reflection of Sound

25. What is reflection of sound?

Reflection of sound is the bouncing back of sound waves after striking a surface.

26. State the laws of reflection of sound.

  1. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.
  2. The incident sound, reflected sound and the normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane.

27. What is an echo?

An echo is the sound heard again after reflection from a distant surface. To hear a distinct echo, the time gap between the original sound and reflected sound should be at least 0.1 second.

28. What is the minimum distance needed to hear a distinct echo?

At about 22°C, the speed of sound in air is approximately 344 m/s.

Minimum total sound path = 344 × 0.1 = 34.4 m
Minimum distance to reflecting surface = 34.4 ÷ 2 = 17.2 m

29. What is reverberation?

Reverberation is the persistence of sound in a hall due to repeated reflections from its walls, roof and other surfaces.

30. How can reverberation be reduced?

  • Cover walls and ceilings with sound-absorbing materials.
  • Use curtains, carpets and rough plaster.
  • Use seats made of sound-absorbing materials.

31. Give three uses of multiple reflection of sound.

  1. Megaphones, horns and trumpets guide sound mainly in one direction.
  2. A stethoscope carries body sounds to a doctor's ears by repeated reflection.
  3. Curved ceilings and soundboards help spread sound across halls.

32. Why are the ceilings of concert halls curved?

Curved ceilings help reflected sound reach different parts of the hall more evenly, so people can hear clearly.

12.4 Range of Hearing

33. What is the audible range of an average human ear?

The audible range for an average human is about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz).

34. What are infrasonic and ultrasonic sounds?

  • Infrasonic: Sound with frequency below 20 Hz.
  • Ultrasonic: Sound with frequency above 20,000 Hz (20 kHz).

35. Name some animals that can hear or use ultrasound.

Dogs, bats, dolphins and porpoises can hear or use high-frequency sounds. Bats use ultrasound to find prey and avoid obstacles.

12.5 Applications of Ultrasound

36. What is ultrasound?

Ultrasound is sound with a frequency greater than 20 kHz. Humans cannot hear it.

37. Write the main uses of ultrasound.

  1. It cleans parts that are difficult to reach, such as spiral tubes and electronic components.
  2. It detects cracks and defects inside metal blocks.
  3. It is used in medical imaging, such as ultrasonography and echocardiography.
  4. It helps detect stones and other abnormalities inside the body.
  5. It can break kidney stones into smaller pieces.

38. How are defects in a metal block detected using ultrasound?

  • Ultrasound is sent through the metal block.
  • If there is a crack or defect, some waves are reflected back from it.
  • Detectors receive these reflected waves.
  • The reflected signal shows the position of the defect.

39. What is ultrasonography?

Ultrasonography is a medical technique that uses reflected ultrasound waves to form images of internal organs, such as the liver, gall bladder, uterus and kidneys.

40. What is echocardiography?

Echocardiography is a technique in which ultrasound waves are reflected from different parts of the heart to form an image of the heart.

41. How is ultrasound used for cleaning?

The object is placed in a cleaning liquid. Ultrasound is sent through the liquid. The vibrations loosen dust, grease and dirt, so the object gets cleaned.

12.5.1 SONAR

42. What is SONAR? Write its full form.

SONAR stands for Sound Navigation And Ranging. It uses ultrasonic waves to find the distance, direction and speed of underwater objects.

43. Explain the working of SONAR.

  1. A transmitter on a ship sends ultrasonic waves into water.
  2. The waves strike an underwater object or the sea bed and are reflected.
  3. A detector receives the reflected waves.
  4. The time between sending and receiving the waves is measured.
  5. The distance is calculated using the speed of sound in water.
Distance to object = (speed of sound × time) ÷ 2
d = vt/2

44. Write two uses of SONAR.

  • To measure the depth of the sea.
  • To locate underwater objects such as submarines, rocks, icebergs and sunken ships.

12.6 Structure of Human Ear

45. Name the three main parts of the human ear.

  1. Outer ear
  2. Middle ear
  3. Inner ear

46. Explain how the human ear works.

  1. The pinna collects sound from the surroundings.
  2. Sound travels through the auditory canal and reaches the eardrum.
  3. The eardrum vibrates.
  4. Three small bones — hammer, anvil and stirrup — increase the vibrations.
  5. The vibrations reach the inner ear and produce signals in the cochlea.
  6. The auditory nerve carries these signals to the brain.
  7. The brain understands these signals as sound.

NCERT Textbook Exercises — Questions and Answers

1. What is sound and how is it produced?

Sound is a form of energy that gives us the sensation of hearing. It is produced by vibrating objects.

2. Describe with the help of a diagram how compressions and rarefactions are produced in air near a source of sound.

  • A vibrating source moves forward and pushes nearby air particles together. This forms a compression (high pressure and density).
  • When it moves backward, particles spread apart. This forms a rarefaction (low pressure and density).
  • The source repeats this motion and creates a series of compressions and rarefactions that travel through air.

Diagram guide: draw a vibrating tuning fork or speaker followed by alternating crowded regions labelled C and spread-out regions labelled R.

3. Cite an experiment to show that sound needs a material medium for its propagation.

Use the electric bell inside an airtight bell jar. As air is removed with a vacuum pump, the bell becomes fainter and finally cannot be heard. The bell still vibrates. This shows that sound needs a material medium.

4. Why is sound wave called a longitudinal wave?

Because particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction in which sound travels. They form compressions and rarefactions.

5. Which characteristic of sound helps you identify your friend by their voice in a dark room?

The quality or timbre of sound helps us identify a person by their voice.

6. Flash and thunder are produced simultaneously. Why is thunder heard a few seconds after the flash?

Light travels much faster than sound. So the flash reaches our eyes almost immediately, while the sound takes more time to reach our ears.

7. A person has a hearing range from 20 Hz to 20 kHz. What are the typical wavelengths in air? Take speed of sound as 344 m/s.

Use λ = v/ν.

  • At 20 Hz: λ = 344/20 = 17.2 m.
  • At 20,000 Hz: λ = 344/20,000 = 0.0172 m (1.72 cm).

8. Two children are at opposite ends of an aluminium rod. One strikes the rod. Find the ratio of times taken by sound in air and aluminium to reach the second child.

For the same distance, time is inversely proportional to speed.

Using speed in air = 346 m/s and in aluminium = 6420 m/s:

Time in air : Time in aluminium = 6420 : 346 ≈ 18.6 : 1

9. The frequency of a source is 100 Hz. How many times does it vibrate in one minute?

100 vibrations occur in one second.

Number in 60 seconds = 100 × 60 = 6000 vibrations

10. Does sound follow the same laws of reflection as light?

Yes. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. The incident sound, reflected sound and normal lie in the same plane.

11. When sound is reflected from a distant object, an echo is produced. If the distance between the reflecting surface and source remains the same, do you hear an echo on a hotter day?

Yes. The speed of sound increases with temperature. On a hotter day, the reflected sound returns sooner, so the time gap is shorter. The echo may still be heard if the time gap is at least 0.1 s.

12. Give two practical applications of reflection of sound waves.

Examples include megaphones or horns that direct sound, and stethoscopes that carry body sounds to a doctor's ears. Curved ceilings and soundboards in halls are also useful applications.

13. A stone is dropped from a 500 m tower into a pond. When is the splash heard at the top? Take g = 10 m/s² and speed of sound = 340 m/s.

Time taken by stone to fall:

h = ½gt²
500 = ½ × 10 × t²
t² = 100; t = 10 s

Time taken by sound to travel up:

t = distance/speed = 500/340 ≈ 1.47 s

Total time = 10 + 1.47 = 11.47 s (approximately).

14. A sound wave travels at 339 m/s and has wavelength 1.5 cm. Find its frequency. Will it be audible?

Given λ = 1.5 cm = 0.015 m.

ν = v/λ = 339/0.015 = 22,600 Hz

It is not audible to an average human ear because it is above 20,000 Hz. It is ultrasound.

15. What is reverberation? How can it be reduced?

Reverberation is the persistence of sound due to repeated reflections. It can be reduced by using curtains, carpets, rough plaster, compressed fibreboard and sound-absorbing seats.

16. What is loudness of sound? What factors does it depend on?

Loudness is the response of our ears to sound. It mainly depends on the amplitude of the sound wave. It also depends on the distance from the source and the sensitivity of the listener's ear.

17. Explain how bats use ultrasound to catch their prey.

Bats produce high-frequency ultrasonic sounds. These sounds strike insects or other objects and return as echoes. By receiving the echoes, bats find the position and movement of their prey.

18. How is ultrasound used for cleaning?

The object is placed in a cleaning liquid. Ultrasound produces strong vibrations in the liquid, which loosen dirt and grease from the object, even in hard-to-reach places.

19. Explain the working and application of a sonar.

SONAR sends ultrasonic waves through water and receives their echoes. The time taken by the waves is used to calculate distance. It is used to measure sea depth and locate underwater objects.

20. A sonar device on a submarine sends a signal and receives an echo 5 s later. Find the speed of sound in water if the object is 3625 m away.

Total distance travelled by sound = 2 × 3625 = 7250 m.

Speed = distance/time = 7250/5 = 1450 m/s

21. Explain how defects in a metal block can be detected using ultrasound.

Ultrasound is sent through the metal. A crack or defect reflects some of the waves. Detectors receive the reflected waves and help locate the defect inside the block.

22. Explain how the human ear works.

The pinna collects sound. Sound travels through the auditory canal and vibrates the eardrum. The hammer, anvil and stirrup amplify the vibrations. The cochlea changes them into electrical signals. The auditory nerve carries the signals to the brain, which interprets them as sound.

Important Formulae

Frequency
ν = 1/T
Time period
T = 1/ν
Wave speed
v = νλ
Wavelength
λ = v/ν
Echo / SONAR distance
d = vt/2
Echo round-trip time
t = 2d/v

Quick Revision

  • Sound is produced by vibrations.
  • Sound needs a material medium and cannot travel through vacuum.
  • Sound travels through air as a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions.
  • Frequency decides pitch; amplitude mainly affects loudness.
  • Human hearing range: 20 Hz to 20 kHz.
  • Ultrasound has frequency above 20 kHz.
  • Echo is reflected sound; reverberation is persistence due to repeated reflections.
  • SONAR uses ultrasound to locate underwater objects and measure sea depth.
  • The ear changes sound vibrations into signals interpreted by the brain.
