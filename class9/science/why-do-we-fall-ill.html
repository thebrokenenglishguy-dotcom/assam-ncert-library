Chapter 13 — Why Do We Fall Ill?

Chapter notes, important definitions and textbook questions with answers in simple English.

13.1 Health and Its Failure

1. What is health?

Health is a state of physical, mental and social well-being. It does not mean only the absence of disease.

2. What does being healthy mean?

Being healthy means being well enough to function properly in our body, mind and social life. A healthy person can work, learn, think and take part in daily activities.

3. Why is health more than being free from disease?

A person may not have a particular disease but may still be weak, unhappy or unable to do daily activities. Good health includes physical fitness, mental well-being and social well-being.

4. Why are personal and community issues both important for health?

  • Our health depends on the physical environment around us.
  • Clean drinking water, proper drainage and waste disposal help prevent disease.
  • Social conditions such as equality, cooperation and good relationships also affect mental and physical health.
  • Therefore, individual health depends partly on the health of the whole community.

5. How does poor sanitation affect health?

Open drains, stagnant water, uncollected garbage and unsafe drinking water allow disease-causing microbes and insects to spread. This increases the risk of illness. Public cleanliness is important for good health.

6. What are the Five Fs in prevention of disease transmission?

The chapter diagram highlights five hygiene areas: fluids, fingers, flies, fields and food. Safe water, clean hands, protection of food, clean surroundings and proper sanitation help prevent the spread of diseases.

13.1.3 Healthy and Disease-free

7. Differentiate between healthy and disease-free.

  • Disease-free: A person is not suffering from a particular disease.
  • Healthy: A person has physical, mental and social well-being and can use their abilities properly.

A person can be disease-free but still not be fully healthy.

8. Can a person be in poor health without having an identifiable disease?

Yes. A person may feel weak, unhappy or unable to work properly without having one clearly identified disease. Health is affected by personal and community conditions.

13.2 Disease and Its Causes

9. What is a disease?

A disease is a condition in which the normal functioning of one or more parts or systems of the body is disturbed. It produces signs and symptoms.

10. What are symptoms of a disease?

Symptoms are the changes or feelings experienced by a person when ill. Examples include headache, cough, loose motions and pain.

11. What are signs of a disease?

Signs are changes that can be observed or measured by another person, especially a doctor. They give more definite information about a disease. Laboratory tests may also help identify the disease.

12. Why are symptoms not always enough to identify a disease?

The same symptom can occur in many diseases. For example, headache may occur due to stress or may be associated with different illnesses. A doctor studies signs and may use laboratory tests to identify the cause.

13.2.2 Acute and Chronic Diseases

13. What are acute diseases?

Acute diseases last for a short period of time. Example: common cold.

14. What are chronic diseases?

Chronic diseases last for a long time, sometimes for many years or even a lifetime. Example: tuberculosis of the lungs can have long-term effects if not properly treated.

15. Differentiate between acute and chronic diseases.

  • Acute: Short duration; usually has less time to cause major effects on general health.
  • Chronic: Long duration; can cause lasting weakness, weight loss or reduced ability to work and study.

16. Why do chronic diseases have a greater effect on general health?

They continue for a long time. They may reduce body strength, cause weight loss and tiredness, and make it difficult to attend school or do daily work.

13.2.4 Causes of Diseases

17. What are the different levels of causes of a disease?

A disease may have immediate causes and contributory causes. For example, an infection may be the immediate cause of loose motions, while poor nutrition, unsafe water and poverty may increase the chance of getting the disease.

18. What is an immediate cause?

An immediate cause is the direct factor that produces a disease, such as a disease-causing microbe entering the body.

19. What are contributory causes?

Contributory causes are conditions that make a person more likely to get a disease. Examples include poor nutrition, unsafe drinking water, poverty and poor public services.

20. Why do all babies exposed to contaminated water not necessarily develop the same illness?

Their health and nutrition may be different. A poorly nourished baby may have weaker resistance and may be more likely to become ill. Genetic differences may also affect susceptibility. The microbe is still an important immediate cause.

13.2.5 Infectious and Non-infectious Causes

21. What are infectious diseases?

Infectious diseases are diseases caused by infectious agents such as viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa and worms. These agents can spread from one person or host to another.

22. What are non-infectious diseases?

Non-infectious diseases are not caused by infectious agents and do not spread from person to person. Examples include some cancers, diabetes and high blood pressure.

23. Differentiate between infectious and non-infectious diseases.

  • Infectious: Caused by microbes or other infectious agents; may spread between hosts. Examples: common cold, tuberculosis and malaria.
  • Non-infectious: Not caused by infectious agents; do not spread by ordinary contact. Examples: diabetes and many cancers.

13.3 Infectious Diseases

13.3.1 Infectious Agents

24. Name the main groups of disease-causing microbes.

  1. Viruses — examples: common cold, influenza, dengue and AIDS.
  2. Bacteria — examples: typhoid, cholera, tuberculosis and anthrax.
  3. Fungi — cause different skin infections.
  4. Protozoa — examples: malaria and kala-azar.
  5. Worms — some intestinal worm infections and elephantiasis.

25. Why is it useful to classify infectious agents?

Different groups of microbes have different biological features. This helps doctors choose suitable medicines. A medicine that works against bacteria may not work against viruses.

26. Why do antibiotics not work against viral infections?

Antibiotics act on processes found in bacteria, such as the making of a bacterial cell wall. Viruses live and multiply inside host cells and do not use the same pathways. Therefore, antibiotics do not kill viruses.

27. Why can penicillin kill many bacteria without harming human cells in the same way?

Penicillin blocks the process used by many bacteria to build their cell walls. Human cells do not have cell walls, so this particular action does not affect human cells in the same way.

13.3.2 Means of Spread

28. How do infectious diseases spread through air?

When an infected person coughs or sneezes, tiny droplets containing microbes enter the air. Other people may breathe them in. Examples include common cold, influenza, COVID-19 and tuberculosis.

29. How do diseases spread through water?

Water can become contaminated with the waste of an infected person. When other people drink this unsafe water, microbes enter their bodies. Cholera is an example.

30. How can diseases spread through physical contact?

Some infections spread through close physical or sexual contact. Examples mentioned in the chapter include syphilis and HIV infection through sexual contact. HIV can also spread through infected blood and from an infected mother to her baby during pregnancy or breast-feeding.

31. What are vectors?

Vectors are animals that carry disease-causing microbes from an infected person or animal to another host. Mosquitoes are common vectors.

32. How do mosquitoes spread disease?

A mosquito can pick up disease-causing microbes while feeding on an infected host. It can then transfer them to another person when it bites. Female mosquitoes take blood meals to help their eggs mature.

33. Name four common ways infectious diseases spread.

  • Through air.
  • Through contaminated water or food.
  • Through physical or sexual contact and infected blood.
  • Through vectors such as mosquitoes.

13.3.3 Organ-specific and Tissue-specific Manifestations

34. Why do different diseases produce different symptoms?

Different microbes may enter through different routes and affect different organs or tissues. The symptoms depend on the part of the body that is damaged.

35. Give examples of diseases affecting different organs.

  • If the lungs are affected, symptoms may include cough and breathlessness.
  • If the liver is affected, jaundice may occur.
  • If the brain is affected, symptoms may include headache, vomiting, fits or unconsciousness.

36. What is inflammation?

Inflammation is a response of the immune system in which immune cells move to the affected area to fight microbes. It may cause local swelling and pain, and general effects such as fever.

37. Why can HIV infection make a person vulnerable to other infections?

HIV damages the immune system. The body becomes less able to fight microbes, so even infections that are usually minor may become serious.

13.3.4 Principles of Treatment

38. What are the two main aims of treatment?

  1. To reduce the effects of the disease, such as fever, pain and loose motions. This is symptom-directed treatment.
  2. To kill or remove the microbes that cause the disease. This treats the actual cause.

39. Why is reducing symptoms alone not enough to cure an infectious disease?

Reducing fever or pain may make a person feel better, but the microbes may still remain in the body. The infection must also be treated by suitable methods.

40. Why are medicines chosen according to the type of microbe?

Viruses, bacteria, fungi and protozoa have different biological processes. A medicine that blocks a process in one group may not work against another group.

13.3.5 Principles of Prevention

41. Why is prevention of infectious diseases better than cure?

  • Some diseases can damage body functions and recovery may take a long time.
  • Treatment may require rest and may keep a person away from school or work.
  • An infected person may spread the disease to others.

42. How can exposure to infectious microbes be reduced?

  • Maintain personal hygiene and wash hands.
  • Drink safe and clean water.
  • Keep surroundings clean and dispose of waste properly.
  • Prevent mosquito breeding and use protection against mosquito bites.
  • Avoid overcrowding and maintain proper ventilation.
  • Use masks when appropriate to reduce the spread of airborne infection.

43. What is immunisation?

Immunisation is the process of making a person protected against a disease by training the immune system to recognise a disease-causing microbe. Vaccines help the body develop this protection without causing the full disease.

44. How does vaccination protect us?

A vaccine introduces a harmless form or part of a microbe, or something that imitates it. The immune system responds and remembers it. If the same microbe enters later, the body can respond faster and more strongly.

45. Give examples of diseases for which vaccines are available.

The chapter gives examples such as tetanus, diphtheria, whooping cough, measles and polio. Vaccination programmes help protect children and communities.

Important Questions from the Chapter

1. State any two conditions essential for good health.

Good nutrition and a clean physical environment with safe drinking water and proper sanitation are two important conditions.

2. State any two conditions essential for being free of disease.

Protection from disease-causing microbes and a properly functioning immune system are important conditions.

3. Are the answers to the above questions necessarily the same? Why?

No. Being free from a particular disease does not always mean a person is fully healthy. Good health also needs physical, mental and social well-being.

4. Why should we take bland and nourishing food when we are sick?

Nourishing food supplies energy and nutrients needed by the body. Bland food may be easier to digest when a person is unwell.

5. What are the different ways by which infectious diseases are spread?

They spread through air, contaminated water or food, physical or sexual contact, infected blood, and vectors such as mosquitoes.

6. What precautions can you take in your school to reduce the incidence of infectious diseases?

  • Wash hands with soap, especially before eating and after using the toilet.
  • Drink safe water and keep water containers clean.
  • Keep classrooms and toilets clean.
  • Do not share personal items such as towels or drinking bottles.
  • Cover the mouth and nose while coughing or sneezing.
  • Keep the school free from stagnant water and mosquito breeding sites.

7. What is immunisation?

Immunisation is the process of developing protection against a disease, usually through vaccination.

8. What immunisation programmes are available at the nearest health centre?

This is a local activity. Ask your nearest government health centre or ASHA/ANM worker for the current vaccination schedule and services in your area.

NCERT Exercise — Questions and Answers

1. How many times did you fall ill in the last one year? What were the illnesses? (a) Think of one change in your habits to avoid these illnesses.

Sample answer: Answers will differ from person to person. One useful change is to wash hands regularly, drink safe water and eat clean, properly prepared food.

1(b). Think of one change you would wish for in your surroundings to avoid these illnesses.

Sample answer: I would like proper garbage collection, clean drains and safe drinking water in my neighbourhood.

2. A doctor, nurse or health-worker is exposed to more sick people than others. How does he or she avoid getting sick?

Health workers follow infection-control practices. They wash or sanitise their hands, use suitable protective equipment, keep vaccinations up to date, dispose of medical waste safely and follow hygiene rules.

3. Conduct a survey in your neighbourhood to find out the three most common diseases. Suggest three steps that local authorities can take.

Sample answer: The actual diseases should be identified by a local survey. Local authorities can provide safe drinking water, improve garbage collection and drainage, and control mosquitoes by removing stagnant water.

4. A baby is not able to tell its caretakers that she or he is sick. What would help to find out (a) that the baby is sick and (b) what is the sickness?

(a) Observe signs such as fever, poor feeding, unusual crying, vomiting, loose motions, rash or difficulty breathing.

(b) A doctor examines the baby, checks the signs and may recommend tests to identify the disease.

5. Under which condition is a person most likely to fall sick?

Answer: (b) When the person has recovered from malaria and is taking care of someone suffering from chicken-pox. The person may be exposed to the chicken-pox infection. Recovery from malaria does not give protection against chicken-pox.

6. Under which condition are you most likely to fall sick?

Answer: (c) When your friend is suffering from measles. Measles is an infectious disease that can spread from an infected person to others, especially among close contacts. Examination stress and travel do not directly expose you to a specific infectious microbe in the same way.

Quick Revision

  • Health means physical, mental and social well-being.
  • Good health depends on personal habits and community conditions.
  • Acute diseases last for a short time; chronic diseases last for a long time.
  • Diseases may be infectious or non-infectious.
  • Infectious agents include viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa and worms.
  • Infections spread through air, water, food, contact, blood and vectors.
  • Treatment aims to reduce symptoms and remove the cause.
  • Prevention includes hygiene, sanitation, safe water and immunisation.
  • Vaccines help the immune system remember and fight particular microbes.
