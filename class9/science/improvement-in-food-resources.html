Chapter 15 — Improvement in Food Resources

Chapter notes, important concepts, textbook in-text questions and all NCERT exercise answers in simple English.

Chapter overview: Food production can be improved through better crop varieties, proper crop management, crop protection and scientific animal husbandry. Sustainable methods are important because natural resources must be protected.

Introduction: Why do we need to improve food resources?

1. Why is food important for living organisms?

Food supplies proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins and minerals. These nutrients are needed for body development, growth and good health.

2. Why is it necessary to increase food production?

India has a large and growing population. More people need more food, but the area of cultivable land cannot be increased much. Therefore, we must increase the efficiency and yield of crops and livestock.

3. What were the Green Revolution and White Revolution?

  • Green Revolution: It led to increased production of food grains.
  • White Revolution: It improved the production and availability of milk.

4. Why should food production be sustainable?

Intensive farming can use natural resources heavily and damage the environment. Sustainable practices increase food production without degrading soil, water and other natural resources.

5. What are the three main groups of activities used to improve food resources?

  1. Crop variety improvement.
  2. Crop production management.
  3. Crop protection management.

15.1 Improvement in Crop Yields

6. What are the major food crop groups and what do they provide?

  • Cereals such as wheat, rice, maize, millets and sorghum provide carbohydrates.
  • Pulses such as gram, pea, black gram, green gram, pigeon pea and lentil provide proteins.
  • Oilseeds such as soybean, groundnut, sesame, castor, mustard, linseed and sunflower provide fats.
  • Vegetables, spices and fruits provide vitamins and minerals, along with small amounts of proteins, carbohydrates and fats.
  • Fodder crops such as berseem, oats and Sudan grass are grown to feed livestock.

7. What are kharif and rabi crops?

  • Kharif crops: Grown during the rainy season, generally from June to October. Examples: paddy, soybean, pigeon pea, maize, cotton, green gram and black gram.
  • Rabi crops: Grown during the winter season, generally from November to April. Examples: wheat, gram, peas, mustard and linseed.

8. What factors affect crop growth and yield?

Different crops need suitable climatic conditions, temperature, photoperiod (duration of sunlight), water and soil conditions. Biotic factors such as pests and diseases and abiotic factors such as drought, salinity, waterlogging, heat and cold also affect crop production.

15.1.1 Crop Variety Improvement

9. What is crop variety improvement?

It is the process of developing or selecting crop varieties with useful characteristics so that farmers can obtain better yield, quality and resistance under different growing conditions.

10. What is hybridisation?

Hybridisation is the crossing of two genetically dissimilar plants to combine desirable characters. It may be:

  • Intervarietal: Between different varieties of the same crop.
  • Interspecific: Between different species of the same genus.
  • Intergeneric: Between plants belonging to different genera.

11. What are genetically modified crops?

These are crops in which a gene is introduced to provide a desired characteristic. This is another method of improving crop varieties.

12. What are the main desirable characters in improved crop varieties?

  • Higher yield: More crop production per unit area.
  • Improved quality: For example, baking quality in wheat, protein quality in pulses, oil quality in oilseeds and keeping quality in fruits and vegetables.
  • Biotic and abiotic resistance: Resistance to diseases, insects and nematodes, as well as drought, salinity, waterlogging, heat and cold.
  • Change in maturity duration: Shorter crop duration can reduce costs and allow more crops in a year. Uniform maturity makes harvesting easier.
  • Wider adaptability: The variety can grow under different environmental conditions.
  • Desirable agronomic characteristics: Tallness and profuse branching are useful in fodder crops, while dwarfness is desirable in cereals because less nutrients are used by the stem.

13. How do biotic and abiotic factors affect crop production?

Biotic factors include insects, pests, diseases and nematodes. They damage plants and reduce yield. Abiotic factors include drought, salinity, waterlogging, heat and cold. They create stress and may prevent normal growth. Resistant varieties help reduce these losses.

14. What are desirable agronomic characteristics for crop improvement?

Tallness and profuse branching are desirable in fodder crops because they produce more plant material. Dwarfness is desirable in cereals because less food is used to support the stem, leaving more nutrients for grain production.

15.1.2 Crop Production Management

15. What is crop production management?

It means using suitable farming practices to obtain good crop yields. These practices depend on the farmer's resources, access to information and technology, soil, water and climate. They include nutrient management, irrigation and cropping patterns.

16. What are the three levels of farming practices based on cost?

They are no-cost production practices, low-cost production practices and high-cost production practices. Farmers choose practices according to their financial condition and available resources.

Nutrient Management

17. Why do plants need nutrients?

Plants need nutrients for growth, development and healthy functioning. Nutrients are supplied by air, water and soil.

18. What are macronutrients and micronutrients?

  • Macronutrients: Nutrients required by plants in large quantities. Examples: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulphur.
  • Micronutrients: Nutrients required in small quantities. Examples: iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, molybdenum and chlorine.

19. How do plants get nutrients from air, water and soil?

  • Air: Carbon and oxygen.
  • Water: Hydrogen and oxygen.
  • Soil: The remaining mineral nutrients, including macro- and micronutrients.

20. What happens when plants do not get enough nutrients?

Nutrient deficiency affects normal physiological processes, growth and reproduction. It can make plants more susceptible to diseases and reduce crop yield. Nutrients can be supplied through manure and fertilisers.

Manure and Fertilisers

21. What is manure?

Manure is an organic material formed by the decomposition of animal excreta and plant waste. It adds organic matter and small amounts of nutrients to the soil and improves soil fertility.

22. How does manure improve soil?

  • It adds organic matter and nutrients.
  • It improves soil structure.
  • It increases the water-holding capacity of sandy soil.
  • It improves drainage and reduces waterlogging in clayey soil.
  • It supports useful soil microorganisms.

23. What are the main types of manure described in the chapter?

  • Compost: Farm waste such as livestock excreta, vegetable waste, animal refuse, domestic waste, sewage waste, straw and weeds is decomposed in pits.
  • Vermicompost: Earthworms are used to speed up the decomposition of plant and animal refuse.
  • Green manure: Plants such as sun hemp or guar are grown and then ploughed into the soil before sowing the main crop. They enrich the soil, especially with nitrogen and phosphorus.

24. What are fertilisers?

Fertilisers are commercially produced plant nutrients. They mainly supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. They help plants grow well and can increase crop yield, especially in high-input farming.

25. What are the disadvantages of excessive use of fertilisers?

  • Fertilisers may be washed away by excessive irrigation and cause water pollution.
  • Long-term excessive use can damage soil fertility.
  • They do not replace organic matter in the soil.
  • They may harm useful soil microorganisms.

26. Compare manure and fertilisers.

  • Manure: Natural and organic; adds humus, improves soil structure and releases nutrients slowly. It usually has a lower nutrient concentration.
  • Fertilisers: Commercially manufactured; contain concentrated plant nutrients and act more quickly, but excessive use can pollute water and harm soil quality.

27. What is organic farming?

Organic farming is a farming system that uses little or no chemical fertilisers, herbicides and pesticides. It uses organic manure, recycled farm waste, bio-agents and biological methods of pest control. Mixed cropping, intercropping and crop rotation are also used.

Irrigation

28. What is irrigation?

Irrigation is the artificial supply of water to crops at suitable times during their growth. It is important because most Indian agriculture depends on seasonal monsoon rainfall, which may be irregular.

29. Why is irrigation necessary?

Water is needed for seed germination, nutrient transport, photosynthesis and plant growth. Timely irrigation helps crops receive enough water during important stages and can increase yield.

30. What are the main sources and systems of irrigation mentioned in the chapter?

  • Wells: Dug wells collect water from shallow water-bearing layers. Tube wells tap deeper water-bearing layers; pumps lift the water.
  • Canals: Carry water from rivers or reservoirs through main canals and smaller branches to fields.
  • River lift systems: Water is directly lifted from rivers where canal flow is insufficient or irregular.
  • Tanks: Small storage reservoirs that collect runoff from catchment areas.

31. What are water-harvesting and watershed-management methods?

Rainwater harvesting and watershed management improve water availability. Small check-dams can store rainwater, increase groundwater levels, slow runoff and reduce soil erosion.

32. Why are droughts harmful to crops?

Droughts occur when rainfall is scarce or irregular. They threaten rain-fed farming, especially in areas with light soils that cannot hold much water. Drought-tolerant crop varieties can help reduce crop losses.

Cropping Patterns

33. What is mixed cropping?

Mixed cropping is growing two or more crops together on the same piece of land at the same time. Example: wheat with gram or wheat with mustard. It reduces the risk of total crop failure because one crop may still produce a yield if another fails.

34. What is intercropping?

Intercropping is growing two or more crops simultaneously in the same field in a definite row pattern. Example: soybean with maize, or finger millet with cowpea. Different nutrient needs help use nutrients efficiently and can reduce the spread of pests and diseases.

35. What is crop rotation?

Crop rotation means growing different crops on the same land in a planned sequence. The sequence depends on the duration of crops, soil moisture and irrigation facilities. Proper crop rotation can allow two or three crops in a year and help maintain soil fertility.

36. Differentiate between mixed cropping, intercropping and crop rotation.

  • Mixed cropping: Two or more crops are grown together without a fixed row pattern, mainly to reduce the risk of crop failure.
  • Intercropping: Two or more crops are grown together in a definite row pattern to use resources efficiently and reduce pest and disease spread.
  • Crop rotation: Different crops are grown one after another in a planned sequence on the same land.

15.1.3 Crop Protection Management

37. What is crop protection management?

It includes practices used to protect standing crops and stored grains from weeds, insect pests, diseases and other causes of loss.

38. What are weeds? Give examples.

Weeds are unwanted plants growing in cultivated fields. Examples mentioned in the chapter include Xanthium (gokhroo), Parthenium (gajar ghas) and Cyperinus rotundus (motha). Weeds compete with crops for food, space and light.

39. What are the three ways in which insect pests damage crops?

  1. They cut the roots, stem or leaves.
  2. They suck the cell sap from different parts of the plant.
  3. They bore into stems and fruits.

These actions affect plant health and reduce crop yield.

40. What causes plant diseases?

Plant diseases are caused by pathogens such as bacteria, fungi and viruses. These pathogens may be present in and transmitted through soil, water and air.

41. How can weeds, insects and diseases be controlled?

  • Use weedicides, insecticides and fungicides carefully when needed.
  • Use mechanical removal of weeds.
  • Prepare seeds properly and sow crops on time.
  • Use resistant crop varieties.
  • Practise intercropping and crop rotation.
  • Plough fields in summer to destroy weeds and pests.

Excessive use of chemical pesticides can poison useful organisms and cause environmental pollution.

42. Why are preventive and biological control methods preferred?

They reduce dependence on chemical pesticides, help protect useful organisms and lower the risk of environmental pollution. Preventive practices include proper seed preparation, timely sowing, resistant varieties, intercropping and crop rotation.

43. How do storage grain losses occur?

Storage losses are caused by biotic factors such as insects, rodents, fungi, mites and bacteria, and abiotic factors such as unsuitable moisture and temperature. These factors reduce grain quality, weight, germination ability and market value.

44. How can stored grains be protected?

Preventive measures include cleaning the produce before storage, proper drying first in sunlight and then in shade, and fumigation with suitable chemicals to kill pests. Warehouses should also be managed systematically.

15.2 Animal Husbandry

45. What is animal husbandry?

Animal husbandry is the scientific management of livestock. It includes feeding, breeding and disease control. It covers cattle, goats, sheep, poultry and fish farming.

15.2.1 Cattle Farming

46. What are the two main purposes of cattle farming?

  • Milk production.
  • Providing draught labour for agricultural work such as tilling, irrigation and carting.

47. What are milch animals and draught animals?

Milch animals are milk-producing females. Draught animals are used for farm labour.

48. What are indigenous and exotic cattle breeds?

Indigenous breeds are local breeds, such as Red Sindhi and Sahiwal, which show good resistance to diseases. Exotic breeds, such as Jersey and Brown Swiss, are selected for longer lactation periods. Cross-breeding can combine desirable qualities.

49. Why is cross-breeding used in cattle?

Cross-breeding combines useful characters of indigenous and exotic breeds. The aim is to obtain animals with good milk production and better resistance to diseases.

50. What care and shelter do cattle need?

  • Clean, well-ventilated and properly roofed sheds.
  • Protection from rain, heat and cold.
  • A sloping floor for drainage and easy cleaning.
  • Regular brushing and removal of dirt and loose hair.
  • Balanced food and clean water.
  • Regular health checks and vaccination.

51. What are the two types of food requirements of dairy animals?

  • Maintenance requirement: Food needed to keep the animal healthy and alive.
  • Milk-producing requirement: Additional food needed during the lactation period to produce milk.

52. What is a balanced ration for cattle?

A balanced ration contains all nutrients in suitable proportions. It includes roughage, which is rich in fibre, and concentrates, which are low in fibre and contain more proteins and other nutrients. Feed additives may supply micronutrients.

53. What are common cattle diseases and how can they be prevented?

Cattle may suffer from external parasites that live on the skin and cause skin diseases, and internal parasites such as worms and flukes that affect the stomach, intestine or liver. Bacteria and viruses also cause infectious diseases. Clean housing, good nutrition, veterinary care and vaccination help prevent disease.

15.2.2 Poultry Farming

54. What is poultry farming?

Poultry farming is the raising of domestic fowl for egg and meat production. Improved breeds are developed for these purposes.

55. What are layers and broilers?

  • Layers: Poultry birds raised for egg production.
  • Broilers: Poultry birds raised for meat.

56. What traits are desirable in improved poultry breeds?

Desirable traits include a large number and good quality of chicks, dwarf broiler parent birds for commercial chick production, summer adaptation and heat tolerance, low maintenance requirements, and a smaller egg-laying bird that can use cheaper, more fibrous feed.

57. What is important in broiler production?

Broilers need protein-rich feed with adequate fat. Vitamins A and K are kept high in poultry feed. Good temperature, hygiene, housing and disease control are important for fast growth, good feed efficiency and healthy meat production.

58. How are poultry diseases controlled?

Proper cleaning, sanitation and regular spraying of disinfectants are needed. Appropriate vaccination helps prevent infectious diseases and reduces losses during disease outbreaks.

59. Differentiate between layers and broilers in management.

Layers are managed for egg production and need suitable nutrition and care throughout their laying period. Broilers are managed for rapid meat growth and need protein-rich, energy-rich feed, suitable vitamins and careful control of temperature and hygiene.

15.2.3 Fish Production

60. Why is fish an important food resource?

Fish is a cheap source of animal protein. Fish production includes finned fish as well as shellfish such as prawns and mussels.

61. What are the two main ways of obtaining fish?

  • Capture fishing: Fish are obtained from natural water resources.
  • Culture fishery: Fish are reared and produced through fish farming.

62. What is the difference between marine and inland fisheries?

  • Marine fisheries: Fishing in seas and oceans. Marine fish may be caught using nets, fishing boats, satellites and echo-sounders. Some high-value marine organisms are also cultured.
  • Inland fisheries: Fishing in fresh water such as canals, ponds, reservoirs and rivers, and in brackish water such as estuaries and lagoons.

63. What is mariculture?

Mariculture is the culture or farming of marine organisms in seawater. It is used to increase production when natural marine fish stocks become depleted.

64. What is aquaculture?

Aquaculture is the farming of aquatic organisms in water. It can be carried out in fresh water or marine water.

65. What is composite fish culture?

Composite fish culture is a system in which five or six fish species with different food habits are reared together in one pond. They use food from different zones of the pond and do not compete strongly with one another.

66. Name the fish used in composite fish culture and their feeding zones.

  • Catla: Surface feeder.
  • Rohu: Feeds in the middle zone.
  • Mrigal and Common carp: Bottom feeders.
  • Grass carp: Feeds on aquatic weeds.

67. What is a major problem in composite fish culture?

Many fish breed only during the monsoon. Fish seed collected from the wild may contain unwanted species. This makes it difficult to obtain pure fish seed in the required quantity. Hormonal stimulation can be used to breed fish in ponds and supply pure fish seed.

68. What are the advantages of composite fish culture?

Different fish species feed in different zones of the pond. This reduces competition for food and allows the available food to be used efficiently, increasing fish yield.

15.2.4 Bee-Keeping

69. What is bee-keeping?

Bee-keeping, or apiculture, is the rearing and management of honeybees for honey and wax. It can provide additional income and needs relatively low investment.

70. Name the bee varieties used for commercial honey production in India.

The varieties mentioned in the chapter are Apis cerana indica (Indian bee), A. dorsata (rock bee), A. florea (little bee) and the Italian bee, A. mellifera.

71. What are desirable characteristics of honeybees for commercial honey production?

Good honey-producing bees have high honey-collection capacity, sting somewhat less, stay in the beehive for long periods and breed well.

72. What is pasturage and how does it affect honey production?

Pasturage means the availability of flowers to bees for nectar and pollen collection. It affects the quantity and quality of honey. The types of flowers available also influence the taste of honey.

NCERT In-text Questions — Quick Answers

73. What do we get from cereals, pulses, fruits and vegetables?

Cereals provide carbohydrates; pulses provide proteins; fruits and vegetables provide vitamins and minerals, along with small amounts of other nutrients.

74. How do biotic and abiotic factors affect crop production?

Biotic factors such as pests, insects, weeds and disease-causing organisms damage crops. Abiotic factors such as drought, salinity, waterlogging, heat and cold cause stress. Both can reduce crop yield.

75. What are the desirable agronomic characteristics for crop improvements?

Tallness and profuse branching are useful in fodder crops. Dwarfness is desirable in cereals because less nutrients are used by the stem.

76. What are macronutrients and why are they called macronutrients?

Macronutrients are plant nutrients required in large quantities, such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulphur.

77. How do plants get nutrients?

Plants obtain carbon and oxygen from air, hydrogen and oxygen from water, and mineral nutrients mainly from soil.

78. Compare the use of manure and fertilisers in maintaining soil fertility.

Manure adds organic matter, improves soil structure and supports soil organisms. Fertilisers supply concentrated nutrients quickly, but excessive use can pollute water and damage soil fertility.

79. Which condition gives the most benefits: (a) quality seeds only, (b) ordinary seeds with irrigation and fertiliser, or (c) quality seeds with irrigation, fertiliser and crop protection?

Option (c) gives the most benefits because good-quality seeds, sufficient water, nutrients and protection from pests and diseases work together to improve yield.

80. Which method is commonly used for improving cattle breeds and why?

Cross-breeding is commonly used. It combines desirable qualities of indigenous and exotic breeds, such as disease resistance and a longer milk-producing period.

81. What management practices are common in dairy and poultry farming?

Both need proper shelter, balanced nutrition, clean water, hygiene, disease prevention, vaccination and regular care.

82. What are the differences between broilers and layers in their management?

Broilers are raised for meat and need feed that supports rapid growth. Layers are raised for eggs and need balanced nutrition and care to maintain egg production.

83. How are fish obtained?

Fish are obtained by capture fishing from natural water bodies and by culture fishery or fish farming.

84. What are the advantages of composite fish culture?

Different species feed in different zones of a pond, so they do not compete much for food. The pond's food resources are used more completely and fish production increases.

NCERT Exercise — All Questions and Answers

Exercise 1. Explain any one method of crop production which ensures high yield.

Intercropping is one method. Two or more crops are grown together in a definite row pattern. The crops are selected so that their nutrient requirements are different. This helps use nutrients efficiently and can reduce the spread of pests and diseases, giving a better yield.

Exercise 2. Why are manure and fertilisers used in fields?

Plants need nutrients for growth. Repeated cultivation reduces soil nutrients. Manure adds organic matter and improves soil structure, while fertilisers supply specific nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. Both are used to maintain soil fertility and improve crop yield.

Exercise 3. What are the advantages of intercropping and crop rotation?

Intercropping: It makes better use of nutrients and other resources, reduces the spread of pests and diseases and can improve yield. Crop rotation: It helps maintain soil fertility, reduces the build-up of pests and diseases and may allow two or three crops to be grown in a year.

Exercise 4. What is genetic manipulation? How is it useful in agricultural practices?

Genetic manipulation involves introducing or changing genes to obtain desired characteristics in an organism. In crops, it can be used to develop varieties with useful traits such as higher yield, improved quality and resistance to pests, diseases or environmental stresses.

Exercise 5. How do storage grain losses occur?

Storage losses occur due to biotic factors such as insects, rodents, fungi, mites and bacteria, and abiotic factors such as unsuitable moisture and temperature. These reduce grain quality, weight, germination ability and market value.

Exercise 6. How do good animal husbandry practices benefit farmers?

Good animal husbandry improves the health and productivity of livestock. Proper feeding, breeding, shelter and disease control can increase the production of milk, eggs, meat, fish and other useful products. This improves farmers' income.

Exercise 7. What are the benefits of cattle farming?

Cattle provide milk and draught labour for agricultural work such as tilling, irrigation and carting. Cattle dung can also be used as manure and for biogas production.

Exercise 8. For increasing production, what is common in poultry, fisheries and bee-keeping?

All three require scientific selection and breeding, suitable feed or food resources, proper housing or habitat, hygiene and prevention of diseases. Good management improves production.

Exercise 9. How do you differentiate between capture fishing, mariculture and aquaculture?

  • Capture fishing: Catching fish from natural water resources.
  • Mariculture: Farming marine organisms in seawater.
  • Aquaculture: Farming aquatic organisms in water, including both fresh water and marine water.
